# Turn the Ship Around! — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing management practice, or advising on team/company operating style.*
*Book: "Turn the Ship Around!: A True Story of Turning Followers into Leaders" — L. David Marquet*

---

## 1. The Core Problem: Leader-Follower

Marquet names the default leadership model taught at the Naval Academy and used in most organizations "leader-follower." Its own definition, quoted from a Naval Academy leadership text: leadership is the art by which one person directs the thoughts, plans, and actions of others to obtain their obedience, confidence, respect, and cooperation. The model divides every organization into two groups: leaders who decide, and followers who execute. It is ancient (Marquet traces its literary roots to The Iliad, Beowulf, and sea-captain fiction like Master and Commander) and it built real things: farming, the pyramids, the factories of the Industrial Revolution. It works well when the primary work is physical.

The failure mode shows up when the work is cognitive instead. People treated as followers develop the expectations of followers: limited decision authority, low incentive to contribute full intellect or energy, a tendency to "run at half speed." The common prescribed fix, "empowerment programs," fails for a structural reason: the method (I, the leader, will empower you) contradicts the message (you should act with authority). Empowerment granted from above still originates top-down, so it disempowers even as it claims to liberate.

Leader-follower also creates a second problem invisible in the short run: it links organizational performance to the personality and continued presence of the leader. A "good" ship does well under one CO and badly under the next, and the officer graded is graded on performance up to the day he leaves, never after. This rewards leaders for being indispensable and irreplaceable rather than for building something that outlasts them. Marquet's diagnostic questions for this: does your organization reward people for what happens after they transfer out? Are people rewarded for the success of their people, or only their own?

---

## 2. The Alternative: Leader-Leader

Leader-leader starts from the belief that everyone can be a leader, and that an organization performs best when everyone actually is one. Leadership, in this frame, is not a scarce trait held by a few; it is a capacity everyone already has that most organizational structures actively suppress rather than need to install.

The practical difference from leader-follower shows up in resilience: leader-leader structures don't depend on the designated leader always being right, and they are not disrupted when the leader leaves, because the operating pattern is embedded in the practices and people rather than in one person's judgment. Marquet's own evidence for this: Santa Fe kept winning awards for a decade after he left, and its officers and enlisted men were promoted at implausibly high rates for years afterward — the mark of a system, not a personality, having done the work.

Marquet frames the book's arc as four movements: first, clearing away the inherited assumption that some people are leaders and others are followers (Part I); then building three interlocking supports for leader-leader — control, competence, and clarity (Parts II–IV). Control is divesting decision-making authority to the people closest to the information, while the leader retains full responsibility. Control alone doesn't work; it requires a workforce with the technical competence to make good decisions, and organizational clarity about what the organization is trying to achieve, so decisions are made against the right criteria. The three reinforce each other in expanding circles rather than being deployed once in sequence.

## 3. Control: Finding and Rewriting the "Genetic Code"

The first move on Santa Fe was to locate exactly where in written policy a decision-making authority was assigned, and change who holds it — often with a single word. The flagship example: enlisted leave requests required signatures from seven people up the chain, ending with the executive officer (XO). Marquet crossed out "XO" and wrote in "COB" (Chief of the Boat, the senior enlisted man), collapsing a fourteen-step process to one. This was technically outside his authority to change, but he judged the risk worth it.

The deeper mechanism this exposes: distributing one decision (leave) forced ownership of everything that decision depended on — watch bills, qualification schedules, training enrollment — because you can't own the small decision without owning the system that feeds it. Marquet calls this discovery that authority cascades downward through dependent systems the reason distributing control is never a single, isolated act.

A structured exercise for finding these points in any organization:
- Identify in policy documents exactly where a given decision-making authority is currently assigned.
- Identify candidate decisions to push down a level; disaggregate large decisions if needed.
- Have each leader complete the sentence "When I think about delegating this decision, I worry that ___" on a card.
- Post the cards, let people read them, then sort the worries. They typically cluster into two categories: competence (they won't decide well) and clarity (they don't understand the goal) — both fixable, and both addressed later in the book.

A companion mechanism, "act your way to new thinking," addresses morale and culture change directly rather than waiting for attitudes to shift first. Rather than debating philosophy, Santa Fe's officers asked: what would we observe if the crew were actually proud of this ship? They generated concrete, observable behaviors (bragging to family, wearing ship gear, greeting visitors confidently) and then simply installed one as a practice: the "three-name rule" (greet a visitor using the visitor's name, your own name, and the ship's name). The premise: you can talk and act your way into a new mindset faster than you can think your way into new behavior, especially under time pressure. The generalized version: have the group complete "I'd know we'd achieved [cultural change] if I saw employees ___," sort for measurable answers, then codify the resulting behavior directly into a written practice or procedure — not a slogan.

## 4. Control: "I Intend To..." — The Language Mechanism

The book's signature mechanism starts from an incident where Marquet, unfamiliar with a new submarine class, ordered a speed setting ("ahead two thirds") that didn't exist on that ship's electric propulsion motor. His senior officer of the deck relayed the order anyway, assuming the captain must know something secret. When asked why he'd follow an order he knew was wrong, the answer was simply "Because you told me to." Marquet's conclusion: in a top-down culture, when the leader is wrong, everyone goes over the cliff with him. He resolved never to give another order.

The replacement mechanism: officers stopped requesting permission ("Request permission to...") and started declaring intent ("Captain, I intend to submerge the ship"). Marquet's only response, when satisfied, was "Very well." This shifts psychological ownership of the plan from the person granting permission to the person executing it.

Disempowered phrases the book flags for elimination: "Request permission to...", "I would like to...", "What should I do about...", "Do you think we should...", "Could we..."
Empowered phrases to replace them: "I intend to...", "I plan on...", "I will...", "We will..."

The mechanism was extended a second time: rather than asking follow-up questions himself, Marquet asked the officer to anticipate his questions and answer them before being asked ("Why don't you just tell me why you think it's safe and appropriate to submerge — all I'll need to say is 'Very well'"). This forces subordinates to think at the next level up the chain, which Marquet credits as an unplanned leadership-development program: the way the ship was run was the training. One explicit boundary was set: "I intend to..." only applied when the captain was awake and available to hear it — control was divested, not abandoned.

## 5. Control: Removing the Leader from the Loop

Several further control mechanisms remove structural habits that keep the leader as the bottleneck or the center of gravity:

**Resist the urge to provide solutions.** After personally pointing at a chart and specifying a tactical position without explaining his reasoning, Marquet watched the plan fail overnight because the crew had no understanding of the assumptions behind it — they had the answer, not the thinking. The fix going forward: even in time-pressured moments, create space (sometimes only seconds) for the team to reach the decision itself. Three response patterns depending on urgency: if a decision is truly urgent, make it, then have the team red-team it afterward; if it can wait briefly, solicit input first, then decide; if it can be delayed further, force the team to produce the input themselves and resist manufacturing consensus — dissent should be preserved, not smoothed over, because uniform agreement means the extra people add nothing.

**Eliminate top-down monitoring systems.** Santa Fe ran a "tickler," a binder tracking every outstanding message or task owed to higher authority, reviewed in long weekly meetings. The system tracked whether work was known about, not whether it got done. Marquet's reframe: whoever owns the outcome should own tracking it — department heads, not the XO, should monitor their own department's obligations. Killing the formal monitoring layer forced clarity about who was actually responsible, and, contrary to fears, follow-through improved rather than worsened. Marquet distinguishes this from data collection that simply "makes the invisible visible" without judgment — that kind of measurement stays; what goes is any system built for senior people to check up on junior people.

**Think out loud.** Formal military communication is deliberately terse and unambiguous (a valve is "shut," never "closed," to avoid confusion with "blow"). That precision crowds out the informal, probabilistic language needed for real teamwork: "I think," "I'm assuming," "it's likely." Santa Fe deliberately cultivated a constant low-level "buzz" of hypothesis, doubt, and running commentary among watchstanders, on the theory that a quiet control room is not a sign of good discipline but a sign that people aren't sharing what they see. This mechanism serves both control (it tells the captain what's about to happen so he doesn't need to intervene) and clarity (it transmits context and reasoning down the chain, not just orders).

**Embrace the inspectors.** Rather than minimizing exposure to external auditors and oversight bodies (Naval Reactors, squadron inspection teams) after a mistake — a shore-power red-tag violation, in the book's example — Santa Fe proactively invited them to critiques and used inspections as free consulting: sharing what the crew did well, and surfacing unsolved problems for outside help. Marquet frames this as control at the organizational level: the crew is in charge of its own destiny, including its own reputation, rather than being a "victim" managed by outside authority.

## 6. Competence: Deliberate Action

Competence is the first of the two pillars that make control safe. Its signature mechanism, deliberate action, was born from investigating a red-tag safety violation: a sailor moved a safety tag aside and threw a breaker without thinking, "on auto." The fix wasn't more training or more supervision (both were tested against the actual incident and found not to explain it) — it was requiring a deliberate pause, verbal statement, and physical gesture toward an action before executing it, specifically to interrupt automatic, unthinking behavior at the exact point where a human touches equipment.

Deliberate action turned out to deliver three separate benefits beyond reducing individual error:
- It let adjacent operators catch a mistake before it happened, because the pause created a window for intervention.
- It functioned as a form of anticipatory signaling: a chief who physically moved toward the correct next switch, without being told, silently cued the officer in charge what to order next — a bottom-up form of communication under stress.
- Later reactor inspectors told Marquet that Santa Fe's crew "tried to make the same number of mistakes as everyone else" but the mistakes never actually happened, because they were caught by the operator himself or a teammate before completion. This is the definition of a resilient system: not fewer attempted errors, but errors that don't propagate.

The mechanism generalizes beyond physical equipment: any point where someone signs a form, authorizes an action, or commits a keystroke is a candidate for deliberate action.

## 7. Competence: Learning as the Basic Unit of Work

Santa Fe replaced the word "training" with "learning" as its organizing verb, on the argument that training is something done to you (passive) while learning is something you do (active). The crew's creed, built collaboratively, reduced the ship's purpose to a single recurring answer: "What do we do on a day-to-day basis? We learn." Every routine task — maintenance, drills, even cleaning — was reframed as an opportunity to extract learning rather than a chore to complete.

The logic chain behind this, made explicit: training increases technical competence; increased competence justifies delegating more decisions; more decision-making authority increases engagement, motivation, and initiative; the result is higher productivity and morale. Skipping the first link (competence) and jumping straight to decision-delegation is what produced chaos on Marquet's earlier ship, the Will Rogers — he called this "control without competence is chaos," and named it as his own prior failure.

A structured exercise for connecting training directly to delegated authority: complete the sentence "Our company would be more effective if [level] management could make decisions about [subject]," sort for a short list, then ask "what, technically, do people at this level need to know to make that decision?" — the answer becomes your training curriculum, explicitly tied to the authority it unlocks (so people know why they're attending and want to).

**Don't brief, certify.** A brief is a one-way, passive event: someone reads a procedure aloud, everyone nods, and the only actual responsibility is showing up. A certification reverses this: the person in charge asks the team questions, and the team must demonstrate readiness before the operation is authorized to proceed — if they can't, the operation is postponed. This shifts the burden of preparation from the briefer onto every participant, and Marquet reports the visible effect: sailors began studying on their own time, unprompted, once they knew they'd be asked rather than told.

**Continually and consistently repeat the message.** Even after officers had visibly agreed to a new authority structure and had participated in defining it, old behavior resurfaced under stress (chiefs privileging their own comfort over their sailors' wellbeing, in the book's example). Marquet's conclusion: people hear a new approach described and think "I know what that's like," picturing something from their past experience, even when what's being described has never existed before — so the message has to be repeated relentlessly, without altering its content, until the picture in people's heads actually changes. He kept a poster on his door of a dog being told "sit" eight times before finally sitting, captioned "Good dog," as a personal reminder not to react to slow uptake as defiance.

**Specify goals, not methods.** A fire drill's timing had a documented, fixed narrative regardless of how fast the crew actually responded, so there was no incentive to move fast. Santa Fe rewrote the standard so that if the crew reached the fire within a defined time window, "the fire was out. Done." — the drill's outcome was now genuinely contingent on the crew's actual performance. Separately, watchstanders were told only the goal ("attack the fire," "a thermal imager is needed in the auxiliary room") without being told who should do it or how — the nearest capable person self-organized. Applied to berthing assignments, this same principle led sailors to rearrange their own bunk assignments around proximity to the damage-control gear they were each responsible for, entirely on their own initiative, once the goal (fastest response) was clear and the method was left open.

## 8. Clarity: Achieving Excellence Instead of Avoiding Errors

Clarity is the second supporting pillar: as control moves down the chain, everyone making decisions needs a shared, correct understanding of what the organization is actually trying to accomplish, because decisions are made against that understanding as a criterion.

The foundational clarity mechanism, introduced early in the book, reframes the organization's central motivation. The nuclear Navy's culture of rigorously tracking and dissecting errors had, on Santa Fe, curdled into a crew whose real goal was simply not making mistakes — and Marquet observed that the surest way not to make a mistake is to not do anything or decide anything. Success under this frame is a negative: an absence of failure. Once a unit reaches "no major errors," there's no reason to push further. Marquet's deliberate substitute: make achieving excellence the goal, with error reduction as a downstream side benefit rather than the target itself. This reframing is presented as feeding organizational clarity because it changes the criterion every subordinate decision gets measured against — not "did I avoid trouble" but "did this make us excellent."

## 9. Clarity: Trust, Legacy, and Guiding Principles

**Build trust and take care of your people.** When Santa Fe's petty officers scored badly on a Navy-wide advancement exam because no one had explained how the scoring actually worked, Marquet ran the numbers himself and discovered the exam accounted for roughly 80 percent of the variance between advanced and non-advanced sailors, even though it was formally only a third of the composite score. This reframed advancement from "a mystical process you don't control" to "a lever you can pull," and the crew's exam performance, and reenlistment rate, both rose sharply once this was made transparent. Marquet's distinction on what "taking care of people" actually means: it is not protecting them from the consequences of their own behavior (that breeds irresponsibility) — it is giving them every available tool to achieve their own goals, professional or personal, including helping some crew members find that Navy life wasn't right for them and exit on good terms.

**Use your legacy for inspiration.** Submariners have an unusually rich legacy (the World War II submarine force, at 2 percent of Navy personnel, sank over half of all Japanese vessels lost) that goes largely uninvoked day to day. Santa Fe built small, low-cost rituals around it: announcing over the intercom when passing the last known location of a submarine lost in WWII, reading Medal of Honor citations when qualifying new crew in submarines, treating a visit to the local submarine museum as officer training. The point is connecting daily, granular work to a larger and genuinely noble purpose, which Marquet treats as a clarity mechanism because it answers the "why" question the crew would otherwise fill in with something smaller (avoid trouble, please the inspector).

**Use guiding principles for decision criteria.** Santa Fe's crew collaboratively wrote and repeatedly used a one-page set of guiding principles (Initiative, Innovation, Intimate Technical Knowledge, Courage, Commitment, Continuous Improvement, Integrity, Empowerment, Teamwork, Openness, Timeliness). The test Marquet applied when drafting them: if a crew member faced a real decision between two courses of action, would this principle actually help them choose? Principles that don't pass that test are wall decoration. He also warns that guiding principles must describe the real organization, not the aspirational one — a company that claims "safety first" while actually balancing safety against profit will produce garbled decisions and lost credibility, because employees can see the gap between the words and the real priority. A blunt diagnostic: ask the first three people you meet in an organization what its stated principles or motto actually mean. If they can't answer, the principles aren't doing any work.

**Use immediate recognition to reinforce desired behaviors.** After a near-collision was averted by an alert throttleman, Marquet pinned a Navy Achievement Medal on him at breakfast, immediately, rather than waiting the usual months for the paperwork to process — deliberately distinguishing "immediate" from "eventually." A structural point about award design: awards that are scarce (only the top 10 percent can win) put employees in competition with each other; awards that are abundant and tied to a fixed external standard ("every team that gets a hose to the fire in under two minutes gets full marks") put the whole group in competition with nature or an external rival instead, which produces collaboration rather than internal rivalry. Santa Fe deliberately pushed the "competition boundary" out to the skin of the ship — no internal "us vs. them" between departments, only the ship versus the mission.

## 10. Clarity: Long-Term Thinking and Encouraging Dissent

**Begin with the end in mind** (explicitly borrowed from Stephen Covey). Santa Fe's department heads were assigned, as homework, to write their own end-of-tour award citation, or performance evaluation a year or more out, forcing concrete, measurable long-term goals rather than vague aspirations ("improve procedural compliance" was pushed until it became "reduce critiques by a specific percentage"). Marquet frames the payoff in two ways: individually, officers who did this exercise actually achieved close to what they'd written, two years later; organizationally, this same long-horizon habit let the crew pull off getting the ship combat-ready three weeks ahead of a scheduled deployment date, purely because the target had been set months in advance and treated as fixed. When the Navy later moved the deployment date up by eleven days on short notice, only the ship already running ahead of its own deadline could absorb the change.

**Encourage a questioning attitude over blind obedience.** During a real nighttime SEAL-team pickup, Marquet gave a direct, urgent order to move the ship in a direction he was certain was correct — and the quartermaster on watch flatly told him he was wrong. He was: the crew had positioned the ship deliberately for a fast exit and Marquet's read of the tactical display was backward. Following his own order would have caused the ship to miss the pickup. Marquet uses this to argue that resilient organizations are ones where errors are caught and stopped, not ones where orders are obeyed regardless of whether they're right — and invokes the 2012 Costa Concordia grounding as the failure case: a chain of officers who apparently never questioned a captain's unauthorized course change into shallow water near an island.

## 11. From Empowerment to Emancipation

The book's final conceptual move distinguishes "empowerment" from what Marquet calls "emancipation," and treats the distinction as necessary, not semantic. Empowerment is a needed corrective step, because organizations have already disempowered people and that has to be actively undone — but empowerment by itself is incomplete for two reasons. First, it doesn't work without competence and clarity already in place; giving someone authority they aren't equipped to use well just produces bad decisions. Second, empowerment still structurally originates top-down: it rests on the premise that the leader holds the power and chooses to grant some of it, which is itself a leader-follower frame wearing different language.

Emancipation, by contrast, starts from the premise that the talent, energy, and judgment already exist in every person, and the leader's role is to stop suppressing them rather than to dispense them. Marquet's practical test for whether a team has actually reached this state: you know a team is emancipated when you no longer have the ability to empower them, because they were never relying on you as the source of their authority in the first place. The book's closer example: when Santa Fe needed hydraulic oil mid-deployment in the Arabian Gulf, a brand-new, unconstrained ensign simply suggested radioing a nearby resupply ship directly — something outside normal channel-and-protocol procedure — and within minutes the entire crew self-organized (surfacing, boat handling, mail transfer, medical checkups, oil transfer) announcing each step with "I intend to..." and receiving only "Very well" in response. No part of that sequence was ordered.

## 12. Diagnostic Questions and Practical Exercises

Marquet ends most chapters with a short set of reflection questions rather than prescriptive steps, and several chapters include a repeatable group exercise. Recurring diagnostic questions worth extracting as a standalone checklist for any organization:

- Where in our policy documents is decision-making authority explicitly assigned, and could any of it move down a level with a one-word change?
- When we think about delegating a given decision, what specifically do we worry about — and is that worry really about competence, or about clarity?
- Do our people use disempowered language ("request permission," "what should I do") or empowered language ("I intend to," "I will")?
- Are we running any monitoring or briefing system whose only function is to let senior people check up on junior people, as opposed to genuinely making the invisible visible?
- Do our people know they'll be asked to demonstrate readiness (certification) or only expected to show up and nod (briefing)?
- Is our organization's real motivation to avoid errors, or to achieve excellence — and would our people describe their job the same way we would?
- Do our stated guiding principles actually function as decision criteria, or are they wall art nobody could explain if asked?
- Are our recognition and awards systems scarce and competitive (pitting employees against each other) or abundant and externally targeted (pitting the team against a fixed standard or an outside rival)?
- Would our people tell us we're wrong if we were about to make a real mistake, and have we ever actually tested that?
- Are we rewarded, structurally, for what happens to the organization after we personally leave it — or only for our own tenure?

The recurring shape of the group exercises Marquet describes: hand out cards, have people complete a specific, structured sentence stem individually, post the cards on a wall, take a break to let people mill around and read them, then sort and prioritize as a group. He uses this same format for finding delegable decisions, for defining a cultural change in measurable terms, and for connecting training topics to the authority they're meant to unlock — the format itself (private written input, public posting, group sort) is presented as a reusable mechanism independent of the specific topic.
