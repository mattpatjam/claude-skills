# Fanatical Prospecting — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing prospecting/outbound/pipeline processes, or advising on sales activity management.*
*Book: "Fanatical Prospecting" — Jeb Blount*

---

## 1. The Case for Prospecting, the Seven Mindsets, and the Fine Art of Interrupting

The book's central claim is a Paradox of Basics, a truth so obvious it has become invisible: the top 20 percent of salespeople who produce 80 percent of sales are separated from everyone else by one thing, fanatical prospecting. Not talent, not skills, not territory, not product, not presentation ability, not closing technique, not sales managers. The number one reason for failure in sales is an empty pipe, and the root cause of an empty pipeline is the failure to prospect. Blount's working definition of fanatical is taken from the dictionary: "motivated or characterized by an extreme, uncritical enthusiasm." The enduring mantra of the fanatical prospector is *one more call*.

The book refuses to soften the work. There is no Easy Button in sales. Prospecting is hard, grueling, rejection-dense, emotionally draining work, and it is the price of a high income. The correct response is Jim Rohn's: don't wish that things were easier, wish that you were better. Techniques in the book will make a prospector more efficient (more prospecting done in less time) and more effective (higher return per unit of prospecting time), but they will not eliminate rejection, make prospecting palatable, or remove the emotional roadblocks that cause procrastination. In sales, business, and life there are only three things a salesperson controls: **actions**, **reactions**, and **mindset**. Everything else is outside the frame and complaining about it is wasted energy.

Success leaves clues. Studying fanatical prospectors surfaces seven core mindsets that recur across every industry, from insurance to real estate to software to medical device:

- **Optimistic and enthusiastic** — attacks each day as a fresh opportunity, brushes past naysayers, reaches for stored enthusiasm on bad days to make one more call. Negative, bitter, victim-mindset people do not succeed in sales.
- **Competitive** — hardwired to win, prepared each day to win the battle for the attention of the most coveted prospects and to outwit and outhustle competitors.
- **Confident** — expects to win, has developed mental toughness and the ability to manage the disruptive emotions of fear, uncertainty, and doubt, and leverages confidence and self-control to persuade prospects to give up time and resources.
- **Relentless** — high need for achievement, never gives up, uses rejection as motivational fuel on the belief that the next yes is around the corner.
- **Thirsty for knowledge** — welcomes feedback and coaching, consumes books, podcasts, audiobooks, online training, and seminars, and views setbacks as opportunities to learn.
- **Systematic and efficient** — executes with near-robotic efficiency, protects the Golden Hours, blocks time, concentrates power, and systematically develops the prospect database into more targeted lists.
- **Adaptive and flexible** — acute situational awareness, early adoption of new techniques and technology, working the three As: **adopt, adapt, adept** (find new best practices, make them your own, work until execution is expert).

The industry argument over whether cold calling is dead is dismissed as "an inane argument focused on semantics of degrees, cold, warm, smoldering, hot, smoking hot," and mostly as cover for never making an outbound call again. The real mechanic is interruption. Sustained success requires interrupting prospects: picking up the phone, walking in the door, sending an e-mail or text, or pinging someone on a social channel when they are not expecting contact and no sales conversation is currently under way. This is true whether the contact is a stranger, a web-form lead, a white paper download, a new social connection, a dormant customer, a trade show contact, or an existing client being called for a cross-sell. It is not the "cold" call that is hard, it is the interrupting. Reps are afraid to make the call, not the cold call. The observed anxiety pattern is identical on warm lay-ups (reactivation calls to former customers) as on calls to complete strangers: obsessing over unknowns, wanting every duck in a row, planning to plan to plan to call.

The structural reason interruption cannot be avoided is that most companies cannot generate enough qualified inbound leads to keep a pipeline full, and reps at the companies that can are earning far less than reps who reach out and create their own opportunities. The largest, most lucrative prospects in a market rarely respond to inbound marketing or social selling at all, because there is already a line of salespeople at their door and they have no reason to engage that way. The only way to start a conversation with them is to interrupt them.

The corresponding strategic principle is **balance**. Relying consistently on a single prospecting methodology, usually the one that generates the least resistance and rejection, consistently generates mediocre results. Balanced prospecting is the portfolio-diversification analogy applied to sales: a deliberate mixture of telephone, in-person, e-mail, social selling, text messaging, referrals, networking, inbound leads, trade shows, and cold calling. The line "But I'm so much better at ..." is diagnosed as an excuse, and reliably indicates a rep who is underperforming against their number.

There is no one-size-fits-all formula. The correct distribution is set by industry, product, service, deal complexity, customer base, territory, geography, economic drivers, position against goal, and tenure. High-density zip codes may favor in-person over phone. Consulting may lean social; certain software may lean inbound. Tenure matters most: a rep new to a territory, company, or industry must be prepared to do heavy dialing or heavy door-knocking and a good bit of cold calling to qualify and build the database, while a tenured rep in the same territory shifts toward referrals, social selling, lead nurturing, and timely calls into opening buying windows. The classic rookie error is emulating the 20-year veteran's low-effort-looking regimen without recognizing that the veteran's success was paid for in advance with years of fanatical prospecting that qualified his database and tuned him to buying windows. A rep selling small and mid-market who adopts the regimen of an enterprise account manager will likely fail.

## 2. The Three Laws of Prospecting and the Anatomy of a Sales Slump

Top sales professionals spend as much as 80 percent of their time on prospecting and qualifying activity, to get up to the plate often and string together singles, doubles, triples, and a few home runs. Three core laws govern whether a steady stream of prospects keeps moving into the pipe:

- **The Universal Law of Need** — governs desperation. The more you need something, the less likely you are to get it. When all hope for survival rests on one, two, or a handful of accounts, the probability of failure rises exponentially. Three mechanisms: desperation taps the downside of the Law of Attraction (you focus on what happens if you fail, and attract it); other people sense desperation through actions, tone of voice, words, and body language, and prospects naturally repel needy, desperate salespeople while gravitating toward those who exude confidence; and desperation makes you emotional and illogical, producing poor decisions that deepen the hole.
- **The 30-Day Rule** — the prospecting you do in a given 30-day period pays off for the next 90 days. Miss a day and it bites you within 90 days. Miss a week and you feel it in your commission check. Miss a month and you tank the pipeline, fall into a slump, and wake up 90 days later desperate with no idea how you got there. Almost always in play in B2B and high-end B2C; in shorter-cycle transactional sales it may compress to a One-Week Rule, with the concept unchanged. The diagnostic value is that it explains the lag: a rep who stopped prospecting in December experiences it as a *closing* problem in March and goes looking for closing techniques, when the cause is 90 days upstream.
- **The Law of Replacement** — you must push new opportunities into the pipeline at a rate that matches or exceeds your closing ratio, because closing one deal removes more than one prospect. The worked example: a rep with 30 prospects and a 10 percent closing ratio who closes one deal has 20 prospects left, not 29, because statistically one close consumes ten prospects. Failure to heed this law is why salespeople get on and stay on the sales roller coaster, and it is the mechanism behind the classic pattern of a manager pushing the team to fill the pipe, two record months, then a collapse because prospecting intensity stopped once there were deals to work.

Ninety-nine percent of sales slumps link directly to a failure to prospect. The anatomy runs in a fixed sequence: prospecting stops (30-Day Rule), the pipeline stalls (Law of Replacement), dead prospects stop closing, confidence erodes, negative self-talk further degrades confidence and enthusiasm, feeling like a loser saps energy and motivation for prospecting, the rep calls the same dead-end prospects over and over while deluding himself that this counts as prospecting activity, the stale pipe gets worse, hope replaces strategy, and the Universal Law of Need lands. **The first rule of sales slumps** mirrors the first rule of holes: when you are in one, start prospecting. Recovery requires putting all energy into controllable actions and typically takes about 30 days of dedicated daily activity to get back on track. Arnold Palmer's "the more I practice, the luckier I get" has an exact parallel: the more you prospect, the luckier you get. It is far more important to prospect *consistently*, every day, than to prospect with the best techniques.

## 3. The Three Ps That Hold You Back, and Mental Toughness

Three mindsets hold salespeople back from prospecting.

- **Procrastination** — the failure to do the little things every day. You cannot do a month of prospecting in one day; the elephant is eaten one bite at a time. Every major failure traces to a collapse in the self-discipline to do small daily disciplines, typically followed by an embarrassing crescendo of desperate, hurried, wasted activity trying to catch up. "Procrastination is the grave in which opportunity is buried."
- **Perfectionism** — the arch enemy of fanatical prospecting, because it generates both procrastination and fear of failure. The contrast case: a rep who perfects his desk, script, and per-prospect research makes seven calls in three hours and gets nothing, while the rep next door runs a list and dials, making 53 calls, reaching 14 decision makers, setting two qualified appointments, and sending 39 prospecting e-mails in one hour, and earns roughly $100,000 more. Zig Ziglar's rule applies: "Anything worth doing is worth doing poorly." Messy success beats perfect mediocrity, because the activity gap is never compensated by the research delta. Research and organization are legitimate, and necessary for C-level or complex, expensive sales, but they belong in advance, before and after the Golden Hours, never inside the prospecting block.
- **Paralysis from analysis** — the "what if" binge ("What if they say no?" "How will I know if ...?"), usually driven by perfectionism, and distinct from true call reluctance. Blount's distinction is blunt: a person genuinely unable to will their fingers to dial is in the wrong job and should quit; analysis paralysis is fixable, and the fix is to make one call, then the next, then the next, with a coach dialing alongside, and to let the what-ifs take care of themselves.

Mental toughness, sometimes called **grit**, is presented as more important than talent, experience, education, skills, or technique, and as learnable (unlike talent and intelligence). James Loehr's seven core dimensions of mental toughness: self-confidence, attention control, minimizing negative energy, increasing positive energy, maintaining motivation levels, attitude control, and visual and imagery control. Croner and Abraham's three dimensions in salespeople: **optimism** (the mother of perseverance), **competitiveness** (the mother of persistence; the drive to avoid losing keeps superstars working longer and harder), and **need for achievement** (the mother of self-motivation; Henry Murray's definition, "intense, prolonged and repeated efforts to accomplish something difficult ... to work with singleness of purpose towards a high and distant goal").

The four pillars of mental toughness in sales:

- **Desire** — the starting point of all achievement. Answer three questions: What do you want? How do you plan to get it? How bad do you want it? Then write the goals and plan down, because written goals force action and create forward momentum that carries past procrastination, perfectionism, and paralysis. If you don't have a plan, you become part of someone else's plan.
- **Mental resilience** — sharpening the saw. Deliberate, continuous investment in learning: books, seminars, workshops, newsletters, trade publications, blogs, podcasts, webinars. Outlearn equals outearn. The reading math: 250 working days at 15 minutes a day is 62.5 hours a year, and at 2 to 3 hours per business book that is roughly 21 books a year. Drive time becomes "Automobile University" (inside reps commute 1 to 2 hours a day, outside reps spend 4 to 5 hours a day in a car).
- **Physical resilience** — mental energy is capped by physical condition. Three pillars: 30 minutes to an hour of exercise a day (10 minutes at a time is as effective as one long session), seven to nine hours of sleep a night, and healthy eating, with one cardinal rule, always eat breakfast, because it kick-starts the metabolism and supplies the discipline to open the day with a high-energy prospecting block.
- **Attitude** — people with positive attitude share two beliefs: they expect to win, and they believe everything happens for a reason (so setbacks prompt "How can I learn from this?" rather than "Why me?"). Four levers when attitude loses altitude: change the company you keep, change your self-talk, change your input, and change your focus. Gratitude is the cornerstone.

When you are in second place, attack the leader; when you are in first place, attack yourself. Winners break down a brilliant performance and make small adjustments, because the 30-Day Rule punishes complacency.

## 4. Know Your Numbers: Efficiency, Effectiveness, and the Worth of an Hour

Sales is and always has been governed by numbers, because the formula is mathematical: what (quality) you put into the pipe and how much (quantity) determines what comes out. Quantity is the science; the quality dimension (size of prospects, qualification level, depth of penetration with decision makers, influencers, and coaches) is part science, part art. Elite athletes can recite their statistics on demand because knowing the numbers is what allows directional correction. Elite salespeople track everything: calls, contacts, e-mails, responses, appointments, sales, social prospecting touches, text messages sent, and new prospects or new information points added to the database.

Tracking opens the door to honest assessment of two distinct variables:

- **Efficiency** — how much activity you generate inside the time block allotted for a particular prospecting activity.
- **Effectiveness** — the ratio between activity and outcome.

The formula is **Efficiency + Effectiveness = Performance**, and the job is to optimize the balance. One hundred dials in an hour that produce no new information, no appointments, or appointments only with low potential prospects (LPPs) is efficient and ineffective. Ten calls that set one highly qualified appointment and update two database records is effective and extremely inefficient, because the block was suboptimized. Dozens of variables drive both: list quality, industry vertical, time of day, time of year, day of week, decision-maker role, product or service, complex versus transactional, call objective, channel, quality of approach, methodology, message, confidence, and mindset.

Critically, the tracking must be **manual and visual**. This is the commonality Blount observes among top performers across all market segments, inside and outside: each has their own style of manual tracking, and each knows exactly where they stand. Most salespeople don't track because delusion is more comfortable than reality. The illustrative case: a rep certain he had made "easily 50 calls" had made 12 over seven hours, and had lost touch with reality precisely because he let the system track for him instead of tracking visually himself. You cannot be delusional and successful at the same time.

The corollary discipline is **knowing your worth per hour**: Annual Income Goal ÷ (Number of Working Weeks × Golden Hours) = What You Are Worth an Hour. The worked example arrives at $52 an hour, which converts 12 hours of self-performed home repair, taken instead of paying a $150 specialist, into a $624 cost. The **Law of Triviality** is the human tendency to waste time on unimportant activities while mission-critical tasks are ignored; it is not uncommon for salespeople to waste 50 percent or more of their time on low-value activity. The per-hour number becomes the gauge for whether any task, activity, or assignment moves you toward your goals, and makes the cost of doing $10-an-hour work during $50-an-hour prime selling time visible.

## 5. Time: Golden Hours, Platinum Hours, and Time Blocking

Asked their greatest sales challenge more than 10,000 times, 80 percent of sales professionals and leaders name time management. For salespeople, most time-management problems are self-inflicted. Every salesperson has 24 hours, only a handful of which are available for selling.

- **Golden Hours** — prime selling time. The hours available for interacting with qualified prospects and moving them into and through the pipeline. Roughly eight per day. If you are not prospecting, qualifying, information gathering, presenting, or closing during Golden Hours, you are not doing your job.
- **Platinum Hours** — the periods before and after the Golden Hours, reserved for necessary nonselling work: building prospecting lists, research, precall planning, developing proposals and presentations, creating contracts and getting approval, social selling activities, e-mail prospecting, call objective planning, planning and organization, administration and reports, responding to e-mail, calendar management, and CRM management.

The enabling mindset is the **CEO mindset**: seeing yourself as the CEO of You, Inc., accountable for results with no one to push blame onto, tasked with generating the highest possible ROI from scarce resources, the scarcest of which is time. Four moves protect the Golden Hours against nonsales encroachment: **just say no** to non-mission-critical requests, consistently, until others get the message; **prioritize** (no salesperson consistently hitting her number has ever been fired for unfinished administrative tasks, while tens of thousands get canned for missing their number); **move important nonsales activity into the Platinum Hours**, which means getting up early, going to bed late, and doing some weekend work; and **delegate**, which is how you scale yourself. Effective delegation begins with clear instructions given in advance, then systematic follow-up ("In God we trust; everyone else, we follow up on"), plus a real investment in relationships with support staff.

Time blocking is described as transformational, and rests on **Horstman's Corollary to Parkinson's Law**: Parkinson's Law says work expands to fill the time allotted; the corollary says work contracts to fit the time allowed. The demonstration case: an inside team averaging barely two prospecting calls an hour, given 30 minutes and a list of 25 prospects, averaged 22 dials and at least one appointment, and after coaching averaged 29 dials in 30 minutes and two appointments, more than they had been producing in an entire eight-hour day. Blount's own team runs three **Power Hours** spread across the day (morning, midday, afternoon), producing roughly 120 outbound prospecting calls a day in three hours, with no e-mail, no research, no CRM management, no social media, and no breaks inside the block. Power Hours work for two reasons: work contracts to fit the time allotted, and anybody can stay focused for an hour. The stated guarantee: an hour a day of 25 to 50 teleprospecting calls plus an hour for e-mail and social prospecting packs a pipeline in under 60 days.

Blocks must be treated as sacred appointments with yourself, scheduled on the calendar, defended the way a booked demo with a hot prospect would be defended, with a "Do not disturb" sign on the door. **Concentrate your power**: the brain does not multitask, it cycles rapidly between tasks, so prospecting efficiency decreases in direct proportion to the number of things attempted at once. The commercial insurance case went from seven dials in an hour (eight and a half minutes per dial, while busy multitasking) to 47 dials an hour by a pure concentration of power: one task only (dial the phone), notes made on the printed list with 30 minutes blocked afterward for CRM logging, mobile devices off and in a drawer, e-mail off, signs on doors, and research and call objectives completed before the block. Two derailers dominate: mobile devices (the average person looks at their phone screen every seven minutes) and e-mail. Anthony Iannarino's prospecting rule number one is never to check e-mail first thing in the morning, because once you open yourself to the demands of the outside world it is very difficult to bring full attention to the most important task of the day, which is prospecting. Important does not mean urgent; genuinely urgent matters arrive by call or text, not e-mail. Block the first one to two hours of each day for a focused telephone prospecting block, then manage e-mail.

## 6. The Four Objectives of Prospecting

Knowing the objective of each prospecting touch makes you efficient, because touches can be grouped into blocks by objective, and effective, because you know exactly what to ask for and how to bridge to the prospect's problems. The objective is the primary outcome expected from the touch. There are four core objectives: **set an appointment**, **gather information and qualify**, **close a sale**, and **build familiarity**. Rules of thumb for assigning them:

- Complex, high-risk, high-cost product or service: primary objective is an appointment with a qualified decision maker, influencer, or stakeholder; secondary is gathering information; tertiary is building familiarity.
- Transactional, low-risk, low-cost product in inside sales: primary is closing the sale, secondary is gathering information.
- Transactional, low-risk, low-cost in outside sales via any channel other than in person: primary is setting an appointment, secondary gathering information. Prospecting in person (knocking): primary is closing the deal.
- Highly qualified database: primary is setting appointments as buying windows open, secondary is building familiarity so the prospect engages when the window opens.
- Purchases possible only in specific buying windows (contract expiry, budgetary period): primary is gathering information to qualify the buying window, secondary is familiarity, shifting to appointment-setting once the window is identified. Never waste an appointment on a prospect who cannot buy because of contractual or budgetary handcuffs.
- New territory, start-up, or new division: primary is gathering information to identify decision makers and qualify buying windows and budgets, secondary is building familiarity.

Prospecting is a contact sport that shuns the nuance, art, and finesse of moving a deal through the pipe. It is not for building relationships, selling, or chatting up the buyer. No small talk, no chitchat, no long-winded scripts. Get to the point, ask for what you want, move to the next touch.

**Set an appointment** — the most valuable activity in the sales process at any stage. An appointment exists only when it is on your calendar and the prospect's calendar and the prospect expects you to show up in person or by phone, video, or web conference at a specific time, date, and place. "Just stop by," "I'll be here anytime," and "call me maybe" are brush-offs, not appointments; treating them as appointments is delusion, and it is what produces an 80 percent no-show rate.

**Gather information and qualify** — Coach Sandro's rule, "don't swing at nothin' ugly." Savvy professionals are disciplined about qualifying because qualified buyers are scarce and a moment spent with a prospect who will never buy is a moment taken from finding one who will. Setting an appointment is the primary objective with prequalified prospects; gathering information is the primary objective with unqualified ones. The bell-curve distribution of any database: a small percentage totally qualified and in the buying window; a larger percentage totally qualified but out of the window for budget or contract reasons; a larger percentage semi-qualified with holes in the data; an even larger percentage potential buyers with almost no or outdated information; and a small percentage who will never buy, are out of business, or are bogus records. The corresponding actions are to set appointments with the highly qualified and in-window, nurture the qualified but out-of-window, gather information on the partially known, and eliminate the bogus, too small, too big, or never-buyers. It is defensible to just set the appointment and qualify later when the product is noncontractual, most prospects are probable buyers, there is no set budgetary period, and the decision-maker role is consistent and usually a single person. Qualify first when the product is complex or contractual (especially with exclusivity), the cycle is long, decisions are made high in the organization, or budgets are set and approved in advance. The first step is to **define the strike zone**: a profile of the qualified prospect, including the optimal time to engage before the buying window opens, built from decision-making roles, account size, buying windows, budgetary windows, and contractual obligations, or in a small company from product and delivery strengths and weaknesses, patterns among best customers, and trigger events. Then measure every prospect, deal, and customer against the profile and develop the discipline to walk away when they don't fit, while distinguishing a calculated, data-driven risk from chasing an ugly deal.

**Close the sale** — the objective when selling transactional, low-risk, relatively low-cost products via phone and in person. It requires getting past the initial reflex response or brush-off, asking one or two qualifying questions, and gaining agreement on the spot for a conversation deep enough to bridge to a solution and close, all in a few minutes, with poise, confidence, and mastery of the sales process.

**Build familiarity** — almost always a secondary or tertiary objective, occasionally primary in strategic prospecting campaigns. Average touches required to engage, by prospect state: 1 to 3 to reengage an inactive customer; 1 to 5 for a prospect in the buying window who is familiar with you and your brand; 3 to 10 for a prospect with high familiarity but not in the buying window; 5 to 12 for a warm inbound lead; 5 to 20 for a prospect with some familiarity, buying-window dependent; and 20 to 50 for a cold prospect who does not know you or your brand.

## 7. The Prospecting Pyramid, Powerful Lists, and Owning the Database

Salespeople who struggle view the prospect database as a **square**, treating every prospect the same and attacking the database randomly with no system and no objective. This is statistically inefficient, because only a small number of prospects are in the buying window at any time, so random dialing has a high probability of reaching poorly qualified prospects. The result is ineffective blocks, far more rejection, low productivity, and damaged confidence and self-esteem.

Top performers view the database as a **pyramid**, segmented by potential or size of opportunity and probability of conversion, with a defined action at each level:

- Bottom: thousands of prospects known only by company name and perhaps some contact information, possibly incorrect. Action: move them up by gathering information to correct and confirm data, fill gaps, and begin qualifying.
- Next: solid contact information including e-mail addresses, possibly competitor data, usage numbers, budget size, other demographics, and contacts for decision makers and influencers. Action: identify the buying window and all potential stakeholders.
- Next: potential buying windows identified, complete contact records for decision makers and influencers including social profiles. Action: implement nurturing campaigns to stay in front of confirmed decision makers ahead of the identified future window.
- Next: **conquest prospects**, a highly targeted, deliberately limited list (10, 25, 50, 100) of the best or largest opportunities in the territory. Action: nurturing and regular touches, stakeholder identification, buying-window qualifying, trigger-event monitoring, and familiarity building.
- Next: hot inbound leads and referrals. Action: immediate follow-up to qualify and move into the pipeline.
- Top: highly qualified prospects moving into the buying window because of immediate need, contract expiration, trigger event, or budgetary period. Action: highest-priority, top of the daily list, move them into the pipe.

The key to the pyramid is a systematic daily focus on gathering qualifying information that identifies buying windows and stakeholders and moves prospects up. Operationally, start each morning with a block on the top-of-pyramid list while fresh, because in-window prospects convert more easily and early wins supply confidence and motivation for the rest of the day; then work conquest accounts for qualifying and nurturing; then qualify the lower pyramid.

**The quality of the list you work from during a prospecting block has a more significant impact on the success of the block than any other element except your mindset.** A high-quality list is the track for the prospecting train. Construct lists using these filters, in combination: prospecting objective (appointment, information, close, familiarity); prospecting channel (phone, e-mail, social, text, in person, networking); qualification level, highest at top; potential, largest at top; probability of achieving the objective, highest at top; territory plan (day of week, postal code, street, geographic grid, city); inbound leads; conquest prospects; decision maker or stakeholder role; industry or market vertical; customers purchasing a specific product or service type; seasonal customers; inactive customers; and leads from a recent trade show or conference. Automate as much as possible with preset filters, views, and reports.

The CRM is framed as the most important tool in the sales arsenal, because it manages details and tasks across many contacts, keeps you organized and prevents derailments, allows segmentation and sorting to build lists on any field, and supports systematic qualification up the pyramid. Stripped of technology, a CRM is a filing system that remembers important things and reminds you when they matter. **Own it like a CEO**: be accountable for database integrity, don't wait for a manager to scream, make copious notes after calls and log them, enter new leads rather than carrying business cards, and learn the tool through trial and error rather than complaining about it. The database is either a trash can or a gold mine. The operating philosophy: put every detail about every account and every interaction with every account and contact in the CRM, make good clear notes, never procrastinate, take no shortcuts. Building a database is like filling in a jigsaw puzzle, and the discipline is recognizing the cumulative value of small wins, celebrating the nugget of decision-maker information uncovered on a call that otherwise felt bad.

## 8. The Law of Familiarity and Social Selling

**The Law of Familiarity**: the more familiar a prospect is with you, your brand, or your company, the more likely they are to accept and return calls, open e-mails, accept a social connection request, respond to a text, accept an event or webinar invitation, download from a link, engage in sales conversations, and ultimately buy. Lack of familiarity is why requests for time draw so many objections. Familiarity lubricates prospecting because it makes the decision to give you time feel less risky. Sean Burke's **Familiarity Threshold** is the point at which a prospect will readily communicate and hold a first-name relationship even when not currently interested in buying, and crossing it also earns permission to use social inboxes and text messaging without being considered intrusive. Because crossing it requires significant investment of time, intellect, emotion, energy, and technology, it can only be done with a conquest list and strategic prospecting plans, not with everyone.

The **five levers of familiarity**:

- **Persistent and consistent prospecting** — every call, e-mail, face-to-face meeting, dropped business card, and voice mail creates familiarity, with the prospect and with the gatekeepers who protect them. This is a core reason persistence pays.
- **Referrals and introductions** — the most powerful and direct path, because a referral confers instant credibility by riding on the coattails of someone the prospect already trusts. Three types: **customer referrals** (from happy clients; the key is a disciplined systematic process for asking), **personal referrals** (friends, family, acquaintances; educate them on what you do and your ideal prospect, then keep reminding them), and **professional referrals** (from professionals in related industries or noncompeting salespeople calling on the same prospect type; mutually beneficial relationships requiring ongoing investment, and the wider the professional network the more referrals). The distilled core of every referral book: give a legendary customer experience, and ask. Asking is the missing discipline, as demonstrated by a room of 100 B2B reps in which every hand went up for great service and three hands went up for having asked for a referral in the past year.
- **Networking** — the real social prospecting. Check chambers of commerce, business and civic organization calendars, and ask prospects and customers which events, conferences, and trade shows they attend. Do not become a walking marketing brochure; create connections by asking questions, listening, and becoming genuinely interested. Follow up to anchor the relationship: handwritten notes referencing something specific from the conversation, a short thank-you text, a LinkedIn connection request, all leads logged in the CRM no later than the next morning, and any promised action taken within 24 hours.
- **Company and brand familiarity** — a recognized brand makes prospecting exponentially easier; a start-up or unknown company puts the rep at a structural disadvantage and makes the sales team an integral part of brand building and market awareness (blogs, trade shows, social network, white papers, e-books, podcasts, webinars). At a large company with a marketing machine, the key is staying out of its gears and letting marketing do its job.
- **Personal branding** — direct investment in awareness of your name, expertise, and reputation, because people buy you. The underused secret weapon is speaking in public regularly, which creates an environment where prospects seek you out: after a speech people walk up, start conversations, freely reveal business issues, and volunteer contact information. Speaking gigs are readily available from chambers, Rotary Clubs, trade organizations, civic groups, and trade show and association meeting planners looking for subject-matter experts.

The warning attached to this chapter: it is easy to spend all your time creating familiarity, and doing it in place of other prospecting activity produces an empty pipe a month later. Familiarity must be balanced against the need for sales today.

Social selling is called the most important technological advance for the sales profession since the telephone, and a critical component of a balanced methodology, but explicitly **not a panacea**. Contact and conversion rates from phone and e-mail dwarf conversion rates on social media. The channel enhances, elevates, and sometimes accelerates prospecting and strongly affects familiarity, but it does not replace focused outbound. **Social selling is not selling**: people don't want to be pitched on social channels, they prefer to connect, interact, and learn, so the channel suits familiarity building, lead nurturing, research, nuanced inbound prospecting, and trigger-event awareness. Channel choice follows two questions, in priority order: on which channels will I find my customers and prospects, and on which channels am I most comfortable. Three to four major channels is the practical ceiling, two is better, and for most salespeople LinkedIn is primary.

The **five objectives (outcomes) of social prospecting**: personal branding and building familiarity; inbound prospecting via education and insights; trigger-event and buying-cycle awareness; research and information gathering; and outbound prospecting via direct engagement (with strategic prospecting campaigns as a related outcome). Personal branding is governed by two constant questions: does my online presence support my reputation as a sales professional who solves problems and can be trusted, and does it help people become familiar with my name and brand in a positive way. Profile elements: a professional headshot (a genuine smile significantly affects perceptions of competence, likeability, and influence, per PhotoFeeler's study of over 60,000 ratings) used identically across all profiles like a logo; a professional cover image; a first-person, conversational summary explaining who you are, your values, what you do best, and why clients trust you to solve their problems; full contact information including phone, e-mail, and website (privacy is the wrong priority in sales, the best thing that can happen is a prospect interrupting you); cross-linked media and rich content; custom URLs; complete history with no gaps; and a quarterly review, tested against the question "Would you buy you?" Common mistakes: poorly written, incomplete, or outdated profiles, unprofessional or absent photos, strongly opinionated political or religious posting, and too much personal information. Familiarity is a two-edged sword, so think before you post.

**Trigger events** are disruptions in the status quo that open buying windows and compel buyers to act, some predictable (budgetary or contractual time frames) and some random (internal issues, or industry, economic, environmental, safety, employment, and market trends), including a past buyer moving to a new company. Monitor news streams, Twitter lists, Google+ circles, LinkedIn update alerts, and group discussions.

**Strategic prospecting campaigns (SPCs)** are long-term, comprehensive, multi-channel efforts to warm and nurture contacts ahead of a future buying window. Core objectives: decision-maker and influencer mapping; warming and nurturing the right contacts; identifying and developing relationships with potential coaches; creating personal and brand familiarity; generating goodwill and tapping the Law of Reciprocity by offering value first; and getting invited in when the window opens, or at least reducing friction at the initial appointment. Because they are time consuming and need ongoing attention, SPCs are limited to a handful of conquest opportunities.

The **five Cs of social selling**: **Connecting** (send a connection request every time you meet a potential customer, new contact, or valuable network addition, because acceptance probability is highest right after meeting and the request itself anchors familiarity; three routes are direct, reciprocal via follow-backs, and passive via published content), **Content creation** (original articles, videos, slide presentations, podcasts, infographics, white papers, case studies, e-books; powerful for trust and credibility but very difficult and resource-intensive), **Content curation** (aggregating and sharing others' relevant content, resting on three pillars: **awareness** of industry trends, competitors, movers and shakers, and thought leaders; **intent**, linking content to an overall strategy and adding comments and takeaways rather than sharing randomly; and **tools**), **Conversion** (actively planning for and working to generate leads and engagement that opens sales conversations, since social prospecting done right is a small personal inbound marketing machine), and **Consistency** (30 minutes to an hour a day, preferably outside the Golden Hours, limited strictly to the block, because social doesn't work if you show up only some of the time and the cumulative impact of short daily blocks is enormous).

Social prospecting tools fall into five categories: content curation, content creation, distribution, engagement, and intelligence. Building enough gravity on an established platform from zero can take six months to two years, which is why social and outbound are combined even by the granddaddy of inbound marketing and the grand pooh-bah of social selling. Combined benefits: amplified familiarity, more targeted lists, trigger-event timing, pre-window nurturing and education, contact information research, buyer-influencer-coach (BIC) mapping, qualifying, and a more relevant outbound message.

## 9. Message Matters: Bridges, the Because, and the Ask

There are no magic words and no perfect pitch. The prospecting message has one purpose: to quickly persuade the prospect to give you their time. Asking for a prospect's time is the most difficult request in the entire sales process, harder than asking for a buying decision. Prospects will not give up time for a features dump, an enthusiastic pitch about being number one, regurgitated generic facts and figures, marketing brochures, or irrelevant information. Prospects meet with you for their reasons, not yours, so the message must articulate the value of the meeting in the context of what matters to them and must demonstrate sincere interest in listening to and solving their unique problems.

**How you say it** matters alongside what you say. Nonverbal communication includes voice tone, inflection, pitch, and speed; body language and facial expressions; dress and outward appearance; and sentence structure, grammar, punctuation, and word choice in written channels. **Confidence** ("a feeling or belief that you can do something well or succeed at something") and **enthusiasm** ("a strong excitement about something; something inspiring zeal or fervor") are the two most powerful and persuasive nonverbal messages. People respond in kind: a relaxed, confident, enthusiastic demeanor transfers to the prospect. Because the physiology runs both directions, the outward posture can be used to generate the inward state, which is the basis of Amy Cuddy's power posing work on cortisol and testosterone, and of the practical devices (sit up straight, chin up, smile into the phone, mirror by the phone, assertive and assumptive word choice, saying "I'm awesome!" when asked how you are).

Three specifications for the message: quick, simple, direct, and above all **relevant**, because relevance lowers the perceived risk of giving up time. This is why a 15-minute discovery meeting is easier to get than an hour-long full demo. The question to answer is **WIIFM**, what's in it for me. Art Sobczak's term for the assumptions you make about a prospect's WIIFM is **Possible Value Propositions**, defined per class of prospect and decision-maker role. Jill Konrath defines a value proposition as "a clear statement of the tangible results a customer gets from using your products or services," outcome focused, stressing business value, with three key parts: it focuses on a business objective that is measured; it disrupts the status quo (people abhor change and only move when they believe they can significantly improve their situation); and it offers proof or evidence from similar situations. Mike Weinberg's **Power Statement** answers the prospect's issues, your offerings that address those issues, and competitive differentiators, built from the question "Why do my customers choose to do business with me?" because differentiation gets attention.

Blount's own preference is the simpler frame, the **power of because**. Ellen Langer's photocopier study: a researcher cutting in line with no reason got yes about 60 percent of the time; with a valid reason ("because I'm in a hurry") 94 percent; and with a nonsensical reason ("because I have to make copies") 93 percent. Saying the word *because*, giving a reason, was more important and more powerful than the reason itself. The practical conclusion is not to invent nonsense, but that a simple, straightforward because outperforms hours agonizing over a complex value proposition. "I'd like 15 minutes of your time because I want to learn more about you and your company" works surprisingly well.

**Bridging** is connecting your solution to the prospect's problem in their language, not yours; in prospecting, the bridge is the because. Two types:

- **Targeted bridges** — common to a large group of similar prospects (decision-maker role, industry vertical, product application). Correct when you have little information on a specific prospect and per-prospect research is not worth the cost, as with a database of thousands of SMBs. Problems are inferred from economic trends or knowledge of what similar businesses in the same industry, geography, market segment, or competitor's product are experiencing, and the message is iterated and refined through conversations.
- **Strategic bridges** — unique to a single high-value prospect and a specific decision-maker role, used for enterprise and conquest prospects and C-level executives. They require research, so they limit daily activity and must be built during the Platinum Hours. Process: determine the objective of the touch (more qualifying information, an initial meeting, an introduction), then research (Google alerts, CRM notes and history, company website, press releases, LinkedIn/Google+/Twitter/Facebook company pages, the contact's own social profiles reviewed for jargon, core values, PR, awards, trigger events, initiatives, changes, and solvable problems, plus industry trends and recent trade articles), then craft a message that relates to their specific situation using their language.

The secret to crafting powerful bridges is emotional vocabulary: frustration, anxiety, stress, fear, peace of mind. **People make decisions based on emotion first and then justify with logic**, which is why pitching logic and features fails. Prospects give up time for three kinds of value: **emotional value** (connecting with painful emotions like stress, worry, insecurity, distrust, anxiety, fear, frustration, or anger, and offering peace of mind, security, options, lower stress, less worry, or hope), **insight or curiosity value** (information that gives them power or leverage, addressing the anxiety that a competitor may have a best practice, system, or process they don't), and **tangible or logic value** (data and case studies, how much, how many, what results, for executives and technical, data-centric roles). The method is to stand in the prospect's shoes and answer a long inventory of questions from their perspective (what causes them stress, worry, anxiety, frustration, anger, distrust, fear; how it feels to lack time, money, resources, or knowledge; what would make them curious; what information they would fear reaching a competitor; what is stealing their time, money, or resources). Then answer the one question that prevents getting shut down: what would cause your prospect to say "So what?" to your message.

**The ask** is the most important element of any prospecting touch; without a direct request for action everything else is academic. Long, moronic scripts and passive hedging ("maybe if it would be okay and if you are not too busy we could kinda maybe get together for a few minutes, what do you think?") exist to avoid the rejection risk of asking directly. Nine times out of ten, difficulty getting appointments, decision makers, information, or the close traces to not asking, and nine times out of ten that is fear of hearing no. There are three steps to asking: **ask with confidence and assume you will get what you want**; **shut up**; and **be prepared to deal with reflex responses, brush-offs, and objections**.

Across thousands of tracked calls, confident, assertive requests get yes about 70 percent of the time, while nonassertive requests succeed about 30 percent of the time, and pairing an assertive request with a because raises it further. Jeffrey Gitomer: "the assumptive position is the strongest selling strategy in the world." The passive/assumptive contrast set: "Is this a good time?" versus "The reason I'm calling is ..."; "I was wondering if ...?" versus "Tell me who, how, when, where, what ..."; "I have the whole day open" versus "I'm super busy bringing on new clients, but I do have a slot available at 11:00 AM"; "What do you think?" versus "Why don't we go ahead and get that set up?"; "What's the best time for you?" versus "How about we meet at 2:00 PM?" In written channels, direct assumptive words and sentence structure are the body language of the written word.

After the ask, silence must be managed, because the rush to protect yourself produces a train wreck: overcoming objections that have not surfaced, over-explaining, offering the prospect a way out, and talking a prospect who was ready to say yes into saying no. The governing structure is the **rule of thirds**: about a third of the time you get to yes fast, simply because you asked, and the goal is to bank those yeses without talking yourself out of them; about a third of the time you get to no fast, which is a blessing because it frees you to move on; about a third of the time you get maybe, hesitation, negotiation, or a false objection, and that is where prospecting is won or lost through effective RBO turnarounds.

## 10. Telephone Prospecting Excellence and the Voice Mail Framework

The telephone is, has always been, and will continue to be the most powerful sales prospecting tool. No other tool delivers better results, fills the pipe faster, or covers more ground in less time. The myth that nobody answers the phone anymore is contradicted by measured contact rates of 15 to 80 percent depending on industry, product, and contact role level, with business services consistently between 25 and 40 percent, and by a single boot-camp day in which 19 insurance agents made 1,311 outbound dials at a 51 percent contact rate. Data going back to the early 1990s shows contact rates via phone have actually *risen* by about 5 percentage points, with three suspected drivers: phones are anchored to people rather than desks (mobile lines, office lines rolling to mobile); no one is calling, so the salesperson who does call stands out; and prospects are burned out on impersonal, irrelevant, often automated prospecting e-mails and are hungry for a live, authentic human being.

The efficiency argument settles the "I'm better in person" objection: on the busiest city street, 20 face-to-face qualifying or appointment-setting contacts in eight hours would be a stretch and 10 is more realistic, dropping further in bad weather, while one hour on the phone with a targeted list at one to two minutes per call yields 25 to 50 calls. The telephone is also more effective than e-mail, social, and text, because speaking to a human raises the probability of setting appointments, selling, and gathering qualifying information.

Four reasons salespeople find the phone awkward: they don't know what to say, say stupid things, or read cheesy scripts that generate resistance; they have no easy-to-execute process that works; they don't know how to handle reflex responses, brush-offs, and objections; and they are afraid of rejection. Four things to stipulate before starting: you will be rejected a lot, because the phone generates more real-time interactions than any other channel; most calls go to voice mail, with connect rates between 20 and 50 percent, which is exactly why you must be effective when you do connect; most frustration comes from overcomplicating a simple, straightforward process; and nobody really likes telephone prospecting, which does not change the fact that peak performance requires mastering it.

The ultimate key is the **scheduled phone block**: one to two hours daily, all distractions removed, e-mail and mobile devices off, others informed not to disturb, clear goals for number of calls, treated as a sacred booked appointment. Blocks can be broken into manageable chunks with their own goals (10 dials rather than 100, 30 minutes rather than two hours), with a small reward at each chunk, or tracked visually (counting down 50 to 1 with a strike through each number), or gamified by counting nos. Timing calls is a losing strategy and the greatest excuse and cop-out of salespeople who don't want to prospect; the investing analogy applies, in that the investor who times the market historically loses to the one who dollar-cost-averages on a regular schedule, and the salesperson who prospects daily on a regular schedule always beats the one who tries to time it. The cited call-timing study found the best day only 1.3 percent better than the worst day. The prescription is Brian Tracy's **eat that frog**: block the first two hours of every day for telephone activity, because your energy, confidence, and enthusiasm peak at the beginning of the day, and prospects have fewer things on their plate then, which means less resistance and more yeses.

**The Five-Step Simple Telephone Prospecting Framework**, executed with no pauses, because the moment you pause you lose control of the call:

1. **Get their attention by using their name** — "Hi, Julie." A person's name is the fastest way to get attention. Explicitly do *not* ask "How are you doing?" and do not pause, because that is the exact moment the prospect realizes you are a salesperson, their get-away-from-this-salesperson-fast mechanism fires, and you get hit with a reflex response.
2. **Identify yourself** — "My name is Jeb Blount and I'm with Sales Gravy." Transparency has two benefits: it demonstrates professionalism and respect for the prospect's time, and it reduces their stress, because people are more comfortable when they know what to expect.
3. **Tell them why you are calling** — "The reason I'm calling is to set up an appointment with you."
4. **Bridge, give them a because** — a short, compelling message connecting emotionally to what matters to them. Avoid "I want to talk to you about my product," "I'd love to get together to show you what we have to offer," and "I want to tell you about our new service," because *talk*, *tell*, and *show* signal a pitch. Use prospect-centered phrasing: learn more about you and your business, share insights that have helped my other clients, share best practices other companies in your industry are using, gain an understanding of your unique situation, see how we might fit, plus emotional words like flexibility, options, peace of mind, save, frustrated, concerned, stressed, waste, time, money.
5. **Ask for what you want, and shut up** — for qualifying, ask for the information you need to determine the next step; for an appointment, ask for a specific day and time; for a sales conversation, ask an open-ended question that gets them talking.

A framework, unlike a script, is a guide that makes you agile and adaptive across situations and frees attention for the message rather than the process.

**Voice mail.** Leaving voice mail is inefficient (20 to 30 seconds each, easily 10 to 15 minutes of an hour block) and the callback rate is single digits, so it cannot be applied across the board. The rule is to leave voice mail "when it matters": not on lists of largely unqualified prospects, but on every call into a conquest list, and on any prospect known or suspected to be moving into the buying window. An effective voice mail should achieve at least one of two objectives: get a callback from a high-value qualified prospect, or build familiarity with a high-value prospect. Three kinds of message guarantee deletion: no contact information, long-winded, and garbled contact information.

**The Five-Step Voice Mail Framework**, which deployed consistently doubles callback rates:

1. **Identify yourself** — name and company up front, which sounds professional.
2. **Say your phone number twice**, slowly, up front, so that a prospect who can infer the subject from your name and company does not have to listen to the rest to get the number.
3. **Tell them the reason for your call** — "The reason for my call is ..." or "the purpose of my call is ...". Transparency is respectful and professional.
4. **Give them a reason to call you back** — curiosity is a powerful driver of behavior, so lead with knowledge, insight, information, special pricing, new or improved products, or a solution to a problem.
5. **Repeat your name and say your phone number twice** again, slowly and clearly, so an intrigued prospect never has to replay the message.

Bonus rule: keep voice mails to 30 seconds, which forces clarity, succinctness, and professionalism. Saying the number four times in one message feels awkward; the goal is to make it easy and pleasurable for the prospect to call back, not comfortable for you.

## 11. Turning Around RBOs and Getting Past Gatekeepers

The feeling of rejection is real and it is personal, and the conventional trainer clichés ("let rejection roll off your back," "they're not rejecting you, just your proposition," "don't take it personal") are rejected as disingenuous platitudes. The brain does not distinguish between rejection of the proposition and rejection of you; the physiological reaction runs through the amygdala's fight-or-flight mechanism, which cannot tell the difference between a bear on the path and a prospect saying no, and the psychological sting comes from an innate need to feel accepted, important, and in control. Per Brene Brown, vulnerability is created in the presence of uncertainty, risk, and emotional exposure, which is exactly the condition of a prospecting call. The goal is not to stop feeling rejection but to build a framework that gains control of the disruptive emotion before the call becomes a train wreck.

Three distinct things get lumped together as objections:

- **Reflex response** — an automatic, conditioned buyer script triggered by being interrupted by a salesperson, often untrue, with no conscious intent to deceive: "We're not interested," "We're happy," "We're all set," "I'm busy," "I'm in a meeting," "I'm just running out the door."
- **Brush-off** — conflict avoidance, letting you down easy: "Call me later," "Get back to me in a month," "Why don't you just send over some information?" (called the greatest brush-off of all time). Per Seth Godin, prospects lie because salespeople have trained them to and because they're afraid: when they tell the truth, the salesperson questions their judgment, so the prospect is disrespected in exchange for honesty, and avoiding the conflict altogether is easier.
- **Objection** — a more truthful, logical rebuttal that typically comes with a because: a newly signed competitor contract, a huge project already under way, a conflicting trip, locked-down budgets. These are rare, and when they come they open the door to turning the objection around or shifting to gathering qualifying information for the future.

RBOs are not unique events, and the secret is planning for them in advance rather than winging every call. There are a finite number of ways a prospect says no, and most fall into six categories: not interested, don't have the budget, too busy, send information, overwhelmed with too many things going on, and just looking (inbound leads). Prospects won't use those exact words ("We've been with your competitor for years and they do a good job for us" is "we're happy"), so look for the pattern and categorize. Then write simple, repeatable **turnaround scripts** for each. Scripts are defended on the grounds that a practiced script makes intonation, speaking style, and flow sound relaxed, authentic, and professional, and frees the mind to focus on the prospect rather than the words. The politician analogy: compelling on teleprompter, stumbling when winging it. The concern about sounding canned is legitimate, which is exactly why actors, politicians, and top sales professionals rehearse until the script becomes their own voice, using a recorder, a role-play buddy, or a coach.

The traditional instruction to "overcome objections" is rejected on definitional grounds: *overcome* means to defeat or prevail over an opponent, and there is a universal law of human behavior that you cannot argue another person into believing they are wrong, because the more you push the more they dig in. Even when this produces a yes it is in spite of the argument, not because of it, and it creates animosity and harsher rejection. The alternative is to **disrupt versus defeat**: deliver a statement or question that violates the prospect's expectation so they lean toward you rather than away. Disrupters work because humans stop and pay attention when they encounter something unexpected; it is a pull process rather than a push process. The martial-arts frame is judo, "gentle or yielding way," winning without causing injury, executed as verbal judo at 100 miles an hour.

**The Three-Step RBO Turnaround Framework: Anchor. Disrupt. Ask.**

- **Anchor** — also called a ledge. A short statement that gives your logical brain (neocortex) the millisecond it needs to catch up with the amygdala and tell it there is no real threat, which is how you regain poise and control of the conversation.
- **Disrupt** — a statement or question that takes away the expected fight. When they say they're happy: "Awesome. If you're happy, you shouldn't even think about changing!" When they say they're busy: "I figured you would be." When they say "just send me some information": "Tell me specifically what you are looking for," which calls the bluff and forces engagement. When they say "I'm not interested": "That makes sense. Most people aren't." Avoid words only salespeople use, above all "I understand," which reads as insincere filler, demonstrates zero empathy, and tells the prospect you are not listening.
- **Ask** — a confident, assumptive request for a specific commitment of time or information, with no hesitation and no awkward pause, immediately following the turnaround. About half the time this produces a second RBO, usually closer to the truth, which you should be prepared to handle and then ask again. Never fight. After two RBOs without a turnaround, graciously move on and come back another day.

Worked turnarounds: "Look, Jeb, I'm busy." → "Nancy, that's exactly why I called." (anchor) "I figured you would be, so I want to find a time that is more convenient for you." (disrupt) "How about we get together next Wednesday at 3:00 PM instead?" (ask). "We're not interested." → "You know, that is what a lot of my current clients said the first time I called." (anchor) "Most people say they aren't interested before they see how much I can save them. I don't know if my service will be a good fit for you and your company, but doesn't it make sense for us to at least get together for a short meeting to find out?" (disrupt) "How about Friday at 2:00 PM?" (ask).

When a prospect is genuinely abusive, the rule is **when the horse is dead, dismount**. The salesperson replays the call, projects a story onto the prospect, and logs a note never to call again, while the prospect has moved on and does not remember the interaction; Blount reports being screamed at on Tuesday and treated like a best friend on Wednesday, which is why he calls prospects who tell him never to call again. Courage is developed in the presence of fear, not in spite of it, and is a muscle that strengthens with exercise. Anger is energy, and one enduring quality of highly successful people is turning disappointment, defeat, and anger into determination, since achievement is the ultimate revenge. Blount's trigger is a 25-year-old index card taped behind his desk with four letters: **NEXT**.

**Gatekeepers.** There are no secret techniques, only strategies, and they rest on recognizing that gatekeepers are people with emotions, worries, motivations, a boss, and a job to do, whose most important job is protecting their executive's time. Success depends on a combination of good manners, likeability, and people savvy. **Seven keys for dealing with gatekeepers**: (1) **be likable**, positive, cheerful, polite, and respectful, because rude and pushy guarantees failure; (2) **use please, please**, per Mike Brooks, the single most powerful technique, using *please* twice in the request; (3) **be transparent**, giving full name and company, because full disclosure sounds professional and worthy of being passed through; (4) **connect**, asking how they are doing, listening to tone of voice, asking about family and interests, since some gatekeepers become better relationships than the boss; (5) **hold the cheese**, never use tricks or schemes, because they harm credibility and land you on the do-not-talk-to list, whereas honesty is remembered and opens gates later; (6) **ask for help**, an honest, authentic plea, sometimes with humor; and (7) **change the game** by sidestepping, calling early or late (the boss is in before the gatekeeper and stays later), leveraging social inboxes such as LinkedIn InMail that gatekeepers rarely have access to, meeting the prospect in person at conferences, networking events, civic clubs, charity events, and trade shows, sending an e-mail, or sending a handwritten note by mail.

Three named hacks for when a lower-level blocker will not give up the decision maker's name and online searches fail. **The calling-other-extensions hack**: dial random extensions until a friendly employee supplies a partial clue (a first name, a department), then use that clue at reception to trade up to a full name and extension. **The salespeople-help-salespeople hack**: press the option for the sales department and honestly ask a fellow salesperson for a hand, which works because sales organizations answer their phones, salespeople know who's who and how to reach them, and salespeople help other salespeople because they have stood in your shoes; in the worked example it produced the VP of Sales' mobile number plus intelligence on why the company was growing and what was wrong with their current training. **The go-around-back hack**: when prospecting in person and reception refuses, approach people loading, unloading, on break, or walking to their cars, transparently and nonaggressively, and skip it entirely where security guards or other access-control measures are present. In sales, persistence always wins, and the more valuable the business opportunity the more likely the prospect is walled in by gatekeepers.

## 12. In-Person, E-Mail, and Text Message Prospecting

**In-person prospecting (IPP)** is the least efficient of all channels, because driving around knocking on doors consumes enormous time, and done randomly it burns a day for very few calls. It therefore supplements and complements other channels rather than replacing them, and except for T-Calls and driving past a genuinely new business, every IPP should be planned in advance. It works best for residential and B2B reps in a local territory selling transactional to semi-complex products to small and medium-sized businesses where walking in is possible without hitting a security wall; at large plants it is useful mainly for information gathering by pumping security guards and gatekeepers about decision makers and competitors. Three bad reasons reps make it their primary channel: a false belief that aimless driving is working, managers who believe the only good salesperson is one they cannot see, and fear or inability to use the phone justified as "I'm just better in person." The disarming comparison: 10 to 20 IPPs on a hot or freezing day versus 25 to 50 teleprospecting calls in one hour off a printed list.

The objectives of IPP: **qualifying** (people often give more information in person, and you can look around to see where your product helps and gather competitor insight), **making appointments**, **sales conversations** (be prepared to close), **building familiarity** (putting a face with a name for both gatekeepers and decision makers), **maximizing the sales day** (reducing windshield time), and **learning your territory**. The primary objective is gathering qualifying information to feed the database and future phone, e-mail, and social lists.

**The Five-Step Hub-and-Spoke System for IPPs**: (1) plan IPPs around preset appointments generated in your phone block; (2) use the CRM, typically a zip code search, to develop a list of nearby prospects; (3) plot three to five prospects on a map around each preset appointment; (4) develop the most efficient driving route with the least windshield time; (5) leave time before or after each appointment to call on those prospects face to face, and don't stop until you reach your goal. This yields 15 to 20 in-person touches a day. The related technique is the **T-Call**: at every planned stop, look right, left, and behind for other opportunities, newly opened businesses, or businesses not in the database, and walk into those doors as well. The reported conversion in the illustrative case is roughly 30 percent of IPPs into future appointments, information on most of the rest, and one or two decision makers a week ready to begin a sales conversation on the spot. IPPs also route around gatekeepers who block on the phone, because it is harder to turn someone away in person.

**Five steps to planning effective IPPs**: (1) **research** in advance for planned calls (decision-maker names, business history, website, recent press releases, CRM notes) and a quick smartphone scan of website and social media for T-Calls; (2) **personalize your approach** with relevant questions, compliments on recent accomplishments, insights, or leverage of nearby customer relationships for instant credibility; (3) **develop an objective for every call**; (4) **be prepared to close**, carrying sale sheets, order forms, contracts, and presentation material, because sometimes you get only one chance (the cautionary case: an owner with every buying signal short of a neon sign, lost because the rep had no presentation material and the owner signed with a competitor two weeks later); (5) **log calls, notes, and follow-up tasks in the CRM** before the close of each day, or on the spot.

**The Five-Step In-Person Prospecting Call Process** mirrors the telephone framework but runs slower with more dialogue: (1) **approach with confidence**, on two keys, expect to win (walk in like you own the place and ask direct questions) and plan questions in advance; (2) **identify yourself and say why you are there**, straightforwardly and transparently, never with lines designed to trick gatekeepers; (3) **gather information** through conversation rather than interrogation, since 80 percent of human communication is visual and IPPs engage all the senses, using open-ended questions, listening, and resisting the temptation to pitch (per Kelly Robertson, effective salespeople direct all attention to the prospect's situation and resist the opportunity to talk about their own company or offering); (4) **ask for what you want**, decided before walking in, with readiness to bridge to closing if the opportunity presents; (5) **turn around objections** with turnarounds prepared in advance.

The supporting habit is putting on your **sales goggles**: training yourself to be acutely aware of surrounding opportunity, looking left, right, and behind every time you enter or leave an appointment, staying alert while driving for new businesses and new construction, reading business names off delivery trucks and signs and quizzing parked drivers, talking to anyone in a logoed shirt or uniform about their company and its decision makers, talking to people in lines, waiting rooms, trains, buses, and planes (reported at more than half a million dollars in business over five years), collecting matching business cards off gas station and restaurant bulletin boards, and using a smartphone for voice memos and photographs of signs, new locations, and truck livery when there is no time to stop. Awareness without action is useless.

**E-mail prospecting** generates 10 to 20 times more engagement and response than social prospecting, and it extends the prospecting window, since e-mails can be written outside the Golden Hours and scheduled to send during prime selling time. Intelligence tools make response rates testable and measurable, which is how a message gets honed for a vertical or a group of similar prospects. E-mail includes the social inboxes (LinkedIn, Facebook Messenger, Twitter direct messages), which add the benefit of skipping gatekeepers and the spam box and of reaching prospects whose e-mail address you don't have.

**The Three Cardinal Rules of E-Mail Prospecting**:

- **Rule #1: your e-mail must get delivered.** Don't send bulk e-mail, because prospecting e-mail is one to one, one e-mail from your address to one individual at a time, which alone clears about 90 percent of spam hurdles. Avoid attached images, because embedded malware causes filters to block or flag them. Avoid hyperlinks, and if one is included, don't embed the URL in text, include the entire URL for transparency, avoid shortened URLs that obscure the address, and limit the total to one including the signature. Avoid attachments. Skip spammy words and phrases, including ALL CAPS subject lines, multiple exclamation points, and the roughly 200 trigger words Kevin Gao lists (*amazing*, *free access*, *cash*, *don't delete*, *do it today*, *increase sales*, *100 percent guaranteed*, *save $*). Don't send to many people in the same company at one time; drip them across the day. Don't send too many e-mails to the same person, because being marked as spam can blacklist you across an entire enterprise. Scrub bounces, treating each as an opportunity to gather better information: remove the address from the CRM, check LinkedIn or Google to see whether the contact is still there, and either remove, update, or hunt for the correct address. Use extra caution with sensitive industries such as financial institutions, defense contractors, and health care, where text only with no links, attachments, or images is recommended.
- **Rule #2: your e-mail must get opened.** The average business executive receives 200-plus e-mails a day and copes by scanning and triaging in split-second open/delete/save decisions. Familiarity is the first lever: layering channels, for example a voice mail, a LinkedIn ping, then an e-mail, is a "triple threat" that compounds recognition, and meeting in person then connecting then leaving a voice mail then e-mailing improves open probability exponentially. The subject line is the second lever, and the three most common mistakes are: **too long** (shorter outperforms longer by wide margins, 50 percent or more of e-mail is opened on mobile, and past 50 characters open rates fall exponentially; the fix is three to six words or 40 to 50 characters including spaces), **questions** (virtually every major study finds question-form subject lines doom the e-mail; the fix is action words and directive statements, with list-based testimonial lines such as "3 Reasons Why ABC Chose Us," referral lines such as "Jeb Blount Said We Should Talk," and statement lines such as "Biggest Fail in Industrial Pumps" being especially powerful), and **impersonal or boring** (the fix is to connect the subject line to an issue the prospect faces, especially an emotional or stressful one, or to compliment a recent accomplishment, with relevant humor where appropriate). There is no universal formula, so experimentation and testing are the real secret, and most salespeople never test.
- **Rule #3: your e-mail must convert**, meaning generate the desired outcome: an appointment, qualifying information, an introduction to a decision maker, a forward to other influencers, a document download, video view, or webinar registration, or a sales conversation. Conquest prospects, C-level contacts, and strategic campaigns require personalized messages; within verticals, markets, and decision-maker roles there is enough common ground to build mass-customizable templates, but even those require research so the e-mail feels unique, because it falls on deaf ears if the recipient doesn't feel it was written specifically for them.

The planning framework is **AMMO**: **Audience** (their role, style, how and when they consume information, how familiar they are with you, which sets tone, structure, and formality), **Method** (from a single standalone e-mail to a multi-message strategic prospecting campaign; short and sweet or detailed, hard hitting or soft, direct, standalone or part of an SPC, nurturing or action oriented, cross-platform), **Message** (what will get their attention, what's important to them, what will cause them to give you what you ask for), and **Outcome** (define exactly what you are asking them to do or provide).

The writing framework has **four elements**: (1) **Hook** — roughly three seconds to earn the open with the subject line and hold with the first sentence, what Kendra Lee calls the "glimpse factor"; make the subject line relevant and the opening sentence about them, and never open with "Hi," "Hello," or "Dear," because no one in business does that except salespeople. (2) **Relate** — demonstrate that you get them and their problem, with empathy and authenticity; where you cannot honestly claim to have been in their role, relate through what others in that role tell you. (3) **Bridge** — connect their problem to how you can help and answer WIIFM, bridging to a specific researched issue where known and to issues common to the role, situation, or industry where not, and speaking their language (for a bank COO: growth, profit, risk, allocate resources, minimize surprises). (4) **Ask** — be clear and straightforward and make acting easy; the model ask disrupts expectations by conceding up front that you might not be a fit, uses the word *learn* rather than *show* or *tell*, references "your unique challenges," lowers risk by keeping the call short and non-pushy, and then assumptively proposes a specific day and time rather than putting the calendar burden on the prospect. The worst e-mails are long jargon-laden pitches, feature-focused product dumps, cheerleading about an "amazing" company, ones that get the name wrong, and ones long enough to glaze the eyes. Best send time is when the prospect is most likely to open and act, for most B2B first thing in the morning to midmorning, testable with intelligence tools. Finally, proofread: read it once, read it twice, step away for 10 minutes and read again, and print the important ones.

**Text message prospecting** is the third rail of sales prospecting, approached lightly. The irony that makes it powerful is that text is impersonal as a channel, lacking the emotional connectivity of face-to-face and verbal communication, yet it *feels* extremely personal, because it is the medium for family, friends, and coworkers and is normally untouched by spam. Per the Lead360 study, the same properties that make text effective also give it the potential to be interpreted as intrusive or in violation of one's personal domain when used for business. Adoption is accelerating because of VoIP hubs that route calls to mobile devices, integrate with the CRM, and send and receive texts from desktop and mobile apps, plus enterprise app-based phone systems and bring-your-own-device programs.

**Familiarity is everything with text**, more than with any other channel, because we rarely text strangers. Per the Lead360 study of 3.5 million lead records from more than 400 companies, a text sent alone converts at 4.8 percent, while the same text sent after a phone contact increases conversion by 112.6 percent. Impact amplifies further when the text follows an e-mail, a social interaction, or a positive in-person networking encounter. Texting a hard-to-reach prospect when all other means are exhausted and the buying window is closing is a defensible gambit but a low-probability play. Four sanctioned use cases:

- **Anchoring conversations after networking events.** (1) During the conversation, when the vague future agreement is made, casually say "Sounds good. I'll text you and we can get together." (2) As soon as you walk away, send a personalized LinkedIn connection request from your phone. (3) Within 24 hours of the event, or two days if travel is involved, text thanks for the conversation, request a meeting, and personalize with something from the conversation. (4) If there is no response, send again a day later, since many prospects won't recognize the number on the first attempt. (5) If the second attempt fails, shift to phone and e-mail, because continuing to text only risks ill will. Bonus step: always send a handwritten note by mail within a week.
- **Following trigger events**, which work with text because trigger events create urgency and text is perceived as urgent. A soft approach works best: text a link to a relevant article or resource and become a resource, which usually converts into a phone call, or where nothing relevant exists and the relationship is strong, reference the trigger event and ask how things are going.
- **Nurturing prospects** who are qualified but not in the buying window, using text as the channel reserved for the best value-added information, personal and nonintrusive, which keeps you top of mind across a quarterly call, monthly e-mail, social, and text cadence.
- **Creating opportunities for engagement** by making the prospect feel important: congratulations on a promotion, a mention in the news, an award, or praise for an article, video, or post, with no direct solicitation attached, ideally paired with liking, commenting on, or sharing the post.

**Seven rules for structuring effective text prospecting messages**: (1) **identify yourself**, name and company at the top, never assuming your number is saved; (2) **message matters**, using complete sentences so the tone is not misread as abrupt, harsh, sarcastic, or flippant; (3) **be direct, be brief**, one to four short sentences or fewer than 250 characters, no rambling, no emoticons; (4) **avoid abbreviations**, acronyms, and slang; (5) **use transparent links**, the entire URL, never shortened; (6) **pause and read it again before clicking send**; (7) **know your numbers**, tracking texts sent, response rates, and conversions into appointments and sales. And do not text while driving.
