# Sales Management. Simplified. — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing sales management/hiring/team-building processes, or advising on sales team strategy.*
*Book: "Sales Management. Simplified." — Mike Weinberg*

---

## 1. The Leadership Premise and the Sales Manager's Real Job

The book is built on two borrowed catchphrases that Weinberg treats as the governing laws of sales performance: **"As goes the leader, so goes the organization"** and **"The level of the team rarely, if ever, exceeds the level of the leader."** The practical consequence is that spend on salesperson training is mostly wasted if the leader's acumen isn't raised first. Weinberg spent four years coaching individual producers before concluding that organizations don't change from the bottom: improving the skills, techniques, and attitudes of salespeople does not transform a sales organization, because the root causes of underperformance are usually cultural and flow down from the top.

The frontline sales management role is framed as one of the hardest jobs in business, and for a specific structural reason: everyone in the company wants a piece of the sales manager's time, and none of them understands the job. The diagnosis Weinberg's father gave him — the origin story of the whole book — is that there is no way to win without a solid grounding in absolute priorities and a laser focus on what drives the business, because **if you let the people placing demands on you dictate how you spend your time, you will fail.**

Two claims recur as the spine of everything that follows. First, sales is about results, full stop: salespeople are not paid by the hour and are not measured by the quantity of work they do, so **the yardstick is always actual results versus goal.** Second, the manager's job is not to manage the sales department but to drive sales, which means **there is no extra credit for dividing your time equally across all of your people.** Across roughly 150 sales organizations, Weinberg reports never once finding a team that missed its number because it lacked some new sales tool or process; teams underperform because leaders ignore or botch the fundamentals of sales management.

## 2. A Results-Focused Culture: Goals, Reports, and Keeping Score

Healthy sales organizations have always published and widely distributed sales reports: sales versus last year, sales versus quota for the month, sales versus goal year-to-date, stack rank by percentage over goal, number of new accounts acquired year-to-date versus goal, percent of annual goal achieved. Weinberg's argument is that this practice was never abusive or archaic — it is simply the mechanism by which a results-focused culture exists at all, and it works without raised voices, profanity, or threats.

He traces the abandonment of scorekeeping to a specific event: the 2008–2009 collapse, when many industrial, manufacturing, and marketing businesses were off 40 to 50 percent or more (about a third of the ad agencies his employer served folded). To protect talent through the downturn, companies shifted pay plans toward a higher percentage of fixed compensation and stopped publishing depressing sales reports. The failure is that **many never restored goal-setting and accountability once the market rebounded**, and they now want the results while remaining slow to rebuild visibility.

Two diagnostic traps in this area:

- **Activity worship** — executives, especially controlling founders in smaller companies, whose report of choice is the call report rather than the monthly or quarterly sales report. Judging producers on activity rather than production is self-defeating, and it is micromanagement by another name. A-players expect to be held accountable for exceeding goals and will not tolerate being micromanaged.
- **Goals that don't exist at the individual level** — the opening consulting story is a VP of sales whose team had no individual goals for new account acquisition and no reports tracking progress against them, in a company whose entire growth came from penetrating existing accounts.

The diagnostic questions Weinberg prescribes: are the goals of the team and each member clear; does every member understand what is expected and how they'll be evaluated; what processes monitor progress against specific goals; how is a results-focused culture actually being created.

## 3. What Keeps Sales Managers From Leading: Diversion, Desk Jockeying, and the Time Draculas

The dirty secret of Weinberg's practice is that in almost every underperforming sales team, the people charged with selling new business spend a surprisingly low percentage of their time selling new business. **Little Effort = Little Results.** They are not lazy; they are working on the wrong things — playing corporate ambassador, safety committee member, customer service advocate, delivery boy. The critical asymmetry: salespeople usually *choose* to be distracted from their primary job, while sales managers are typically *kept* from theirs by companies that bury them in work unrelated to leading the team or driving revenue.

**The five biggest time drains ("Time Draculas") that keep managers from leading:**

1. Slaves to email, perpetually checking and replying, living in reactive mode.
2. Sitting in on a ridiculous number of meetings and conference calls with little to do with driving revenue (death by meeting).
3. Playing either Assistant GM or Firefighter-in-Chief.
4. Buried in administrative and non-sales work, asked to create or plow through an obscene number of reports, head constantly in CRM screens.
5. Not owning their calendars, not protecting priorities, not planning well.

The composite case study: a director of sales with 22 direct reports (15 regional salespeople, 7 support), no administrative help, mandatory attendance at five-plus non-sales meetings, and over 200 emails per day — who conducted zero fieldwork, zero 1:1 results reviews, zero pipeline reviews, and zero team calls. He could have worked 60 hours a week in fully reactive mode and still not cleared his plate.

**The CRM and email trap.** A CRM will not fix a broken sales organization ("just like having kids will fix a bad marriage") — it exposes weaknesses and adds work. The specific dysfunction is that CRM adoption changes how managers see the job: they become obsessed with compliance and, without ever saying the words, communicate that entering tasks and opportunities promptly matters more than advancing and closing deals. Weinberg has seen salespeople consciously choose administrative tasks over selling because the consequences for not updating the CRM were harsher than for missing sales goals. The diagnostic question: **is your CRM working for you, or are you working for it?** The parallel sin is managing people-who-manage-relationships by email — one-way messages demanding status updates and scolding overdue tasks, from managers who spend almost no time face-to-face with their people. A CRM screen does not let you see, feel, or hear how your people do their most important job.

## 4. Role Confusion and Ego: How Managers and Executives Sabotage Their Own Teams

Five distinct structural and behavioral failures, each with its own signature.

**The player-coach selling manager** — the part-time manager who also carries a personal quota. Weinberg says he has never seen it work, because the two roles are opposites. The successful individual producer wins by being **selfishly productive**: blocking out the world, controlling her calendar, refusing to play good corporate citizen. The successful manager wins *through* her people and must be accessible, lead meetings, develop talent, remove obstacles, coach, and put team members' needs ahead of her own. The two cannot be gracefully alternated. Worse, the model creates internal competition — the manager cherry-picking the best leads and opportunities — which destroys trust across the team. Ditka's formulation: "Players play and coaches coach."

**The hero complex** — adapted from Liz Wiseman's *Multipliers*. Multipliers make everyone around them better, smarter, and more productive; Diminishers arrive with all the answers, tell people both what to do and how to do it, and drain energy and intelligence from the team. Wiseman's question ("are you a genius or a genius-maker?") becomes: **are you the hero or the hero-maker?** Symptoms: grabbing the yoke on sales calls, inserting yourself into a hot deal just before it closes so your fingerprints are on the paperwork, finding "flaws" in perfectly fine proposals, and claiming credit in front of the executive committee. The best managers subdue their own egos, deflect credit rather than steal it, look for coaching opportunities rather than takeover opportunities, and step in front of the bus for their people.

**The fire chief's helmet** — the sales leader whose competitive nature and problem-solving talent pull her into manufacturing, quality, logistics, or workflow problems. These are often things she's genuinely good at, which is exactly why the opportunity cost goes unnoticed. The related pattern is the manager who becomes "The Shell Answer Man," the fount of all knowledge at the crossroads of all sales team communication.

**Sales and operations under one head** — most common in companies or divisions under about $50 million. The general manager reliably defaults to operational issues, because those are urgent and in front of him while sales problems surface only at month- or quarter-end. That bias then permeates the sales force: account managers scheduling service crews, inside salespeople picking warehouse orders and managing international shipping logistics. Weinberg's rule, inherited from Donnie Williams: **"Don't ever confuse sales and operations"** — the sales car has one pedal, the skinny one on the right, and the job is to keep the RPMs in the power band, not to have mercy on operations.

**The big-ego "sales expert" executive and the charismatic visionary** — two failure modes at the top. The first talks through 90 percent of sales calls (about himself), pontificates in team meetings, quizzes salespeople publicly to embarrass them, and demands call reports from four layers down: **Embarrassing + Pontificating + Micromanaging = Disengaged Team.** The second is subtler and arises from strength: the entrepreneur who sells vision on force of personality and cannot comprehend why his team can't do the same. The corrective principle is **"sales follows strategy"** — clarity from the top is a non-negotiable prerequisite for a new business attack. The job of the sales force is to execute the company's strategy, not to invent it on the fly. If leadership cannot say which markets to pursue and why, exactly what the team is selling, and specifically to whom, it is a strategy problem, not a sales problem.

## 5. Underperformance, Compensation, and the Anti-Sales Culture

**Tolerating the perennial underperformer.** Talk about accountability without follow-through does more damage than silence. Weinberg's test is a simple analogy chain: how many months would you tolerate inaccurate financials from your controller, mismarked shipments from your shipping clerk, or late delivery from a project manager? So why is monthly and quarterly sales failure survivable? Two specific excuses he rejects: long/complex sales cycles (a reason to change the measurement, not to abandon it), and 100-percent-commission models ("they're not producing and they're not costing me anything"). Keeping low producers costs the culture, pollutes the office, signals that underproduction is tolerated, occupies desks and support resources, and carries real opportunity cost.

**COMPensation and COMPlacency start with the same four letters.** If you have complacent salespeople, start the analysis with the comp plan. Three failure patterns:

- **Flat plans** — too little spread between top and bottom earners. The mechanism is symmetrical and destructive: underperformers are overpaid for what they produce (giving them comfort and a safety net to stay), while top producers are effectively cheated and start asking what a competitor would pay them. A flat plan accomplishes the exact opposite of what a smart plan should.
- **Treating all sales dollars as equal** — on a financial statement a dollar is a dollar; in compensation it is not. A new dollar of sales to a newly acquired customer is both more valuable for growth and a better measure of proactive selling effectiveness than a recaptured existing dollar. The prescription is therefore explicit: **pay higher commission rates for new sales and lower rates for repeat sales.** If babysitting existing accounts pays the same as the heavy lifting of acquisition, the salesperson will over-service the base.
- **Commission annuities** — commission works as a behavioral tool only if it must be earned and if it is genuinely variable. Payments that arrive like clockwork whether or not anything new is sold are commission in name only and might as well be base salary. They produce "cruise control sellers" — including the nominal number-one producer who turns out to be caretaking the number-one territory and not even working his existing customers.

**The anti-sales culture.** Weinberg's asymmetry argument: **a miserable accountant can still do great work; a miserable salesperson cannot.** An accountant can hate the job and still close the books accurately. Selling requires wanting to sell — a disengaged seller won't fight to get in, won't ask the hard questions, won't push past resistance or pursue a deal that has gone dark. There are no miserable top performers in sales, because when the culture gets bad enough top producers leave, and there are no unemployed A-players to replace them. The most corrosive specific mechanism he names is **arbitrary commission deductions by an over-empowered accountant or controller**, taken without asking questions or involving the affected salesperson; when leadership knowingly allows it to continue, that silence tells the sales team exactly how it is valued. Other markers: withholding credit from sales after a record quarter while thanking every other department, using salespeople as free labor on non-sales projects (which the weakest sellers accommodate most, lowering their production further, for which they are then blamed), and open-season criticism of the sales team as a matter of company fashion.

## 6. Why Salespeople Are Perceived as Vendors Rather Than Value Creators

The commoditization problem is not primarily caused by better-informed buyers or professional procurement departments; it is caused by salespeople's own approach, attitude, and behavior. Four named sins:

**Living in reactive mode and arriving late.** The most common and damaging driver. Weinberg attacks the widely quoted statistic that the modern buyer is 70 percent through the buying process before engaging a seller as a "bogus straw man": the *only* reason buyers get that far alone is that reactive salespeople sit waiting to be called in. Being late means buying criteria are already set and the seller is at the back of the line, while the proactive competitor occupies what he calls **"Position A"** — the consultant's seat, bringing value and insight, helping define the buyer's requirements, possibly having created the opportunity in the first place. From behind, only a low price buys attention.

**Leading with the product.** Making the offering the hero of the story tells the customer, loud and clear, that the seller is more interested in what he's selling than in the customer's issues. **When you live by the product, you die by the product** — leading with features and price is an invitation to be put on a comparison spreadsheet.

**Amateurish sales calls.** The common sins: failing to establish professionalism or assert control by setting up the meeting, sharing an agenda, and getting buy-in; arriving already in presentation mode and jumping to a demo; talking far too much and listening too little; and giving off the vibe of pitching *at* the prospect, which provokes a guarded, adversarial posture. The governing rule: **discovery precedes presentation — always.**

**Doing whatever the customer requests.** The super-responsive, highly relational, overly accommodating seller who is more concerned with being liked than respected *lowers* rather than raises the buyer's perception of him. The tests Weinberg applies: does the seller run headfirst into a premature presentation or demo, or pause, ask what prompted the request, and professionally push back until he has met stakeholders and understood the situation well enough to make the presentation relevant? Does he chase a premature proposal after twenty minutes of enthusiasm, or keep probing to learn what a good proposal requires and whether the prospect is even qualified? On blind RFPs from companies where no relationship exists, Weinberg's position is categorical: in twenty-five years he has never won one, and of thousands of salespeople he has met, not one has.

The high standard he borrows from Neil Rackham to judge whether a call was any good: **would the customer write a check for the sales call**, because the call did something so useful that the customer values it?

## 7. The Framework: Three Categories of Sales Management

"Sales" is not one big problem; it is many disparate things under one umbrella, which is why executives feel overwhelmed at the prospect of fixing it. Weinberg's filter sorts every sales issue into three buckets, in this deliberate order:

1. **Sales Leadership and Culture**
2. **Talent Management**
3. **Sales Process**

The order matters and is not arbitrary. Sales process is the piece Weinberg personally likes most and comes to most naturally, and he still places it third: you cannot transform a sales organization by coaching everyone into better salespeople, so leadership/culture and talent management must be fixed first. He states outright that **a team's leadership and culture are more critical to sales success than the skills of its producers.**

Diagnostic questions for the first bucket: what does it feel like to be on this sales team; is it a high-performance culture and why; are members laser-focused on goals and results; what does accountability look like here; how often, how big, and how loud are victories celebrated; is the manager leading or reacting; do salespeople leave team meetings better equipped and energized or drained; do team members feel supported, valued, and appreciated; does the comp plan drive the desired behaviors; in what ways is the manager putting fingerprints on the team; how much of the leader's time goes to non-sales activity; what is the level of intensity, passion, and heart-engagement.

Talent management gets its own sub-framework, the **Four Rs of Sales Talent Management** (detailed in Section 11): Right People in the Right Roles, Retain Top Producers, Remediate or Replace Underperformers, Recruit.

Sales process gets three manager responsibilities (Section 12): point the team at the right targets, arm the team and make it proficient with its weapons, monitor the battle.

## 8. The Healthy Sales Culture and the Radical Reallocation of Time

Culture is not a soft concept. Weinberg builds the chapter around the single healthiest sales culture he encountered in roughly 150 engagements — a company whose CEO told him, "everything flows from culture; culture is everything," and who called culture the company's secret sauce. Observable features of that culture: whiteboards everywhere charting a sales statistic, goal, or result; a loud room that felt like a championship locker room; per-person productivity two to three times the industry norm; an annual goal-setting process the CEO ran personally with each salesperson; managing every person as an individual and knowing what makes each tick (his rubber-band metaphor: find how far you can stretch each person, for their own good, without breaking them); regular manager-salesperson 1:1s to review goals and results, remove obstacles, and stretch the person.

Two counterintuitive details of that company. First, its hiring process was designed to **repel the wrong people** rather than to attract the best available talent — much of the lengthy interview process existed to scare candidates away. (The contrast case: a CEO who, fifteen minutes into a two-hour interview with a candidate the team had mixed feelings about, interrupted the questions and started selling the candidate on the company.) Second, communication in team meetings was the most direct, blunt, and confrontational Weinberg had seen anywhere, including heavy use of role play and brutally honest management feedback — and it worked because it was never personal, and because the same leaders were meeting 1:1 with those people and demonstrably had their interests at heart. The stated shared values read: we are elite; we keep score and talk about the scoreboard constantly; we are loud and proud; we guard who we add; we check egos at the door; when we flop in practice we get called out because it is not acceptable to flop in the real game; when we win big, we celebrate big.

**The reallocation.** Wanting that culture changes nothing; the battle "is begun and won" when the leader transitions to spending the majority of his time on high-value, high-payoff sales leadership activity. Weinberg names **three highest-value activities**:

1. Conducting one-on-one meetings with individual salespeople
2. Leading sales team meetings
3. Working alongside salespeople (observing, coaching, helping) when they are with customers and prospects

His blunt framing: when you are buried under 200 emails a day, trapped in meetings, holding the fire hose, scrambling for reports, and have no control of your calendar, **you are not leading anyone anywhere, and you have exactly the sales culture you deserve** — the one you created, by design or neglect.

## 9. The 1:1 Results-Focused Meeting and the Sales Management Accountability Progression

The single practice Weinberg credits with transforming sales cultures most reliably, and which he says may be the most important task on a manager's plate. The distinction he insists on: "I'm in regular contact with my people" is not the same as **a formal, scheduled meeting (phone or face-to-face) with each salesperson specifically to review their results and their pipeline of future opportunities.** Cadence is monthly; duration can be about twenty minutes.

**THE SALES MANAGEMENT ACCOUNTABILITY PROGRESSION**

1. **Results**
2. **Pipeline**
3. **Activity**

The order carries the magic, and the meeting can end at any stage.

**Results first**, because sales is about results and because leading with the scoreboard stops the verbal smokescreen underperformers use to dazzle managers with everything they have going on. Review last month against goal, year-to-date against goal, and relative ranking versus peers. If results are excellent, the meeting is over — pat on the back, keep it up. Because it is just facts, the meeting requires no histrionics: no raised voices, no profanity, no threats. Weinberg also locates accountability deliberately in private: sales reports are still published, posted, and reviewed in team meetings, and discomfort at the bottom of the rankings is intentional, but calling out and explaining poor individual performance publicly creates a demoralizing environment.

**Pipeline second**, because past results can't be changed but future results can. This is a quick, *joint* review of the general health of the funnel, not a deal strategy session or coaching session: number of opportunities, stage, dollar volume, likelihood of closing. The question being answered is whether this person has enough in the hopper to hit goal in the coming months. If the pipeline is robust, the meeting ends there.

**Activity third and only when forced.** Poor results plus a weak pipeline leave the manager nowhere else to go: pull out the calendar, review meetings held and planned, pull out the business plan, check committed activity levels, ask who is being targeted and what is being done to refill the pipeline, and offer help. Weinberg's ruling on the predictable objection: **when results are poor and the pipeline is weak, asking about activity is good management, not micromanagement.** The response to a salesperson playing the micromanagement card is that there are two options — improve results and pipeline quality so activity need not be discussed, or go work somewhere that is comfortable with failure.

**The two power questions** that give a fast, honest read on effectiveness and prevent smokescreens:

1. Can you name the new opportunities in your pipeline today that were not here when we met last month — the fresh opportunities you identified or created in the past month?
2. Can you name the existing opportunities you moved forward in the sales process since we reviewed your pipeline together last month?

Asked three months in a row, these two questions produce a reliable verdict on whether a salesperson can create new opportunities and advance existing ones — and, Weinberg's closing question about the third consecutive failed month, on how many more months of that conversation you need before making a talent decision.

## 10. Team Meetings and Fieldwork

**Sales team meetings** foster competition, cast vision, share best practices, conduct training, build relationships, and let salespeople see they are part of something bigger than their own territory. Why they miss the mark: no stated purpose; zero respect for time (late starts, late managers, meetings that run past their end); salespeople present in body but not in mind; uncontrolled bitch sessions where one complaint draws a feeding frenzy; meetings that are not sales meetings at all (one client's regional "sales" meetings devoted roughly 20 percent of the time to sales, the rest to inventory, service issues, and custom project status); using the meeting to beat up individuals rather than saving hard conversations for the 1:1; and the manager carrying the entire load as planner, host, facilitator, teacher, trainer, and motivator. The standard: **every salesperson should leave better equipped and more energized than when they walked in.**

Weinberg's menu of agenda items: **Personal Updates** (a minute or two each on what's going on in their lives, to build empathy and context); **Review Sales Results and Highlight Outstanding Performance** (scold in private, celebrate in public); **Success Stories** (a few reps tell how they won a deal, with permission to brag and instructions to be specific); **Product Training**; **Best Practice Sharing** (rotate team members through ten-minute segments — high value to peers, zero work for the manager); **Deal Strategy Brainstorming** (a rep presents a stuck deal, peers ask diagnostic questions, then everyone offers suggestions); **Executive or Other Department Guest Presentation**; **Book or Blog Review** (a chapter per meeting, with a different salesperson assigned to lead each discussion); **Sales Skill Coaching/Training** (as opposed to product training, which usually crowds it out); **Business Plan Presentations or Mid-Year Reviews**; **Brief, Controlled Bitch Session** (a bounded airing of grievances, where management scores points by listening and more points by removing the obstacle); **Non-Sales-Related Inspiration**; **Takeaways** (each person names two — one to implement immediately, one requiring time and work).

The tone-setting principle comes from Jim Harbaugh's answer on how he would turn a program around: **"We are going to start winning at our first team meeting."** Personality style is not prescribed — various styles succeed — but one trait disqualifies a sales manager outright: **negativity.** If the leader lacks a winning attitude, the team cannot have one. A closing tactic: ask a few of your best people for honest feedback on past meetings and what would be most valuable to them.

**Fieldwork** — working alongside a salesperson as she does her job — applies equally to inside and outside teams. It is not a nice-to-have: "this is the transmission. No transmission = no car." **Five benefits**, usually all available in a single day out:

1. **Observing them in action** — how they prepare and conduct calls, how they plan days, and the intangibles: is the car clean, is she dressed appropriately, does she know her way around the territory, is she on time. If this is her best effort in front of an important guest, what happens when no one is watching?
2. **Coaching before and after calls** — pre-call planning in the parking lot (who are we meeting, their personalities, their expectations, what's a win here, what's the plan, what are the biggest challenges), then a debrief in which **the salesperson gives her own assessment first** ("How'd we do?" / "What did you do well and what might you have done differently?") before the manager shares observations. Weinberg takes notes on the interaction rather than the content, and tracks the talk ratio: salespeople are often horrified to learn they spoke 80 percent of the meeting. You do not need to be a sales guru to give honest feedback on rapport, agenda-sharing, positioning, probing, presenting, and next steps.
3. **Windshield time and mealtime** — learning what makes each person tick, which is the prerequisite for helping them win. Friendship is not the goal; knowing the human being is.
4. **A firsthand read on the market** — because salespeople's complaints about product, pricing, or strategy are discounted as excuses until a leader sees the reality himself. Two cases: a CEO who revised company strategy after one trip through North Carolina agencies in 2009, and a sales executive who had aimed his whole team at a dying customer segment on a gut feeling, which thirty-plus ride-along calls exposed immediately.
5. **Relationships with key customers** — offensively, a manager's presence creates access to more senior people and elevates the conversation; defensively, it de-risks a salesperson leaving or falling out of favor with an account.

Three rules for fieldwork: **be present** (take two set breaks for email and calls, then turn the phone off — off, not vibrate, not do-not-disturb; if you can't go two or three hours without email, something is fundamentally broken in how you or your company define your job); **don't do your salesperson's job** (model the way then get out of the way; rescuing a drowning rep occasionally is fine, doing it as a rule means you are the problem, and painful failures create powerful learning opportunities); and **go with the salesperson, not without her** (calling on her accounts alone creates mistrust, diminishes her in the customer's eyes, and wastes a mentoring opportunity). On frequency Weinberg refuses a universal number — one-third to two-thirds of the time depending on the business — but notes that in roughly 150 companies he has worried only twice that a leader was in the field too much, and that in almost every case the manager could stand to get out of the office more.

## 11. The Four Rs of Sales Talent Management

Talent is the second bucket and, next to leading well and building culture, the most critical part of the job. The metaphor Weinberg inherited from his father: **it's not the power of the electric company that lights the room, it's the wattage of the bulb** — brand strength and corporate sales plans are worthless if the bulb facing the customer is short on watts. His caveat against "talent trumps everything": poor leadership and an unhealthy culture will eventually overcome even the strongest people.

**1. Right People in the Right Roles** — the first and hardest priority, obstructed by two realities: there is a shortage of solid sales talent (by his own observation, **true A-players are 10 to 20 percent of the sales population**; a talent-rich company might reach one-quarter, a talent-poor one one-tenth), and most companies lack the desire, insight, and patience to define sales roles properly. Three moves:

- **Kill the catch-all generic sales job.** Chart every phase of what the "salesperson" is expected to do, from getting a prospect's attention through discovery, probing, consensus-building, presenting, proposing, negotiating, and closing, and then everything after the sale: onboarding, main contact, relationship maintenance, quoting additional work, entertaining, cross-selling, and fighting service fires. Then ask whether one human can do all of it, and whether any other position in the company carries that range. Almost no one is good at both extreme ends of the spectrum: prospecting and hunting on the front end, account management and maintenance on the back end. The illustrative failure: one company's "tribrid" territory role that combined a $30,000 merchandising/display-building job, a $75,000 territory manager job, and a $120,000 key account manager job — because six-figure salespeople will not build and dust displays.
- **Stop pretending zookeepers will hunt.** The hybrid hunter-farmer role dominates B2B and is "not the best of both worlds; it's the worst." The majority of the sales population has account-manager DNA: highly relational, likable, service-oriented, reliable, productive when fed work or servicing customers — excellent at managing accounts, not at creating them. Hunting involves risk, conflict, and rejection permanently; top hunters are energized by exactly those three things, and relational or analytical sellers are their polar opposites (to them rejection is not just personal but paralyzing). The **zookeeper** label sharpens the usual hunter/farmer contrast: someone who bottle-feeds a baby mammal is never going to trade the bottle for a weapon. The observed behavior: the more pressure applied to sales engineers to find new business, the better they get at finding service emergencies at existing customers. The maxim from a veteran: **"Their hunter will kill your farmer every time."**
- **Free the hunters to hunt.** Companies short on hunters load the few they have with non-hunting responsibility, which Weinberg calls the dumbest thing he sees in sales. The prescription: strip as much account management and service responsibility away from your hunters as possible. His own case: a company that removed account management from him, surrounded him with competent account managers, and made new-business hunting his only job — the team booked over $7 million in his third year, roughly triple a typical good team's production.

Also under this R, the recurring hiring trap: **the big-reputation wily industry veteran.** The pattern is that the star became number one years ago when the market was hot, was then fed the best leads and the best accounts, and has not personally hunted down a new account in a decade — so she brings over a handful of past customers, never fills a pipeline, and never hunts. The compounding errors: happy ears that skipped asking *how* she became and stayed top producer, no clearly defined expectations for how she'd invest her time and hunt once onboard, and almost none of the recruiting and interviewing rigor that a regular candidate would face.

**2. Retain Top Producers** — because A-players are nearly impossible to replace. Never assume they're happy because they're on top of the rankings or because they're making a lot of money; top producers are the most driven people on the team, can be the most demanding, and are often the first to jump when frustrated or when they sense something structurally wrong that will cap future winning. Learn what makes each A-player tick rather than assuming public recognition (Weinberg has coached private, introverted killers who hated being called up in front of the company); drivers vary — freedom, a voice in big decisions, a visible trophy, private appreciation from senior executives, being genuinely heard on policies, tools, and compensation. The counterintuitive management principle, from his father: **managers overinvest in their worst performers at the expense of their best.** Top producers deserve more of your attention, not less, because they know what to do with it and are the people most likely to go get the additional sales you need. Concrete prescriptions: block calendar time solely for investing in A-players; over-support them, clear obstacles, and point them like lasers at the biggest and best markets and opportunities; consider a Top Dog Retreat where half the time treats the best producers like royalty and half is spent taking their input and letting them ask management hard questions. The checklist: who would devastate you if they left, how happy are they, how do you *know*, what are you personally doing to communicate their importance, what are other executives doing, and how are you demonstrably making their work lives easier.

**3. Remediate or Replace (coach up or coach out)** — the happy medium between the trigger-happy manager and the conflict-avoiding optimist. Remediation has two stages and one goal. **Informal remediation** comes first: telling a salesperson his performance is unacceptable and that you will work with him on it. **Formal remediation** follows if results don't improve and is typically nothing more than the formal process for removing him. A well-executed remediation always works, because both outcomes are good: either the person improves to an acceptable level, or he is set free from failing to succeed elsewhere. If the manager is already convinced the person is not a keeper (attitude, work ethic, integrity, role misfit, mis-hire), skip informal remediation entirely and move straight to formal to speed up termination.

Triggers for starting informal remediation: results lag the team; your gut puts a question mark on the person without your knowing exactly why; the salesperson doesn't seem to grasp that you and the company are serious about results; back-to-back frustrating 1:1s where answers on pipeline and activity don't reassure you; you're convinced the person needs clear marching orders and more intense direct supervision; or you believe overinvesting in coaching and fieldwork with this person will pay dividends. The governing rule: **if you are not sure whether it's time to put someone on informal remediation, then it's time.** Early is better than late; waiting is management malpractice. Informal means informal — no HR, no other executives required.

Two critical characteristics of a successful remediation. First, **crystal-clear, spelled-out expectations** — "show improvement" is not acceptable. The illustrative form: in the next sixty days, secure eighteen discovery meetings with named target accounts and create eight new pipeline opportunities, two of which reach a named stage and two more a later stage; plus a written mini-plan naming the specific accounts to be pursued, a planning calendar within two days so the manager can arrange fieldwork, a shift from monthly to weekly meetings (progress update first, coaching after), and the salesperson repeating the agreement back. Second, **the salesperson must fully own the targets** — the "CEO of his territory" framing, with the manager stopping the finger-pointing the second it starts. A salesperson who refuses to be fully accountable for what he produces is asking to be replaced, and the manager's job is to oblige. Once informal remediation has failed, no one turns it around during the formal stage; pull back, get HR involved, follow the termination procedure with a clean conscience, and redirect your attention.

**4. Recruit** — Weinberg's warning sign is executives who brag about near-zero sales turnover, which usually indicates a team that values longevity over results. **Recruiting for the manager is like prospecting for the salesperson**: very important, never urgent, and it never calls you — you have to call it. Two keys: maintain a segmented list (best referral sources for sales hires — customers, industry contacts, suppliers, your own salespeople, highly networked people in your sphere — plus a running list of actual candidates, the "bench"), and carve out recurring calendar time devoted exclusively to recruiting, exactly as you ask salespeople to time-block prospecting. In his experience, the leaders who were best at attracting top talent were masters at working referral sources and dedicated significant blocks of time to recruiting.

**The two killer interview questions**, saved for the end of every interview. First, *tell me about the last two significant deals you won due to your own proactive sales effort, the whole story from beginning to end* — how you identified or created the opportunity, how you got in, the discovery process, the stakeholders, how you built interest and consensus, and the chronology through presenting, proposing, negotiating, and closing. A true winner relishes the chance to strut; a poseur panics at that level of detail, and how hard it is to produce a recent success story is itself diagnostic. Second, *the ninety-day scenario*: assume we hired you, oriented you, gave you an email address and business cards, and then your manager and I left for Hawaii for ninety days — sketch out how you'd approach the job, where you'd start, what you'd do, where you'd go to learn more or get help, and what you'd be able to tell your manager you accomplished on day ninety-one. Watch how much clarification they request, and whether they build a real plan or shoot from the hip. Related interview signal: does the candidate make the interview about herself or about the company — is she prepared with great questions, seeking to understand what the company needs and why people succeed or fail in the role, or just promoting herself?

## 12. Sales Process: Strategic Targeting, Arming the Team, and Monitoring the Battle

**Step 1: Strategic targeting.** Targets are the named existing customers and prospective customers a salesperson identifies and commits to pursuing for new business. Managers routinely take for granted that their people have well-considered lists, but even the most talented seller will fail pursuing the wrong targets, and since the target list effectively drives how proactive selling time is spent, management must have input into who is on it. "Strategic" is just a fancy word meaning thought was actually applied.

Two failure modes to interrupt. **The milk run** — territory managers running the same routes assigned five years ago, in Abilene on the second Tuesday because their predecessor was, regardless of whether any important accounts remain there; they have come to see the job as managing a piece of geography rather than maximizing revenue. **Sales autopilot** — inside sellers and hunters who open the CRM activity screen, work down thirty scheduled tasks, and when asked which prospects they're pursuing this week answer "whichever accounts pop up on my screen," with no thought as to whether clearing overdue red tasks will produce a single meaningful opportunity. The screening rule for existing customers: **if an account is not large, growable, or at risk, it is not worthy of the salesperson's time.** Managers should call regular **targeting time-outs** and force these questions: do my current targets give the best chance of winning new business; have I fallen into a rut calling the same names repeatedly; is it time to re-segment my accounts or territory; how do I clean up open CRM tasks so I'm working what's most likely to produce. Corollary: a high-frequency attack beats a low-frequency attack **only when aimed at the best targets** — there are no awards for the most calls or the most miles.

On list size, Weinberg is deliberately contrarian: **less is more.** Salespeople prefer long lists and big territories for comfort and fear of missing out, and managers get a false sense of security from a team covering a wide swath. But an overlong list defeats focus and produces a scattershot "one and done" pattern — shoot and run, shoot and run — when prospecting usually requires multiple touches on the same target to earn a way in. The two operative words for a target list are **finite and workable**: enough targets to work, not so many that repeated shots at the same target over a dedicated period become impossible. A shotgun works for marketing; **the sniper rifle is the preferred sales tool.**

**Step 2: Arming the team.** Once targets are selected, the manager has two duties: ensure the troops carry the necessary sales weapons, and ensure they become proficient at firing them. A releasing point first: **the sales manager does not need to be the sales expert.** Culture, 1:1s, team meetings, fieldwork, and the Four Rs can all be handled well by someone with little sales acumen, and effective leaders without selling backgrounds are common, especially in smaller companies. That is not a pass to look away from arming the team — you are 100 percent responsible for it happening, whether via yourself, an internal associate, or an outside expert. And the accountability is symmetric: **if you want to hold the team accountable for results, you must be accountable for equipping them to win.**

The most important weapon is **the sales story** (what others call the value proposition or elevator pitch): a collection of compelling talking points whose pieces end up in every other weapon — LinkedIn profiles, phone outlines, voicemails, face-to-face calls, demos, webinars, presentations, and proposals. The diagnostic: ask several executives and salespeople to "tell me about [company]" and count how many different versions come back, then note how little of it is compelling. **Three common story sins:** too complex (takes ten minutes or ten slides to land), boring, and self-focused — leading with what the company does, how long it's been in business, ownership structure, and pictures of facilities. An effective story gives salespeople confidence to prospect; changes the dynamic of the sales dance and positions them as experts and consultants; gets attention; lets customers see quickly that what you sell addresses the issues they face; helps articulate true value; warms the customer up to answer probing questions; justifies a premium price and market position; differentiates from competitors; and makes salespeople prouder of their company.

Beyond the story, Weinberg's position is that **there is no magic bullet** and that the basics of selling still work — the overwhelming majority of accepted sales truths remain true, and leaders who focus on fundamentals succeed while those chasing shiny new toys perpetually struggle. The questions he prescribes for auditing the basics of the sales call: what is the minimum acceptable pre-call research and information gathering; does the company have a prescribed structure for sales calls from beginning to end; what materials and tools should be brought and which should be left behind or saved for later (hint: a projector); has the best way to set up the call by sharing an agenda and seeking buy-in been worked out; how well do people deliver a succinct version of the story to position themselves as customer-issue-focused problem solvers before probing; are team members armed with insightful probing questions that also demonstrate familiarity with the customer's likely issues; is everyone clear that discovery must precede presentation, and do they know how to respond when a customer insists on starting with a capabilities overview; how comfortable are they fleshing out objections and securing commitment to next steps; what happens after the call, what gets recorded, and what is done with it.

**Step 3: Monitoring the battle.** Targeting and arming are preparatory; monitoring is continuous, conducted through personal observation, sales reports, pipeline reports, 1:1 and team meetings, and individual business plans.

**Individual business plans are a gift to the sales leader.** Each salesperson drafts and presents an annual plan against a simple template. Writing it forces the seller out of the daily grind to think about the big picture, reinforces that the territory or book is *theirs* and that they own the strategies and results, and forces reflection on what worked last year and what should be abandoned. The template:

- **Goals — what are you going to achieve?** Total revenue or gross margin for the year, number of new accounts or new pieces of business acquired, dollars sold to existing versus new accounts, product-mix goals, and a "name and claim" of the monster or dream account they will land this year. (Weinberg notes how often a rep closes a dream client in the year they named it.)
- **Strategies — how are you going to do it, and where will it come from?** Market focus, target account lists, major cross-sell opportunities, most growable and most at-risk accounts, what new approaches will get them in front of prospects, how they'll penetrate current customers, and for territory roles, how they'll segment accounts and cover the territory more intentionally.
- **Actions — what are you going to do?** Activity and metrics: "The Math." Calls, initial face-to-face meetings, presentations committed to; how calendar time will be blocked for prospecting and pure new business; which key activity goals they will be held to.
- **Obstacles — what's in the way?** Asked up front, on day one, because almost every salesperson already knows what will get in the way. This lets management remove real obstacles or name them as the excuses they are, rather than discovering nine months in an issue the rep worried about from the start. Obstacles take many forms: training or knowledge gaps, prohibitive policies, travel budgets, old technology, the anti-sales department, family issues.
- **Personal Development, Growth, and Motivation — how do you want to grow this year and what will keep you motivated?** Courses, conferences, a mentor, outside coaching, books or blogs, professional development areas, and their own philosophy of what keeps them going.

Having the team **present** the plans to peers and management multiplies the value: strategies get shared, smart ones applauded and foolish ones challenged, public declaration creates instant accountability, and the manager learns who invested real effort versus who threw it together the night before, and who presents well versus who bombs in front of a room (a warning about how they perform in front of customers). The plan then keeps paying all year as a monitoring tool: pull it before a field day or when someone seems off track, name the specific strategies and actions they committed to, and ask how those are working — using their own words to hold them accountable, and stopping them cold when the answer is that they haven't gotten around to their own plan.

**Insist on the reports you need.** Do not compromise and do not accept "we didn't purchase that module" or "the ERP makes that hard." If you genuinely need a report to monitor the attack and make good decisions, you should have it; a CEO asking for the same thing would get it without question. The second reason is political: the same people resisting your report requests will be the fastest to generate their own showing that sales is over-discounting, overspending, or missing margin. Decide exactly how you want to see results each month — by territory, salesperson, product line, customer, or supplier, versus goal and versus last year — then ask for it politely, buy the accounting or IT colleague lunch, and do not take no for an answer.

**Be selfishly productive.** The closing principle mirrors the book's opening: **you were not hired to do work, you were put in the position to produce results.** Working a bazillion hours, jamming the calendar, and living perpetually overwhelmed is not a badge of honor and does not make a leader more valuable or more secure. The most effective executives are **selfishly productive** — ruthless with their time, jealously guarding their calendars as if time were their oxygen supply, and masters of the word "no." **Time-blocking** works both defensively and offensively: defensively, a calendar already filled with what you should be working on is unavailable to others; offensively, it guarantees sufficient time is carved out for the high-value activities. The test of whether something is fundamentally broken: a drowning sales manager whose team is missing its numbers who is nonetheless too busy to work in the field with his people or to hold a monthly 1:1 with each salesperson.
