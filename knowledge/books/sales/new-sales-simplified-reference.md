# New Sales. Simplified. — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing new-business-development/prospecting processes, or advising on outbound sales strategy.*
*Book: "New Sales. Simplified." — Mike Weinberg*

---

## 1. The Premise: Sales Is Simple, New Business Is Its Own Discipline

Weinberg's starting position is that selling is not complicated and that the people making it sound complicated are "either confused themselves or trying to confuse others by creating a smokescreen to hide their lame effort and poor results." The whole job reduces to one sentence: people and companies have needs, we represent businesses with potential solutions to those needs, and our job is to connect with customers and prospective customers to determine whether our solutions meet their needs. "The more and better we do that simple job, the more successful we will be."

What makes new business development distinct from selling in general is that nobody arrives at it by accident. The book's most-repeated line is **"No one defaults to prospecting mode. No one."** There is no scenario in which a salesperson finds fifteen free minutes and spontaneously grabs a target list to start dialing. Account management, customer service, internal meetings, and personal distraction all have gravitational pull; prospecting has none. Everything in the framework exists to counteract that.

Weinberg identifies three macro forces that left the current generation of salespeople unequipped for it. First, most people employed in sales have never truly had to hunt, because long stretches of economic prosperity (most of the 1990s, and 2002 through 2007) meant inbound demand was sufficient and highly relational reps thrived reactively. Second, the **Sales 2.0 fallacy** — the claim that proactive targeting and cold calling are dead and that buyers will approach you when they are ready. Weinberg treats this as actively destructive, because it is precisely what the passive-reactive salesperson wants to hear: "giving a sugar addict trying to get in shape a king-size candy bar." His position is that technology and new media are "a great supplement to, not a replacement for, our prospecting efforts." Third, a **shortage of sales mentors** — first-line sales managers have devolved from field coaches who took pride in teaching people how to sell into desk jockeys more concerned that their reps keep the CRM updated than whether they can actually sell. The consequence is salespeople "more proficient at entering tasks into Salesforce.com than they are at executing the basics."

The operating slogan throughout is **"Sales is a verb."** The most successful new-business salespeople tend to be the most active salespeople, and the standing formula is that good things happen when a talented salesperson with a potential solution gets in front of a prospective customer who looks, smells, and feels a lot like the existing best customers.

## 2. The "Not-So-Sweet 16" Reasons Salespeople Fail at New Business Development

Compiled from observing hundreds of salespeople across roughly fifty companies, this is a diagnostic list, reviewed at the kickoff of every coaching engagement, designed to be used as a mirror. Weinberg's instruction is to identify the three or four that apply to you, and to ask colleagues, because "sometimes we have a hard time seeing ourselves accurately."

1. **They haven't had to or don't know how** — never forced to hunt, never had it modeled. "Go make calls" does not qualify as coaching or training.
2. **They are always waiting (on the company)** — waiting on new marketing materials, a new website, clearer marching orders, warm leads. Top performers wait for nothing and "proactively attack target accounts even if it means getting into trouble because they're so far out in front of the support curve."
3. **They are "prisoners of hope"** — a term Weinberg attributes to Tom Reilly (*Value-Added Selling*). They stop working the process and pursuing new opportunities because they are so hopeful the precious few deals in the pipeline will close, and instead spend their time talking about, worrying about, and wondering about a deal predicted to close last month. "A few moldy deals, passivity, and luck are not a winning formula."
4. **They can't "tell the story"** — self-focused, long-winded, confusing messaging that neither differentiates nor compels. "Is there a greater sin in sales than boring your audience?"
5. **They have awful target account selection and a lack of focus** — either calling the wrong list or, far more commonly, refusing to focus on the list they have. They attempt an account once, get nowhere, and turn to a completely new set of targets, so they never gain traction.
6. **They are "late to the party"** — arriving after the prospect is already shopping means reacting instead of leading, initial opinions already formed, evaluation process already defined, and being seen as a potential supplier rather than a value creator. Worst case is responding to an RFP a competitor helped write. Weinberg explicitly rejects the guru position that a well-qualified, educated, ready buyer is what you want.
7. **They have a negative attitude and pessimistic outlook** — "Lazy, complacent, excuse-making salespeople with a victim mentality lose. Period." Sales winners take full responsibility for results.
8. **They are guilty of a fake or pitiful phone effort** — the *fake* effort is the well-intentioned time block consumed by news sites, LinkedIn, stock checks, and email; the *pitiful* effort is making the calls while so nervous and uncomfortable as to be ineffective.
9. **They are not likable, don't adapt their style, and have low EQ** — people buy from people they like. Quirks, habits, and an inability to flex communication style to the buyer's (fast-talking passion-driven rep calling on a reserved data-seeking technical buyer) break the connection.
10. **They can't conduct an effective sales call** — no plan, or a plan never shared, so control defaults to the prospect and the seller talks far too much. They forget the purpose is to find pain, problems to solve, and opportunities to capture, and they run right past the clues buyers offer.
11. **They love to babysit their existing accounts** — the most sensitive item on the list. Weinberg is not arguing against serving customers; he is naming the opportunity cost of a salesperson spending 95 percent of their time on existing accounts, which "sounds a lot more like a customer service role than a sales role."
12. **They are busy being good corporate citizens** — it is rarely the person voted most pleasant, selfless member of the team who thrives at acquiring new business. "Top-performing salespeople tend to be productively selfish with their time."
13. **They don't own their own sales process** — without a clear mental picture of the path to a sale, they default to the buyer's process: presenting before any discovery, or **Premature Proposal Syndrome** (proposing before identifying decision criteria, all the key players, and the true underlying issues driving the request).
14. **They don't use and protect their calendar** — it is rare to find a salesperson with a written business plan, and rarer still to find one whose business plan dictates what goes on the calendar.
15. **They stopped learning and growing** — most salespeople read nothing about sales. "Arrogance, ignorance, and underachievement are a dangerous and ugly combination."
16. **Honestly, they are not built for it** — the hardest to say. Two behavioral profiles struggle structurally in a hunting role: the highly relational person who cannot tolerate the conflict, risk, and rejection required to push past resistance ("they hear one 'no' and immediately say 'thank you for your time'"), and the highly analytical, super-conscientious type who needs all the data before acting and fears being wrong. "Sales is about action, and analysis-paralysis is not a quality that tends to produce new business development success."

## 3. The Company's Responsibility for Sales Success

Weinberg holds that sales cannot be fixed in isolation, and devotes a full chapter to the environmental factors that make the framework unworkable no matter how well an individual executes it. Coaching engagements repeatedly metamorphose into consulting for exactly this reason.

**Sales follows strategy.** Clarity is non-negotiable. The sales team's job is to take a clear strategy and execute it to perfection in the market; salespeople should not be making it up as they go. The chief executive owes the sales organization crystal-clear answers on: the company's reason for existence; the direction the company is headed and why it is the correct course; what we sell and why we sell it; which markets to pursue and where we are positioned in them; the competitive landscape and why we are better or different; and why our pricing model is appropriate for the value we create. Where those answers do not come, "100 percent of the sales shortfall lies at the feet of the CEO, not the head of sales, and certainly not on the sales rep."

**A low view of sales.** In too many companies salespeople are treated as the enemy and the problem, and the sales manager's desk becomes the garbage dump for every operational complaint. It is wrong, it does not help, and it does not increase sales.

**Heavy service burden and the hybrid hunter-farmer role.** Weinberg names this "the single largest issue detracting from new business development success." Most organizations agree that only 10 to 15 percent of a sales team qualifies as true A-player hunters, while solid account managers are in abundant supply. The illogic is then loading the few hunters with account management work. The fishing metaphor: the one person out of nine who can actually catch marlin is required to bring the fish back, clean it, prepare it, cook it, serve it, then bus the tables and do the dishes, so he fishes 25 percent of the time. The question to force is what happens if you set it up so your best catcher spends 75 percent of the time fishing.

**Illogical compensation plans.** Two specific faults. First, too large a portion of total compensation is fixed, so there is not enough discrepancy between what top and bottom performers earn; underachievers get overpaid and top producers underpaid, which retains the underperformer and pushes the top performer to leave. The principle: the more variable the possible results, the more variable the total compensation should be. Second, treating all sales the same. If an existing client base is "essentially a commission annuity" paying the same every year for maintenance, the rational rep overservices it. Weinberg's prescription is to decrease commission payout on existing business over time and pay an overly generous commission in year one of new deals.

**Mistrust, micromanagement, and treating the sales team like children.** Sales is as much a matter of the heart as of the head. Reps' demeanor, pride in the company, energy, and confidence all matter, which means an anti-sales culture makes coaching a waste: "It's meaningless if the people have no passion for their jobs."

## 4. The New Sales Driver

The core framework, road-tested across ten years as a hunter and fifty client engagements, deliberately reduced with each revision until only three components remained:

**THE NEW SALES DRIVER**
**A. Select targets.**
**B. Create and deploy weapons.**
**C. Plan and execute the attack.**

The battle metaphor is intentional and is used because it communicates the model to salespeople and companies more effectively than softer language. Targets come first because it is impossible to pursue something without knowing which direction to head. Weapons come second because we must not only be armed but become proficient at firing those weapons at the selected targets. Execution comes third because "it's all academic unless you actually take the field" — you must declare which weapons you will shoot at which targets and when, then do it, monitoring and measuring activity along the way.

**The bold declaration:** if an individual salesperson or a company's sales team is not successfully acquiring new business, the cause of the failure is in one of the three components. Restated as the three possible diagnoses:

- **Poor target selection or lack of focus on selected targets**
- **Lame sales weapons or lack of proficiency deploying weapons**
- **Inadequate planning or lack of execution of the plan**

Most teams struggle with more than one. The declaration carries three explicit assumptions, all of which must hold before a sales problem can be treated as a sales problem: the business has a clear strategy, a defined place in the market, and demand for its offering; the sales compensation plan is not working against the desired sales effort; and the sales talent would at least qualify as "average" (a B- or better).

## 5. Selecting Targets: First for a Reason

Choosing target accounts, which is effectively choosing how to invest your time, is "one of the few truly strategic things we do in sales" and one of the rare chances to step back from repetitive execution and ask big-picture questions. Weinberg advocates senior leadership involvement in the decision, because the salesperson is entitled to input ensuring strategic alignment and management should care how the sales organization invests its time.

**The target list must be finite, focused, written, and workable.**

- **Finite** — successful hunters lock in on a defined number of strategic targets and methodically work and rework that set until they get noticed, get in, and gain traction. A pile of folders, trade publications, a Book of Lists, and database printouts is not a list.
- **Focused** — periods of greatest success come from laser focus on a vertical market or account type. Repeated calling on the same type of company makes you an expert in its language, nuances, and business issues, builds a case-study arsenal, and makes early successes easy to replicate. "There's no prize for sales creativity. Find the path of least resistance and then focus like mad on that very path."
- **Written** — handwritten and color-coded on a whiteboard or printed on a spreadsheet, top performers can point to their list at any moment. Scrolling through CRM screens is not the same as pulling out a concise one-page list; the act of writing and posting the list itself increases activity and results.
- **Workable** — the right number depends entirely on the type of sale, sales role, and sales cycle (from a dozen enterprise accounts to 400 for an inside rep). Too many and none get deserved attention; too few and the rep runs out of targets and surfs the Internet.

**Segmenting existing accounts.** All accounts are not created equal, and working accounts by habit and convenience rather than opportunity is "the milk run." Divide existing customers into four categories: **Largest** (in dollars spent, not organization size), **Most Growable** (best opportunity for incremental revenue), **Most At-Risk** (highest probability of losing some or all of the business), and **Other** (everything that qualifies for none of the first three). Accounts can appear in more than one category. The governing principle is **intentional imbalance** — deliberately overinvesting in Most Growable, Largest, and Most At-Risk while giving the Other category little time or mind share. "There are no prizes for salespeople who work really hard. The rewards accrue to those who move the dial."

**The Who and Why questions** used to build the list: Who are our best customers (by industry, size, business model, location)? Why did they initially become customers, and why do they still buy from us? Who do we compete against? Why and when do they beat us, and why do prospects choose us over them? Who used to buy from us, and why did we lose the business? Who almost became a customer but didn't? Who has referred business to us in the past, and who should be referring business to us? "Building a great list is easy once we have these answers and just about impossible without them." The first priority is pursuing prospects that look, feel, and smell like the very best existing clients.

**Referral and indirect selling.** In roles where referral sources and decision influencers matter more than the end user (loan officers working real estate agents, flooring reps calling architecture firms to get product specified), the referral sources are themselves the targets and require their own strategic, finite, focused, written, and workable list, with activity and results tracked by source type and intentional imbalance applied over time toward the sources producing the best opportunities.

**Resources for identifying targets:** local business journals and the annual **Book of Lists** from American Cities Business Journals; **Hoover's** (a Dun & Bradstreet company) for corporate data and custom list building; **LinkedIn**, described as the best-liked, fastest-growing, and still most underutilized tool for sales professionals; and trade shows and industry associations, where attending is cheap relative to exhibiting, walking the aisles identifies real prospects, and breakout sessions reveal the hot issues facing target accounts.

**Dream targets.** Reserve a few list slots for gigantic prospects, a term Weinberg credits to Chet Holmes (*The Ultimate Sales Machine*) and to Anthony Iannarino's writing. Two rules: limit the selection to four or five, and sketch a unique mini-attack plan for them, setting aside a small percentage of time every week or two to advance the ball; and keep fervently working the normal targets, because dream-client pursuit is high-risk and low-probability and the danger is becoming a prisoner of hope.

**Targeting contacts higher in the organization.** The counterintuitive point is that calling higher is not harder or more frightening, it is easier and more fun, and the fear is self-induced from incorrect assumptions with no supporting evidence. Executives are generally smarter, more professional, and bigger-thinking, and more concerned with solving business issues and improving results than with protecting the status quo. The key to their interest is speaking about the issues on their mind, not product detail, which is why a sharp customer-focused story is essential. The asymmetry that settles the argument: if you shoot high and get told no, you are no worse off and can still work the level you customarily target; but if you start low and get told no, "it's infinitely harder to then take your pursuit up the ladder." The second-best outcome is the executive redirecting you internally, which converts the next call into a referral call.

## 6. The Weapons Arsenal

Weinberg's inventory of what is available to fire at selected targets. The framing is the salesperson as an elite fighter pilot loading the jet for the mission. Two failure modes bracket the section: reps who are ill-equipped and therefore reluctant to engage, and reps who are armed but ineffective and take terrible shot after terrible shot.

The arsenal: **the sales story** (foundational, incorporated into every other weapon); **networking**; **social media**; **e-mail** (the primary first-contact weapon for many hunters, less disruptive and less reliant on phone skill, so a creative and less intimidating entrée); **the proactive telephone call** ("bar none, the phone is still the most deadly and accurate weapon to score a face-to-face meeting"); **voice mail** (an opportunity to drop a tidbit of story and begin building a relationship, not a thing to whine about); **traditional printed marketing materials**; **digital marketing tools** (blogs, podcasts, online videos, webinars, used to deliver ideas and value as appetizers that entice prospects to want more); **white papers and industry experts** (third-party or academic information that buyers receive more readily, and which implies the salesperson providing it has a solution to the issues presented); **the initial face-to-face sales call** (the pinnacle; the goal of every weapon launched beforehand is to set it up); **probing questions**; **case studies**; **samples and demos**; **trade shows**; **facility tours**; **team selling**; **entertainment**; **presentations**; **proposals** (the final weapon fired and the one they can say yes to); and **references** (more credible than anything you can say about yourself, worth cultivating in variety so you can pick the most relevant ones, and acceptable to coach on what to say).

The critical filter: of all the weapons listed, only a handful are truly owned by sales; the rest are developed and maintained elsewhere in the organization and sales simply fires them. The three mission-critical weapons the salesperson fully controls, and which have the most dramatic impact on performance, are **the sales story, the proactive telephone call, and the face-to-face sales call.**

## 7. The Sales Story and the Power Statement

No weapon is more important than the sales story, because it is used all day every day and is incorporated into every other weapon: marketing letters, e-mails, LinkedIn profiles, telephone outlines, voice mail, sales calls, presentations, and proposals. Weinberg reports that the vast majority of his clients had no uniform, coherent story anyone in the organization could express, and that over-reliance on marketing tools and toys is a symptom: "We shouldn't need a four-color handout to tell a prospect about our company... It should take a minute, maybe two, to tell our story, pique a prospect's interest, and begin a sales dialogue." A manufactured story that does not align with reality crumbles in the hands of the sales team, because salespeople know what customers actually experience.

**Your sales story is not about you.** "Prospective customers are not interested in what you do; they are only interested in what you can do for them." The most common sin is self-focus. The **"So what?" test** is the diagnostic: after every statement a salesperson makes, ask "So what?" and count how much of it is self-focused drivel with no meaning to the customer. The related failure is that leading with what you do invites the reply "We already have that." Weinberg's related theme is that **sameness will kill us**, and that differentiation is not only what earns the meeting but what justifies premium pricing: "A premium price requires a premium story." Justifying the delta between your price and the alternatives is the salesperson's job, since a company with the lowest price would not need salespeople at all. The secondary payoff of a strong story is internal: confidence and pride, which changes how the rep approaches prospecting and engages prospects.

**Three critical building blocks, in this order:**
1. **Client issues addressed** — customer pains we remove, client problems we solve, opportunities we help customers capture, results we achieve for clients.
2. **Offerings** — simply what we sell, the services, solutions, or products we bill for.
3. **Differentiators** — why we are better and different from other alternatives, and therefore the best choice to address the issues in block one.

"And the sequence matters, a lot." Lead with client issues because that is where the power is derived: they are the sharp tip of the sales spear, they are relevant, they shake prospects out of their slumber and delay reflex resistance, they position you as a problem solver, and they foreshadow where you plan to take the conversation and set up the probing questions you will ask later. Leading with offerings is the worst option because it screams commodity. Leading with differentiators is better than leading with product but is still about you, risks being meaningless to the prospect, and risks being read as bragging. Leading with issues also qualifies the prospect: **No Issues = No Sale.** If your best description of why customers turn to you produces no reaction, the conversation is over and you move on.

**The Power Statement** is Weinberg's replacement for the elevator pitch and the value proposition. It is a one-page, two- to three-minute encapsulation of the story, used in full form face-to-face on sales calls and excerpted for every other weapon. Its structure:

- **Headline** — one to two sentences of introduction, providing context and letting the audience place you in a category.
- **Transitional phrase** — a brief lead-in that sets the stage and opens the door to the client issues, starting with either the type of business you pursue or the position of the contact: "GHI companies turn to [Company] when…" or "Senior marketing executives look to us when they…" This is a deliberate device that lets you speak in the third person about what you accomplish, couching your strongest selling points as if they were a testimonial from your best clients rather than a brash declaration.
- **Client issues / pains removed / problems solved / results achieved** — three to seven issues in conversational bulleted format, each described briefly using provocative or emotionally charged words: striving to achieve, frustrated from dealing with, under significant pressure to eliminate, committed to accomplishing, they've had it with, facing threats or regulatory pressure from, finally tired of living with.
- **Offerings** — a few sentences, no embellishment. Deliberately short and sandwiched between issues and differentiators because offerings are the least compelling component of the story.
- **Differentiators** — at least five reasons you are the best choice, led into by an intriguing sentence such as "[Company] continues to grow (or dominate our space) because we are very different from what you will find in the marketplace…"

**The Sales Story Exercise.** Run it with a group where possible, using three blank sheets for a brain dump, one each for client issues, offerings, and differentiators. Tackle client issues first because it is the most critical and takes the most effort. Seed the sheet with: Why did your best customers initially come to you? What business problems were they facing? What results were they looking to achieve? Then dig deeper: What pains are potential customers likely to experience by choosing or staying with the wrong provider? What problems do prospects experience trying to self-perform what you should be handling? What opportunities will they miss or results will fall short because they are not your customer, i.e. what is the opportunity cost of not working with you? Then go outside: call (do not e-mail, because you want voice inflection, emphasis, and emotion) your favorite customers and raving fans and ask why they came to you in the first place, what they were looking to achieve, what issues they were looking to address, why you still have their business, and how you have made their life and business better. Finally, mine your own marketing materials for phrases about business issues addressed, being careful not to slide back into reasons your company is wonderful. Argue the list down to roughly seven best client-issue bullets, then wordsmith each to follow the transitional phrase. Differentiators come faster and should cover the full range from culture to technical expertise to proprietary processes to service guarantees, and should include yourself where you are a genuine differentiator. Offerings should take under five minutes. Expect a few weeks of tweaking; carry it around and keep tinkering.

**The power statement is an internal document, not a handout.** It is a source document from which every other weapon is excerpted: trim the fat and add a salutation to produce the strongest introductory letter you have ever sent; personalize an introduction, pivot to a transitional phrase, and drop in two key issues for a drip e-mail, repeating one issue and introducing a new one in the follow-up a week later; and convert the client-issue bullets into open-ended probing questions for discovery by simply turning each statement around. It is also **the commodity antidote**, giving reps in highly price-sensitive businesses a way to articulate the meaningful reasons other than price and availability that customers buy from them.

## 8. The Proactive Telephone Call and Voice Mail

Weinberg refuses the term "cold call" because of the visceral negative reaction it triggers, and renames it **the proactive telephone call**. It is the most requested coaching topic in his practice. He concedes he does not enjoy making these calls, has done them for a long time, and is very good at them. The Sales 2.0 claim that proactive calls are no longer needed is called out as the most dangerous myth in the movement precisely because it is what reluctant prospectors want to hear: inbound marketing is "a magnificent supplement to, but not a replacement for" the outbound proactive call.

**Erase the tapes and fix the mindset.** Most reps arrive predisposed against calling because they picture themselves as the scripted, droning, headset-wearing telemarketer interrupting a family dinner. The corrective belief: making proactive calls to strategically selected target accounts that very likely have business issues you can address is not telemarketing. "You, my friend, are an important business person representing a company whose product or service potentially delivers great value to the prospect you are targeting." Once you view yourself as a professional problem solver who can probably help, the motivation for the call changes and everything downstream changes with it.

**Voice tone and approach.** The prospect processes nothing but how you sound and what your first few words communicate. The core problem is that salespeople *sound like salespeople*: a manufactured "sales voice" that makes an otherwise likable conversationalist sound like a dork. Have trusted peers listen without your knowing and tell you bluntly whether you sound different on prospect calls than with peers; if so, lose the sales voice. Use a casual, comfortable, genuine tone, even slightly informal. Being overly respectful and formal is not helpful: it is unnatural, contributes to the contrived sales voice, and positions you as subservient or intimidated. See yourself as an equal to the buyer.

**Script or no script?** No and yes. Weinberg rejects scripts because they imply theater, memorization, and reading, and because most reps cannot carry one off, which destroys the mindset and tone work above. He is a strong proponent of **call outlines and scripted talking points**: calls should be logically structured and several key phrases scripted verbatim, because consistency is what makes it possible to judge what is working.

**Laser focus on the objective.** There are only two legitimate objectives. For the **inside rep**, the goal is to have the full sales conversation, either on that call or scheduled for later. For the **outside rep**, there is exactly one true objective: get the face-to-face meeting. Knowing the objective matters because the prospect will put up defenses that must be circumvented, and if you are unsure where you want to end up you will get lost along the way.

**Stop overqualifying.** Weinberg's deliberately contrarian position. One of the main reasons outside reps underperform is that they are not in front of enough prospects; the math is not working because activity is too low. "Show me the sales rep that's failing because he's having too many unqualified appointments with strategically selected target accounts and I'll show you a thousand failing from a lack of activity." The framework itself settles the argument: selecting targets is step one, so the decision to want this account face-to-face has already been made strategically and should not be second-guessed on the fly during a first phone call.

**Introductory phrases.** The opening seconds are the hardest and most critical, because the moment prospects realize they have answered a sales call the resistance shield deploys.
- **"Let me steal a minute"** — delivered after the prospect's first name, your first and last name, and your company. It is conversational and casual, it is nothing like what every other salesperson says, and it acknowledges up front that you are an unexpected intrusion, which is human and real. Explicitly rejected openers: "How are you today?", "Did I catch you at a good time?", "Can I have a minute of your time?" — all of them hand the prospect an easy way to give you an answer you don't want, and all of them tip your hand as just another rep.
- When you can tell the prospect is genuinely harried, **let them go** — tell (do not ask) them you will call back, and score the points for being human. The second call is easier, you are more confident, the prospect thinks more highly of you, and you have an opening line: "I'm the guy who caught you late on Tuesday trying to run out to FedEx."
- **"I head up…"** — the next phrase out of your mouth, positioning who you are before going further. Its value is as much psychological as communicative: it makes you sound and feel like an important business person rather than a telemarketer. Regardless of formal title, everyone heads up something.

**The telephone mini power statement.** Insert a miniature version of the full power statement, serving "a killer appetizer, a delicious mouth-watering tease that creates a strong hunger for the main course," which is the meeting. Take **two favorite client-issue bullets** — one unique and provocative, one broad and applicable to almost any prospect on the list — and optionally **one differentiator**. That is the limit: any more takes too long and sounds like reading a script. Deliver it conversationally, naming the prospect's own company where you can, and then **stop.** The instinct to fill the silence is wrong; you want the prospect to process the issues and associate you with solving them. Based on the response, either ask for the meeting or fire one differentiator or one more client issue and then ask.

**For the inside rep: build a bridge.** After the telephone power statement, bridge to the sales conversation with simple probing questions, relevant precall research, or by directly asking how to go about building a meaningful relationship with the company. The most beneficial thing the inside rep can do is listen and record information useful for continuing to work the account.

**Ask for the meeting, ask again, and once more.** Be ready to ask **three times.** The prospect is programmed to say no automatically; it is not your fault and nothing you did or failed to do. "So don't take it personally. But don't hang up the phone, either." This is one of the points where prospecting requires conflict and pushing past resistance, which is why highly relational people struggle with it. Reps consistently credit their improved phone results to the willingness to ask three separate times, and it is usually on the third ask that the prospect relents.

**Three magic words** to use in every request for a meeting:
1. **Visit** — "appointment" is clinical and nobody wants another meeting; you visit with family and friends. "Would you have thirty minutes to visit with me next Tuesday or Wednesday?"
2. **Fit** — demonstrates confidence, disarms the prospect, is non-threatening, and shows you are not desperate. "Let's get together to see if we might be a fit to help you…"
3. **Value** — either "we'll review your current situation and see if we can bring some value to what you're doing," or as a promise that they will get value from the meeting itself.
The trifecta combines all three after the predictable first and second no: "Judy, I understand that you're [objection]. Visit with me anyway. I promise you'll get value and ideas from our time together, even if we end up not being a fit to help you."

**Winning with voice mail.** Reps reach voice mail roughly three-quarters of the time, so it must be treated as an opportunity rather than a complaint. Six rules:
1. **Adopt a positive perspective** — it takes X touches to break through; be thankful for the chance to make this one a positive touch.
2. **Expect and prepare for it** — if you will get voice mail three-quarters of the time, be prepared with a well-crafted, articulate message instead of babbling, rambling, or hitting the pound key to kill the evidence.
3. **Use a snippet of your story** — one or two main client issues, same logic as the mini power statement, leaving them intrigued to hear more.
4. **Take the long view; see it as a campaign** — accept that it takes multiple messages, picture this one as part of a series, and vary the story snippet each time. Almost nobody calls back after one message.
5. **Ask for a call back; state that you will call again** — leave the number at a pace that allows writing it down, and signal that you are serious about reaching them. "Don't be a 'one and done' salesperson."
6. **Be human; use humor or guilt, not anger** — the goal is to convert the buyer from seeing an anonymous robocaller to seeing a real person. The third message, with something funny or a light play on having earned a callback through perseverance alone, tends to do the trick. Never show anger or frustration: "We have no right to be angry at prospects who don't know us and haven't asked us to call. If they're not calling back, it's on us, not on them."

Practice on smaller or more insignificant accounts first.

## 9. The Face-to-Face Sales Call: Preparation and the Eight Phases

The initial face-to-face call with a target prospect is "the pinnacle of what we do as sales professionals," and business is won earlier in the process than most people think, by those who get in front of declared target accounts and set the tone for the relationship.

**It's your call; you need a plan.** Even when the prospect pursued you and requested the meeting, it is your call. Most outside reps have a clear goal for their calls and no formalized plan for how the meeting should flow. Weinberg's account of the rep who refused to plan because he preferred his meetings "organic" is the cautionary case: the call vacillated between the buyer controlling the meeting and the buyer sitting silently while the rep babbled. The unavoidable consequence: **if we don't have a plan and process when sitting down with a prospect, the meeting defaults to the buyer's process** — "What do you have for me?", "You've got your thirty minutes. Go.", or the aggressive prospect who peppers you with questions until thirty minutes are gone and you have learned nothing.

The **pre-call planning drill**, drawn from the mentor-manager model: Who are we meeting with? Describe each person's behavioral style. What is important to each person attending? Why do they think we are here today and what are they expecting? What is going on in their business that I need to know? What is your main objective today, and what is a win walking out of here? What is your plan for the call? Where are we vulnerable and what is our Achilles' heel? Which competitors are involved and who is more entrenched? The matching post-call debrief: How did you handle yourself? What would you do differently next time? What was I trying to accomplish at point X? What is your plan for following up and what help do you need?

**Bring a pad and pen; leave the projector at home.** "Presenting is not a synonym for selling." It would take an extraordinary argument to justify a projector at an initial meeting. The sales law: **discovery always precedes presentation. Always.** Pitchmen and product-pushers present before they understand the situation; consultative professionals gather information, connect, and begin building a relationship first. The named failure modes are the **"show up and throw up"** and the **"spray and pray"** — the salesperson goes first, sprays out everything he can, then prays he hit something relevant.

**God gave you two ears and one mouth.** Track your talk-listen ratio; reps who feel good about their balance routinely discover they spoke 80 to 90 percent of the words. The target ratio Weinberg pushes toward is one-third talking, two-thirds listening. "When you're talking you aren't learning."

**Selling from the same side of the table.** The seller-buyer relationship is set up as adversarial and does not have to be. Approach the prospect as an ally, and communicate that physically: do not default to sitting opposite the buyer. Sit perpendicular at a ninety-degree angle, or beside them at a round table. Decide before sitting down how to physically communicate that you are there as an ally, not an adversary.

**The phases of a winning sales call** — seven phases for a prospect, eight for an existing customer. The order has a significant effect on the outcome. The governing image is pilot-in-command: "We don't go on sales calls. We conduct them."

1. **Build rapport and identify the buyer's style.** Two goals: make the prospect comfortable and connect relationally (current headlines, last night's game, something intriguing from their LinkedIn profile, not the mounted marlin), and figure out who is in front of you behaviorally. The answer to "how long should rapport building last" is: **as long as the person you are meeting wants it to last.** Then slightly adapt your approach to be more like the customer: smile with the happy buyer, speed up and push hard with the hard-charger, slow down and be ready with detail for the analytical. Weinberg avoids the word "mirroring" because he does not want salespeople to act, but accepts the concept: adapting lets the buyer actually hear you because you are communicating in a behavioral language that makes them comfortable.
2. **Share the agenda and set up the call.** Named "the most important phase of the sales call." Three benefits: it is a **big differentiator** because almost nobody does it, and it signals you are not an amateur and that you respect the allotted time; it **informs the buyer where you are headed**, which is a professional courtesy; and it **lets the buyer know you expect a dialogue**, shaking them out of the selective listening they default to when expecting a monologue. The structure of the setup: confirm the time available and commit to ending on time; state that you will take two or three minutes on the issues you solve for people in their position and why they bring you in, and briefly on how you are different; state that you will then turn the tables and ask questions to understand their situation; state that depending on what you hear you can share relevant case studies or options; state that you will then discuss whether there is a fit or a logical next step. Then hand it back with a smart question: **"That is what I was looking to do in our time together. What would you like to get from this meeting; what were you hoping to accomplish today?"** — with the bolder variant, depending on how hard the meeting was to secure: "Why did you invite me in?" or "How come you agreed to visit with me?"
3. **Clean up their issues** (existing customers only). "Dealing with the junk up front." A customer still seething about a missed shipment or unfulfilled promise cannot hear you until you clear the air.
4. **Deliver the power statement** (three minutes maximum). This is where the full-form power statement delivers maximum impact, with neither projector nor brochure. Watch the customer's physical reactions during the client-issues segment for a wince, a nod, a hand gesture, or sudden note-taking. If they interrupt with a clarifying question, answer briefly and **stop yourself from launching into a full sales pitch** — "Be patient and keep your powder dry" — and note what aroused their interest so you can explore it in discovery. At this point you are seven to fifteen minutes in and in complete control.
   The answer to reps who fear the prospect will resent questions: do not view the prospect as a witness facing cross-examination, view yourself as the doctor and the prospect as the patient. You would not trust a physician who spent an hour telling you how great he was and then wrote a prescription. The power statement is what buys the credibility to examine: the doctor sits down, makes small talk, gives a three-minute overview of her education, specialty, and philosophy of care, and only then examines, diagnoses, and proposes.
5. **Ask probing questions (discovery).** "More selling can be accomplished by asking a series of great questions than by executing a highly polished presentation." Four categories:
   - **Personal questions** — what matters to this person individually: short- and long-term goals, how they are being evaluated, what results they are working to achieve, how you could make their life easier, how and when they get bonuses or promoted.
   - **Strategic and directional questions** — the prospect's macro world: what is happening in their space, who they are up against, industry trends working for or against them, corporate initiatives or new strategies catalyzing change, pressure to grow or to reduce costs. Precall research makes these specific rather than generic, and there is no excuse for a B2B call without having scanned public information, press releases, social media, and anything senior executives or your contact have said or written.
   - **Specific issue- and opportunity-seeking questions** — built by converting each client-issue bullet from the power statement into an open-ended question. Then: **listen to the answers.** When you hit a real issue, do not move on — camp out, ask follow-ups, ask how they have attempted to conquer it and why that has not worked, and ask the consequences of not solving it. "When you find a scab, pick at it. Keep probing. And when it starts to bleed a little, pick harder." Also circle back to whatever they reacted to during the power statement and ask why that piqued their interest. When a prospect asks a question, the simplest protection against heading down an unproductive path is "That's a good question. Why'd you ask that?"
   - **Sales process questions** — the information needed to advance the opportunity and the reason deals go dark. The eight factors to discover: decision authority and decision influencers; timelines; available dollars or budget; willingness to make a change; stage in the buying process; decision criteria (how the decision will be made, not who makes it); alternative options; competition. Great salespeople tackle all of these before presenting a proposal, and "sometimes the best way to speed up closing a deal is to slow down the sales process to gain the necessary information." The best-phrased authority question, which Weinberg credits to others: **"Along with yourself, who else really cares about this issue?"** Others: Where are you in the process of evaluating options? Where will the money come from to fund this initiative? How committed are you to making a change, and what is the likelihood you leave your current supplier? Tell me the criteria you'll use to make your decision. What other alternatives are on the table?
6. **Sell.** Now the patience and discipline earn their return. Grab your literature, review charts, share stories of results achieved for other clients, describe possible solutions, provide testimonials. Tailor everything: skip what is not relevant, dwell on what resonates with the buyer's issues, and tie your offerings directly to the needs uncovered **using the buyer's own words.**
7. **Determine fit and seek out objections.** Smile, nod gently, and say: "Based on our conversation and what we've shared with each other, it looks like we might be a fit to help you." Pause. If no response, "What do you think?" Do not fill the silence or resell. Two takeaways are required: confirmation that there is a genuine fit, and potential obstacles and objections flushed into the open. Weinberg treats surfacing objections while face-to-face as the best thing that can happen, because you can address them on the spot or strategize around them. If your gut says something is amiss and the contact will not say it, ask directly: "I get the feeling you have concerns or see obstacles about going forward. Tell me what you're thinking. I'd rather hear your concerns now than pretend they don't exist."
8. **Define and schedule the next step.** The question every salesperson must be able to answer after every call is "What's the next step?" No matter how well the meeting went, "if you left without defining, agreeing to, and scheduling the next step, then you failed." One question bridges it: **"What do you suggest as an appropriate next step?"** — which also lets the prospect direct and stay in control, and relieves the pressure traditional closing techniques create. The preferred move is to propose steps for both sides: "How about if I do EFG, and you pull together JKL?" Mutual commitment. Suggesting the step earns only partial credit; you must **schedule** it: open the calendar, pick the date, repeat it, book it, shake on it. Finally, do not fall all over yourself thanking them for their time. Be thankful and respectful, but "act like you've been here before," because you delivered real value and the prospect is the one with the pain.

## 10. Preventing the Buyer's Reflex Resistance

Buyers, especially prospects, have an automatic, almost instinctive negative reflex reaction to salespeople, and everyone does it, including people who sell for a living. The words non-sales colleagues use to describe salespeople collectively: self-absorbed, manipulative, verbose, unreliable, high ego, pest, time waster, disconnected from my reality, poor listener. **"It's not your fault, but it is your problem."** The waters were poisoned before you got here, and you still have to sell against it.

The structural consequence is that **every aspect of the sales attack needs a second objective** alongside its primary one. Four questions to run every interaction through:
- How will the prospect or customer feel about you and your company following this interaction?
- What message are you sending about the experience the customer will have working with you?
- How can you inform the customer that you understand this interaction is not about you?
- What can be communicated to demonstrate you are worthy of the prospect's time and driven to bring maximum value?

Five levers determine whether the shield goes up or stays down:

- **Our beliefs** — if you genuinely believe potential customers will be better off working with you and you have their interests at heart, buyers perceive and reward it. "Buyers detect insincerity as quickly as they can smell cheap cologne." Representing yourself as a problem solver who exists to bring value is what sets you apart, and wise buyers are much slower to resist a salesperson whose clear motivation is to help them succeed.
- **Our sound** — the prototypical sales tone brings out the caution flag immediately. Lose the sales voice; practice a normal, friendly, casual, confident voice.
- **Our view of the prospect** — the study Weinberg ran comparing top and bottom performers found that struggling reps held two defeatist assumptions: that the leads were not serious prospects ("tire-kickers," "window-shoppers"), and that the leads were merely price shoppers hunting the cheapest deal. Top performers entered each conversation assuming a legitimate opportunity and assuming the lead reached out because they were stuck and looking for help.
- **Our feelings toward the prospect** — top performers were not only optimistic about winning but had warm, positive emotions about the prospect ("I love these leads"). Struggling reps harbored anger and frustration toward prospects before ever speaking with them. Salespeople are poor poker players and telegraph those emotions.
- **Our words** — buyers screen every word through "is this about them or about us," and the moment they discern self-focus the resistance goes up. **The single best way to slow or prevent reflex resistance is to lead with client issues whenever we communicate** — buyers will not resist messaging that begins with items top of mind for them, which "causes them to replace their mental 'no soliciting' sign with a bright neon welcome sign."

## 11. Presenting Is Not Selling

Weinberg's position is categorical: **by sales law, a first meeting cannot be a presentation. Ever.** Presenting is not a synonym for selling; it is only a part of the selling process. The lessons he draws from the boardroom disaster that formed this view: never cede control of the selling process to someone else regardless of who owns the account relationship; when something doesn't feel right it is because it isn't right, so speak up and ask hard questions rather than going along for the ride; and even when asked to present, turn it into a dialogue by asking questions throughout, especially at the beginning.

PowerPoint itself is not the problem; what people do with it is. It enables monologues about our company, our processes, our people, and our perfect solution. The corrective is a **four-slide recipe** that forces early focus where it belongs:

- **Slide 1: Title slide.**
- **Slide 2: Suggested agenda.**
- **Slide 3: "Companies turn to [Your Company] when…"** — three to five relevant bullets lifted from the client-issues section of the power statement.
- **Slide 4: "Our understanding of your situation…"** — several items learned from discovery work to this point.

Slide 4 is the critical one, because it transitions from the generic statements of slide 3 to prospect-specific issues, demonstrates the discovery work completed, and bears witness to the investment made in understanding their business. At slide 4, convert the monologue into a dialogue: tell the audience you would like to stop there, ask where you nailed it and what you misunderstood, ask the most senior person in the room to rank or prioritize the items, and if time allows dig deeper into consequences. Everything learned there lets you customize the balance of the presentation on the fly.

**The gating rule:** if you cannot create slide 4 because you lack the information, you have no business making a presentation, even if the customer asked for one. "The fact that the prospect requested or even insisted you make a presentation doesn't make it right or smart for you to comply. The goal is to win business, not simply to follow the directions of a prospect." So **insist on a meeting before the requested presentation.** Assert that you have a process for delivering maximum value that entails several steps before presenting, and that if they want your best they must help provide what you need.

**When the prospect will not meet beforehand** (rigid RFP protocols, agency pitches, industry norms), the workaround is to convert the first portion of the presentation into the discovery session you never got to conduct. The prescribed move: after introductions and rapport building, calmly and confidently lay out an agenda that names how much content you brought, states that in order to present the most relevant material you would like the first fifteen minutes to understand why you were invited in and what they are facing (threats, opportunities, market shifts, and if they are willing, their experience with the current or previous provider), then gives the senior leader three minutes on the company and why clients select it, then asks questions and decides on the fly what makes the most sense to present. Framed to the prospect, this even becomes a feature: they get to watch the pitch being built in real time.

**Break the mold to set yourself apart.** Compliance feels safer and is not. "There's nothing safe about going head-to-head against four competitors who are all using the same strategy and trying hard to differentiate themselves with slight nuances in their presentations." The illustrative contrast is the kitchen remodel: three contractors arrived with identical binders, brochures, and recommendation letters and measured the room; Jerry nosed around the pantries, asked about the children and a future dog, noticed an oddly boxed soffit, went to his truck for a saw, mirror, and flashlight, asked how likely the project was, and cut a hole in the wall because he had to know what was in there to craft the solution. Then he sat down, opened no binder, and asked, "What is your vision for this room?" He won on the spot. It is the visual demonstration of why discovery must precede presentation.

## 12. Planning and Executing the Attack

The third component of the New Sales Driver, and the one where the framework either produces revenue or stays academic. "Working out in the preseason and studying the playbook won't put points on the scoreboard."

**Time blocking** is the named mechanism: making appointments with yourself for activities that are priorities, so that blocks of time are dedicated to essential initiatives and you get to your calendar before anyone else can fill it. Prescriptions: a minimum of **ninety minutes per block** (it takes time to settle in and find the groove) and roughly **three hours as the upper limit** (focus degrades and other duties come calling). Frequency scales to goals: for a rep doing almost no prospecting, **two two-hour blocks per week** can produce exponentially better results; for a rep with significant account acquisition goals, **eight or nine two-hour sessions per week** is conceivable and still amounts to only about one-third of working hours.

**Three keys to time blocking:**
1. **Put the blocks in your calendar** — not just in your mind.
2. **Keep the appointment with yourself** — treat it as sacrosanct, as important as any other slot. Weinberg's Seinfeld analogy: anybody can take the reservation; holding it is the important part.
3. **Remain on task throughout the block** — stay in outbound mode. Do not check e-mail; if you must keep it open because you are prospecting with it, do not look at the inbox, because one message from a friend or important client derails the whole block. Phone on do-not-disturb, no inbound calls. "You are not a heart surgeon. No one is going to die on the table because you can't be located for an hour or two."

**The math works; work the math.** Sales is a numbers game, and "most top-performing salespeople are the most active salespeople." The most effective attack is a high-frequency attack. Every business should know, from a relevant sample of data, how much activity at the beginning of the process it takes to produce a closed sale. A generic seven-stage process: (1) prospect targeted, (2) initial conversation, (3) meaningful dialogue / first meeting, (4) needs identified, fit established, mutual agreement to move forward, (5) second meeting / key data received, (6) presentation and/or proposal delivered, (7) deal closed and won. Working backward from the goal with illustrative conversion rates (win one of three proposals; three of four fits agree to review a proposal; two-thirds of first meetings advance; half of first conversations produce a meeting), a goal of **12 closed deals** requires **36 proposals delivered**, which requires **48 opportunities reaching stages 4 and 5**, which requires **72 initial meetings**, which requires **144 initial proactive conversations.** The dual conclusion: you must put a certain volume into the top of the funnel, and improving proficiency with the weapons improves the conversion rates and makes the whole math chain more efficient. "Generally, we cannot control the result, but we certainly can control the volume of activity."

**The individual business plan**, written annually, with five required components:
1. **Goals (what you are going to achieve)** — total revenue dollars; gross profit dollars or percentage; number of new accounts acquired; net new business dollars; revenue from existing accounts; product category, cross-sell, or new product goals; major goals for specific named accounts; personal income goals.
2. **Strategies (how you are going to do it)** — which existing accounts get extra energy (lists of largest and most growable focus accounts); which geographies, verticals, or channels you will pursue, with the focused finite target prospect list attached; major cross-sell opportunities inside existing accounts; how you will approach new accounts, get in the door, and move opportunities forward; what other strategies or tools (team selling, events, referral sources, social media) you will use.
3. **Actions (specific sales activities you will commit to)** — hours time-blocked and committed to proactive new business development; number of outbound calls, meaningful conversations, face-to-face sales calls; number of trips to key markets, major presentations, facility tours or client visits; number of proposals delivered and dollars proposed.
4. **Obstacles (what's in the way)** — put them on the table so they can be addressed and help can be requested: product knowledge, sales support, lack of technology, distractions or the company's anti-sales department, current account management and customer service burdens, personal health or family issues.
5. **Personal development (how you plan to grow this year)** — seminars and conferences, books and blogs, specific industry training, writing or social media or association involvement, peer coaching or seeking a mentor.

Plans should be presented, ideally to the whole team in thirty-minute slots (twenty to present, ten for feedback and Q&A), because that creates instant accountability, spreads best practices, and lets others stop a plan that is bound to fail. The value comes from regular review against one question: "Am I doing what I said I needed to do in order to succeed?" and from checking alignment: "There should be a lot of alignment between what we see in your plan and what we see on your calendar."

**Preplan travel.** Block time for prospecting and also prebook travel to target cities. Advance fares are cheaper, but the real point is commitment: nothing demonstrates it better than buying a ticket for a trip four or six weeks out before a single meeting is scheduled, which forces the phone time to fill it. Weinberg's preference for Southwest ("my Sales Force One") is functional rather than sentimental: no change fees means trips can be rescheduled freely, low fares mean a team member can be brought along for a second meeting, and reasonable last-minute pricing means you can answer a prospect's request with "We can be there tomorrow or Friday. Which works better for you?"

**A balanced effort produces a balanced pipeline.** Divide prospects and opportunities into three categories: **Targeted** (committed to proactively pursuing and moving to Active), **Active** (sales dialogue started, potential opportunity seen, process must continue to move them to Hot), and **Hot** (real opportunities emerged, urgency exists, proposal delivered or imminent). The purpose is not forecasting accuracy but visibility into the health, status, and balance of the pipeline, and a framework for segmenting time.

**A healthy pipeline has three characteristics:** it is **full** (opportunities aplenty, no single deal makes or breaks the quarter or year), it **shows movement** (accounts progress from stage to stage, most deals are not stale and growing mold), and it is **balanced** (accounts and opportunities appear in every segment). Imbalance in either direction signals trouble: mostly Targeted means little effort starting relationships or poor work uncovering opportunities, and a poor short-term outlook; mostly Hot with little Active means targeted accounts are not being worked and things look grim after the current deals resolve.

**The cast-in-stone formula** for maintaining that balance is to intentionally divide time into thirds: **one-third working hot opportunities, one-third working currently active deals, and one-third in proactive pursuit of targeted prospects that are not yet active.** Reps who genuinely attempt it come back with the same admission: they had no idea how heavily they were overinvesting in hot opportunities and how little time they were actually spending creating new opportunities with targeted accounts. The reason the discipline is needed is the same reason the whole framework exists: salespeople gravitate naturally toward the hot end of the pipeline, and no one defaults to prospecting mode.

**There is no magic bullet.** Weinberg's answer to reps asking for the most powerful closing technique or the secret sauce is to redirect to the basics with his own questions: show me your actual target account list and the strategic thinking behind it; tell me how much time and effort you have invested working that list; let me hear your sales story; how much time are you spending proactively calling prospects; outline your structure for conducting sales calls; how much of your time goes to managing existing customers versus proactive selling; show me your pipeline report and how you invest time across stages; pull out your written business plan and tell me your strategies for opening new accounts and which key sales activities you have committed to. "The resulting discomfort from my blunt questions speaks volumes as to why so many salespeople underperform... They want help running trick plays but are not interested in the basics, like blocking and tackling."
