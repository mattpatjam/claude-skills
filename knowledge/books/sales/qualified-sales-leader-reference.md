# The Qualified Sales Leader — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing negotiation/closing/sales processes, or advising on deal strategy.*
*Book: "The Qualified Sales Leader: Proven Lessons from a Five Time CRO" — John McMahon*

---

## 1. The Fox and the Hedgehog: Mastering the Basics

McMahon opens with a parable (Sly Smartass the fox versus Billy Basics the hedgehog) to make the point that simplicity beats cleverness: a sales force that has mastered a small set of fundamentals will consistently beat one chasing constant new tricks. Before any process or qualification method can work, a sales leader needs ten foundational habits in place.

- **Common vocabulary.** Teams fail to communicate not because they disagree, but because they use the same words (Champion, coach, Economic Buyer) with different private definitions. Fix this first, or nothing else compounds.
- **Listening.** People listen/comprehend at ~250 words per minute but speak at ~150 wpm. That gap breeds impatience — leaders start composing their next question before the rep finishes. Listen to understand, not to reply.
- **Be HERE.** Full presence. No phone, no rehearsing your response, no thinking about your own forecast while a rep is talking. "Live in the story" the rep is telling — picture the account, the buyer, the stage.
- **Use intuition.** Treat gut instinct as a second information-processing channel alongside analysis. Triangulate between what the rep says, what your head concludes, and what your gut senses.
- **Self-awareness.** Leaders must audit which of their own traits help and which sabotage their people's willingness to engage honestly.
- **Transformational, not transactional, mindset.** A transactional manager trades punishment-avoidance for compliance; reps tell the manager what they want to hear. A transformational leader trades coaching for honest account information — reps bring bad news early ("bad news can't wait") because they trust the exchange. The test of success: the rep becomes self-sufficient enough to no longer need you.
- **Don't be a glorified scorekeeper.** Managing only by activity KPIs (calls, emails, POCs) without ever analyzing *why* deals do or don't advance is scorekeeping, not leadership. The corrective loop is **Inspire → Coach and Develop → Inspect** — in that order, repeated indefinitely, always inspecting for the *why*.
- **Intentional outcomes.** Never walk into a meeting without a defined desired outcome for both the rep and yourself. Intention precedes value.
- **Keep it simple.** If your grandmother wouldn't understand your explanation, it's too complicated. The leader's job is to make complex things simple for both reps and customers.
- **Urgent curiosity.** Illustrated by Carlo Carpanelli, the "greatest salesperson" — his differentiator wasn't intelligence or instinct (McMahon had hired plenty of those), it was an urgent need to get answers *now* rather than schedule a follow-up. Time is the enemy: a 90-day quarter nets out to roughly 61 actual selling days once weekends, holidays, and vacation are subtracted (12 weeks × 6 weekend/holiday-adjusted days ≈ 63, minus ~2 vacation days = 61). That scarcity is why urgency in information-gathering matters as much as urgency in the customer's buying decision.

**Golf-coach principle for skill development:** never hand a rep five things to fix at once. Isolate one skill, drill it to consistency, then add the next. Trying to fix everything simultaneously convinces the rep they're not cut out for the job.

## 2. The B2B Sales Process: Six Stages and Gates

A sales process is the map; a qualification method (see MEDDPICC, Section 11) is the GPS that tells you where you actually are on that map. McMahon's reference six-stage B2B process (adapt stage count/order to your own buying process, but keep the discipline):

1. **Discovery** — identify pain, problem, or initiative; identify the players.
2. **Scoping** — quantify and implicate the pain; build preliminary cost justification and POV plan.
3. **Economic Buyer Meeting** — the Go/No-Go gate; confirm priority and funding.
4. **Validation Event** (POV/POC) — prove the solution delivers the promised value.
5. **Business Case and Final Proposal** — formal value-realization document presented to the EB.
6. **Negotiate and Close** — procurement, legal, paper process.

**Who benefits from a defined process, and why:**
- Reps get a repeatable map: higher win rates, larger average deal size, faster velocity.
- Managers get consistency and a common language to diagnose where a rep is stuck (knowledge gap vs. skill gap).
- Customers get clarity on why the product differs from alternatives and what value it delivers.
- Executives get resource efficiency, better win/loss visibility, and — critically — a materially more accurate forecast.

**Exit criteria ("gates").** Every stage needs a tangible, customer-verifiable event that must occur before a deal can advance — not a rep's subjective judgment call. Example gate for Discovery: a confirmation email to the customer recapping the pain, the players, the desired business outcome, and the next scheduled meeting. Gates can carry their own KPIs (percentage of deals transitioning stage-to-stage, average time-in-stage).

**Why skipping steps compounds catastrophically at scale.** If five reps on one team each misrepresent a single $100K deal's stage, that's a $500K distortion. Roll that up through three managers and fifteen reps and it's $1.5M. At the CRO/CEO level the distortion compounds further — this is the direct mechanical cause of "surprise" misses on the quarterly number.

**The core logic chain the book keeps returning to:**
- Find pain → pain finds Champions → Champions get you to the Economic Buyer → the EB has access to major funds → you sell big deals.
- Quantify the pain → drives higher price points. Implicate the pain (show the negative consequences of inaction) → drives urgency.
- Run the chain in reverse and you get the failure mode: no discovered pain → small or no opportunity; no quantified pain → no cost justification; no implicated pain → no urgency; no Champion → no EB access → no access to major funds → small deals or no deal.

## 3. Discovery: Finding Pain (Not Selling)

Discovery is investigation, not pitching. The rep resists talking about the product in order to fully understand and confirm the customer's pain first.

**What to establish during Discovery:**
- The pain, problem, or corporate initiative driving potential need.
- The customer's current ("as-is") process and environment.
- Their desired business outcome.
- The key players: potential coaches, Champions, gatekeepers, technical users, and "enemies" (the competition's Champion).

**Representative Discovery questions:** Why do you think this is a problem? Why does this happen? Why remedy it now? How often does it occur? Have you tried to fix it before, and how? What company measure does this affect? Who is held accountable for that measure? Walk me through your current process. Who else is involved? What else does it depend on? What's the desired business outcome?

**The art of Discovery — "skate to the open ice."** Discovery is a give-and-take, not an interrogation. Take the space the customer gives you to extract information, but sense when they want something back from you (a summary, a relevant capability, a proof point) and give it before pushing further.

**Immersion loop.** A rep can only be fully present in the conversation if they already have cold command of: the customer's use case, typical pains in that use case, their open-ended Discovery questions, their own product's unique differentiators, how those differentiators solve the pain, the typical quantifiable value of the solution, and customer success stories (before/after). Without that base knowledge, the rep is thinking when they should be listening — the conversation becomes mechanical. Full immersion is a constant four-step loop: **Question → Listen (to understand) → Confirm (verify understanding) → Feel (sense the customer's response).**

**Diagnostic principle:** if a rep cannot answer the "Three WHYs" (Section 11) for a deal, that deal has not exited Discovery — regardless of what stage it shows in the CRM.

## 4. Scoping: Quantify and Implicate the Pain

Scoping takes the pain surfaced in Discovery and turns it into numbers and consequences.

**Two outputs Scoping must produce:**
- Quantified metrics of the "as-is" state and preliminary metrics of the "to-be" state (this feeds the cost justification, which in turn anchors price during negotiation).
- Implication of the pain — the negative consequences of *not* solving it now. Quantifying pain justifies price; implicating pain creates urgency. Skip implication and the deal has no reason to close this quarter rather than some future quarter — "time kills all deals" without urgency.

**POC → POV mindset shift.** McMahon insists reps stop saying "Proof of Concept" and start saying "Proof of Value." The product already works — a POC pointlessly re-litigates that. What must be proven is that the product's capabilities deliver quantified business value in this specific customer's use case.

**The three parts of the Scoping journey:**
- *Scoping itself* — the method for understanding and quantifying pain, checking product-pain alignment.
- *Go/No-Go meeting with the Economic Buyer* — confirms the pain is a genuine priority before investing further.
- *The POV* — the criteria, required capabilities, and timeframe for the validation event.

**Three documents Scoping should generate:** a cost justification (as-is/to-be metrics), a POV plan tied to that justification, and a preliminary pricing proposal.

**Timing matters.** This information-gathering can only happen *early*. Wait too long and a "dynamic shift" occurs: once the customer can already see how your product works, they stop sharing pain and process metrics and only want pricing — you've lost your leverage to build a value case.

**"Box Up" story — implication questions in practice.** An insurance rep's cold call sequence: Discovery questions (kids? mortgage? spouse working?) → Quantification questions (college cost? mortgage size? monthly spend?) → Implication questions ("If you 'box up' tomorrow, would your wife have to go back to work? Sell the house? Who picks up the kids?"). The implication questions convert an abstract pain into felt fear of a negative future state — that fear is what changes buying priority from "someday" to "now."

**"Above the noise" — targeting pain that matters at the top.** A CIO described himself as a forest ranger, not a firefighter: he can't fight every fire, only the ones that threaten the whole forest. Most vendors solve fires the company can live with. To sell high, find pain tied to revenue, profitability, or risk at the level the buyer actually operates — the specific words change by level, but everything eventually has to translate to those three measures.

**Common enterprise pain categories to target:** regulatory non-compliance (e.g., PCI, HIPAA, GDPR), competitive pressure forcing infrastructure change, security exposure from new attack surfaces (cloud, mobile, remote work, AI), productivity/cost/quality drag from broken processes, and reputational risk from customer-facing failures.

## 5. Building the Ideal Customer Profile (ICP)

Targeting matters because propensity to buy and sales complexity both vary sharply across an otherwise-qualified customer list. McMahon's build sequence for an ICP:

1. List your product's unique and *defensible* differentiators (provably unique head-to-head, not just marketed as unique).
2. Map the specific pain points those differentiators solve.
3. Map those pain points to specific customer use cases.
4. Quantify the business benefit of solving each pain in each use case (revenue, cost, risk, productivity terms).
5. List the negative consequences of the customer *not* solving the pain.
6. Identify which companies/industries actually have those use cases.
7. Identify the specific buyer personas who own those use cases (title, boss, budget, job measures, daily operating rhythm).
8. Prioritize companies/industries by the size of the customer value your solution would create.

**Propensity to buy** is a second filter layered on top of ICP fit: short product lifecycles, "all in" on the target technology stack (e.g., cloud), a track record of hiring top talent, appetite for leading-edge technology, pending regulatory deadlines, and other "impending events" all raise propensity. A company can score high on ICP fit (big pain, strong product alignment) yet still be deprioritized if propensity to buy is low — though propensity should be assessed at the division/team level, not just the monolithic company level (a slow-moving bank as a whole may still have one fast-moving digital team that fits).

**Sales complexity** is the third filter: multi-stakeholder, multi-level, multi-department, multi-location decisions; difficulty reaching the C-suite; long POV/evaluation requirements; heavy cost-justification hurdles; and lengthy legal/procurement processes all raise the cost and duration of winning a deal. High complexity plus low propensity should push an otherwise-attractive account down the priority list, especially for a smaller sales force.

## 6. Champions: Finding Them, Earning Trust, Testing Them

**Definition.** A Champion has *influence* in the account, which gives them access to the Economic Buyer. Influence is independent of formal position — this is the central distinction the book hammers.

**Org chart vs. power chart.** An org chart shows reporting lines and formal authority. It does not show who actually has influence over decisions. Build a 2x2 of Influence (x-axis) vs. Authority (y-axis):
- **Influence + Authority (IA):** business acumen plus position — your likely *business Champion*.
- **Influence, no Authority (INA):** deep domain expertise without title — your likely *technical Champion*. Business Champions often lean on an INA to validate a solution technically.
- **Authority, no Influence (ANI):** positional but not persuasive — may be a useful coach, never a Champion.
- **No Influence, no Authority (NINA):** can still be a coach or information source, never a Champion.

**Where to look for Champions:** who's working on critical projects; who was recently promoted; who's been assigned new initiatives; who's personally accountable for key decisions; who made the last major departmental purchase; who the C-suite consults on critical calls; whose statements move a room; who asks the sharpest probing questions in meetings; who others name as most influential. Ops staff attached to a C-level executive (Sales Ops, Enablement, Finance Ops, etc.) often know exactly who influences that executive's decisions.

**"Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence."** If you haven't found a Champion yet, that does not mean one doesn't exist in the account — it means you haven't found them yet. Keep looking, including in presentations and meetings where an unexpected person asks unusually sharp questions.

**Why would anyone be your Champion?** Champions attach themselves to solving a notable business problem because it delivers a personal win: recognition (being seen as the problem-solver), control (over the solution and its users), productivity gains for their department, promotion, or status. Selling to a potential Champion means answering their unspoken "what's in it for me."

**Earning trust before testing.** Champions are politically shrewd — they will not align with a rep over a small pain, and they will not attach themselves to an unprepared salesperson, because their own reputation is on the line if the deal fails. Every rep starts as "just another salesperson" facing a default of no trust. Trust is built through a repeating loop: **Ask** (prepared Discovery/Scoping questions) → **Message** (align the solution to the pain and company fit) → **Confirm** (verify or realign the message). A Champion evaluates trust across three dimensions: business value of the solution (does the future gain outweigh the current pain), performance value of the vendor company (will it support implementation, adoption, optimization), and experience value of the salesperson (has working with this person built a real partnership).

**Educating, not selling, once a Champion is identified.** Once a rep believes they have a Champion, "selling ceases to be selling — selling becomes educating." Brief the Champion on competitive traps, common objections, how to justify the purchase internally, and arrange reference calls and executive meetings. Role-play the Champion's own meeting with the Economic Buyer before it happens — this both prepares them and further tests whether they're a real Champion (do they show up with genuinely prepared material, or defer everything back to you?).

**Testing a Champion — the questions and the asks.** Test knowledge: how are purchases made at this company; what's the typical evaluation process; who made recent major purchases; can they map the as-is process; can they explain why the pain is "major"; can they explain the implications of not solving it; can they compare your value to the competition's; do they know what happened in competitor meetings. Test through action: ask them to arrange a demo to another department, introduce another stakeholder, share evaluation documents, connect you with a reference customer, sponsor an EB meeting, help build the POV criteria, introduce you to Legal/Procurement, or help build the cost justification. **Warning:** testing before you've earned trust and done the educating "burns" a potential Champion — sequence matters.

**The "good guy / bad guy" test with the boss.** Tell the potential Champion your own boss thinks you're wasting time on this account and thinks you should walk away — then ask, "What should I tell my boss?" Their answer reveals whether they're really invested.

**Stealth Champions and the professional walk-away.** Some Champions — often for political reasons, sometimes because they're the Economic Buyer themselves — will never explicitly endorse you, but quietly shape criteria and process in your favor. If you suspect a stealth Champion exists but can't get confirmation (e.g., unfavorable POV criteria in a committee-run RFP with no identifiable Champion), a professional, respectful "walk away" letter tests it directly: if there is no stealth support, you lose nothing you weren't already going to lose; if there is, the committee will reach out within hours asking you to stay — and you re-enter with real leverage to reshape criteria.

**How you know you actually have a Champion (not a coach):**
- Deal status moves from *unpredictable* to *predictable* — no surprises, events unfold as discussed.
- The relationship is *collaborative* — critical information flows both directions, you jointly build decision criteria and process.
- The Champion is *proactive* — they introduce you to other players unprompted, bring you competitive intel, offer reference calls, request executive meetings. Their actions show vested interest, not just words.

**Coach vs. Champion — the operational difference.** A coach provides information but never takes action on your behalf. A Champion takes action. A coach speaks in technical terms and sees the problem through "small eyes of product fit" (this solves my narrow issue). A Champion speaks in business terms and sees it through "large eyes of business fit" (this solves a problem the business cares about). A coach cannot become a Champion (no influence, no EB access) — but a Champion can also function as a coach.

**Even Economic Buyers need a Champion.** EBs rarely want to be your only, visible advocate — for political reasons and because they need someone accountable under them for a successful implementation. Every EB needs someone to make the implementation work, catch problems early, serve as a reference, help quantify value for renewal/upsell, and defend the account against competitive attack and churn.

**Champion strength vs. the competition's Champion.** Always assess whether your Champion would win an internal argument against the competitor's Champion. If not, find additional or higher-level Champions to counterbalance.

## 7. The Economic Buyer

**Definition.** The Economic Buyer (EB) is the person with *discretionary use of the funds* — they can reallocate budget across projects, and their approval makes every other signature a rubber stamp. This is a precise definition; "the person with the budget," "the CFO," and "the finance person who approves it" are all imprecise substitutes that different reps and managers will interpret differently.

**The EB meeting is the Go/No-Go gate of the sales process.** A successful meeting (priority and funds confirmed) means you're "over the wall" and can proceed. An unsuccessful one means the process is effectively over — the EB has discretion and has decided your product isn't a priority worth reallocating budget for.

**Preparation is what makes the meeting feel confirmatory rather than terrifying.** Four practices reduce EB-meeting anxiety: (1) do the homework — annual reports, 10-Ks, analyst calls, so you never waste the EB's time re-explaining what they already know; (2) act as a business partner — surface a business issue they *hadn't* considered, not one they already have handled; (3) position the solution against corporate and personal job measures (revenue, cost, risk at the company level; time-to-market, productivity, quality at their personal level); (4) speak in the EB's own business terms, which may differ from the terms that resonated with your Champion.

**Standard EB meeting agenda:** summary of Discovery/Scoping findings; current (as-is) state; negative consequences of that state; proposed (to-be) state; benefits of the future state; required capabilities to get there; customer success stories with quantified before/after; how your solution maps to the criteria; confirmation of remaining decision-process steps.

**The four question categories EBs almost always ask:**
- *Much-ness* — how much does it cost, cost to implement, and how much will we save?
- *Soon-ness* — how soon can it be implemented, how soon is ROI realized?
- *Sure-ness* — how sure are you it will work in our environment, how sure is the cost justification, which reference customer can confirm this?
- *Easy-ness* — how easy is implementation, how easy is it for our people to learn?

**One closing question to always ask the EB before leaving:** "Would it be okay to call you under only two conditions — if our final business case is materially different from the preliminary one after the validation event, or if anyone tries to significantly change our agreed validation criteria?" This pre-authorizes a comeback path and puts competitors on notice not to tamper with the criteria.

**A big-lens story worth remembering:** a Champion may justify a purchase through their own functional lens (e.g., engineering productivity) while the actual corporate EB justifies the same purchase through an entirely different, larger lens (e.g., production scrap costing the equivalent of two idle plants). The higher up the buying chain, the bigger and more consolidated the lens — always ask what larger business measure the EB personally cares about, which may not be the measure your Champion cares about.

## 8. The Tree: Why You Must Sell High, Early

An extended metaphor from top rep Carlo Carpanelli: think of any organization as a tree. Roots and trunk have little money; branches have some; only the very top of the tree controls the big money. Most reps enter at the trunk, work out onto low branches, and only try to climb once they've already been "boxed in" — by an insecure low-level manager, the competitor's Champion, or an installed-base Champion protecting their turf.

**The trap, step by step:** the rep gets pulled into a POV under criteria the competition already shaped, invests real time, then — after losing — appeals upward (their manager, CEO, CRO calls the EB). But it's too late: the EB simply asks whether the rep agreed to the POV criteria going in. If yes, the answer is "it sounds like you got outsold." The rep's own presentation to lower-level stakeholders, ironically, only better equipped those stakeholders to justify the competitor's win.

**The fix:** start at the top of the tree, early, before criteria are set — not after you've already lost control. And once at the top, speak the language spoken there: revenue, cost, margin, productivity, risk, time-to-market, market share — not product features. Speaking product language at a high level gets you "relegated to the level you sound like."

This ties directly back to Champion strength (Section 6) and cost-justification urgency (Section 4): the earlier and higher you establish pain, quantify it, and attach a Champion, the less exposed you are to a competitor who climbed the tree first.

## 9. The Validation Event (Proof of Value)

**What it is.** The natural conclusion of the sales process — you either win it (get the order) or lose it (don't). For the customer, it validates that your solution solves their problem. For the rep, done correctly, it should be a "prelude to an order": you've already secured EB confirmation that the winner of the validation gets a purchase order, subject only to legal review and pricing.

**What a Validation Event is NOT:**
- An event held early in the sales process.
- A time to educate the customer on your product for the first time.
- An opportunity to start Discovery.
- A chance to find a Champion.
- A time to "make friends."

**Prerequisites before running one:** a confirmed Champion; that Champion has helped shape the validation criteria and scoring; a cost justification built from real Discovery/Scoping findings where gain clearly exceeds pain; and EB confirmation of priority, budget, authority, timing, and the remaining process steps.

**The $1,000-wager framing.** McMahon uses an analogy: would you wager $1,000 on a sporting competition without first asking what the events are, how they're scored, and whether they play to your strengths? A validation event without pre-agreed, favorably weighted criteria is exactly that bad a bet.

**During the event:** reconfirm as-is/to-be metrics, adjust them if new information surfaces, capture concrete evidence the solution solves the pain, and keep reinforcing the Champion's personal win.

**After the event:** build a summary report of findings and review it face-to-face with the Champion to lock in their buy-in — there should be no ambiguity left in the business case. If results materially changed the original cost justification, build a new one and take it back to the EB — both to justify price and to re-trigger urgency.

## 10. Business Case, Negotiation, and Procurement Tactics

**The Business Case** is the formal, face-to-face value-realization document presented to the EB after the Validation Event — write it as if the *customer* wrote it, not as a vendor pitch. It should include: their corporate objectives and strategic initiatives; the business problem/opportunity and how solving it serves those initiatives; before/after use-case scenarios; how the solution delivers the outcome (people, process, timeframe); and a final cost justification translated fully into the EB's own business terms (specific dollar figures for revenue, margin, risk, time-to-market — never just "increased productivity" in the abstract). This document also becomes the baseline for measuring actual post-implementation success, which later justifies upsell and cross-sell.

**Buyer risk peaks right after the Validation Event** — the EB now faces the real decision and the personal risk of having Championed something that might not deliver. Counter this by reminding them of the validation results, the cost of inaction, comparable customer outcomes, reference-customer conversations, their own personal win, and — critically — a concrete Implementation Plan with dated milestones (project start date, stakeholder training date, resource-reservation date, contract-finalization date working backward from the outcome date). A well-built implementation plan both reduces perceived risk and manufactures additional urgency to sign.

**Why you have negotiating strength at this stage (if you didn't skip steps):** you helped write the decision criteria that any late-arriving competitor would be judged against; you have proof from the Validation Event that your differentiators solve the customer's pain; you set the price anchor with the EB via the cost justification; and you've built real trust with the Champion and EB that a late competitor cannot replicate quickly. Skipping earlier steps is exactly what leaves a rep exposed here — with no Champion or no EB-agreed cost justification, price gets set by the customer's guess at perceived value or by a competitor's lowball, not by your evidence.

**Procurement's job is to separate price from the business value you've already established** — creating an artificial split between "the business side" (who wants your solution) and "the procurement side" (measured on discounting it), and sometimes issuing a "gag order" preventing your Champion/EB from talking to you further. Procurement will also solicit competing quotes late in the process specifically to manufacture negotiating leverage — which is exactly the signal an alert late-arriving competitor uses to realize they need to move fast and undercut on price.

**Named procurement tactics to recognize (not exhaustive, but the book's list):** the "pissed off for no reason" opener; the "take it or leave it" ultimatum; the "you need us more than we need you" logo-discount ask; the "we love you but…" charade; the "no budget" bust; "good guy/bad guy" (procurement vs. their own boss); the "disappearing friend" who goes quiet until the last days of the quarter; the "shame" play (accusing you of price-gouging); the "one more thing" late addition; the "have a heart" economic-hardship appeal; and the "it's over" gambit (a purchase order already attached to their price, take-it-or-leave-it).

**The counter is simple and consistent: remain calm, and remember time is now on your side, not theirs.** You created their urgency during Scoping (implication of pain) and reinforced it via the EB meeting and Implementation Plan dates — every day that passes, *they* face the consequences of inaction, not you. You never have to answer a pricing ultimatum immediately; "let me think about it" or "let me consult my manager" costs you nothing and buys real time, because their business, not yours, is the one without time.

## 11. MEDDPICC: The Full Qualification Framework

**What MEDDPICC is — and isn't.** It is *not* itself a sales process (a common misconception). It is a qualification framework — the GPS layered on top of the sales-process map — usable at any point to (1) rapidly identify whether a real opportunity exists, (2) pinpoint exactly where you are in the process, (3) surface the gap between what's known and unknown, (4) build an action plan to close that gap, and (5) diagnose specific rep knowledge/skill gaps. The letters, and the stage each primarily maps to:

**Metrics** (maps to Scoping) — the quantified customer pain and quantified business benefit of the solution: concrete as-is vs. to-be numbers (e.g., "12 months and 5 people at $300K burdened cost each, down to 2 people and 3 months"). Without quantified metrics there is no defensible cost justification, and the customer alone determines perceived value and the price they'll pay. Qualifying questions: What's the use case? Who owns it? What's the quantified pain? Have as-is/to-be been outlined? What's the quantified business value? Does gain clearly outweigh pain? Has a preliminary cost justification been built and validated with the Champion?

**Economic Buyer** (maps to the EB meeting/Go-No-Go) — see Section 7 in full. Qualifying questions before the meeting: who does the Champion say is the EB; has that been validated with multiple people; what's the agenda; has the Champion pre-briefed the EB? After the meeting: were Discovery/Scoping findings and the Three WHYs confirmed; what's the EB's desired outcome and most-impacted measure; did they agree all vendors test to the same criteria; can you call them if criteria change; do they have authority and will they allocate budget; what's their timeframe and why; were remaining decision-process steps confirmed?

**Decision Criteria** (formulated in Discovery, finalized in Scoping) — the buyer's "shopping list" of required capabilities, against which every vendor will be scored. Whoever helps write the criteria controls the deal. Track direction of change over time: criteria moving toward your differentiators = you're gaining control; criteria moving toward a competitor's differentiators = you're losing it. Qualifying questions: do your capabilities map to their pains; did you help your Champion write the criteria; which of your differentiators are in it; has it changed, when, and in whose favor; who's driving that change; are you prepared to walk the EB through how your solution meets the criteria?

**Decision Process** (formulated in Scoping) — the specific people, events, and dated timeline the customer will use to evaluate and select a vendor (e.g., vendor overviews by a date, demos by a date, POC by a date, cost justifications by a date, vendor selection by a date). Qualifying questions: what are the events and dates; who besides the Champion is involved and have you met them individually; has the process changed, who changed it, was your Champion involved; is your Champion or the competitor's Champion controlling it; are you prepared to confirm it with the EB, including a post-POV EB meeting?

**Paper Process** (the signature/approval process, relevant to Negotiate and Close) — the specific people and durations required to actually produce a purchase order after a decision is made (financial review, budgeting cycle, legal redlining, procurement issuance). Forecasting without knowing this is guesswork — a deal decided with four weeks left in the quarter but a six-week paper process will not close this quarter no matter how "won" it feels. Qualifying questions: does the Champion actually know the approval steps (if not, question whether they're a first-time buyer at this size, or not a real Champion at all); who are the stakeholders and durations at each step; who are the named Legal and Procurement contacts; has their Legal redlined your paperwork yet?

**Identify Pain/Problem/Initiative** (maps to Discovery) — resolved through the **Three WHYs**:
- *Why do they have to buy?* — reveals whether a major pain/initiative was actually identified, whether it's "above the noise," and whether the rep can describe it in business terms.
- *Why do THEY have to buy?* — identifies who in the organization actually owns/is most impacted by the pain (the person with personal skin in the game, not necessarily the most obvious departmental head — McMahon's CAD-selling example shows the "logical" buyer, the CAD Director, had *no* personal pain and every incentive to deflect, while the VP of Engineering, who owned the productivity outcome, was the real target).
- *Why do I have to buy from us?* — the beginning of decision criteria: do your differentiators map to their specific pain, i.e., is the customer "buying what you're selling"?
- (A closely related fourth question threads through Scoping) *Why do I have to buy now?* — tests whether the rep understands and has implicated the consequences of inaction, and can name the specific company measure that suffers.

**Paper process, Identify, and Metrics all gate whether a deal has genuinely exited Discovery/Scoping** — if a rep can't answer the Three WHYs, the deal is still in Discovery no matter what the CRM stage field says.

**Champion** (developed through Scoping onward) — see Section 6 in full. Qualifying questions: did the rep develop the criteria/process with the Champion or just get told them; did the Champion help build the before/after scenarios, cost justification, criteria, process, and EB-meeting agenda; where does the Champion sit on the IA/INA/ANI/NINA power chart, and how do you know; what's their personal win; how have they been educated, and how have they been tested; who do they say the EB is; have they scheduled the EB meeting?

**Competition** (ongoing, but especially acute pre-close) — know who else is in the account, when they entered, who their Champion is and how strong, and treat any change in decision criteria or process as a red flag that the competitor's Champion may be gaining influence. Counter by keeping your Champion stronger (find multiple Champions, high in the tree) and by arming your Champion with fear/uncertainty/doubt counters to the competitor's known weaknesses.

**BANT vs. MEDDPICC.** BANT (Budget, Authority, Need, Timing) is a real and useful subset — it maps cleanly onto the Economic Buyer conversation specifically. But it does not address stakeholder development (no Champion concept), doesn't address the competition, and doesn't map to a full sales process stage-by-stage the way MEDDPICC does — so it can confirm an EB conversation went well without ever revealing whether the rep is actually in control of the account.

## 12. Recruiting and Developing A-Players

**Recruiting is the single highest-leverage thing a sales leader does.** McMahon's deliberately blunt framing: a team of C-players led by a manager who does everything else (onboarding, training, process) perfectly will still struggle to be the best team. A team of A-players led by a manager who does everything else only average will "find a way to win." Hence: "A leaders recruit A reps; B leaders recruit C reps" — B leaders are insecure enough to fear that a stronger hire threatens their own position.

**Four categories for any position description** (build these out specifically for your own sales process and growth stage, not generically):
- **Knowledge** — the WHAT TO DO. Product, competition, process, methodology knowledge specific to your sales motion.
- **Skills** — the HOW TO DO IT. Executional ability to run each step of your specific sales process.
- **Characteristics** — durable personal traits (see below) — the part you cannot train into someone.
- **Execution Experience** — actual track record, treated as only partial evidence (see below), not the primary basis for a hiring decision.

**Why a resume is weak evidence on its own:** it's unverified (built by the candidate, for the candidate, to highlight strengths and hide gaps); it reveals company/title history but not actual knowledge of sales process, qualification methodology, or specific skills (cold-calling, pipeline generation, objection handling, listening); and it reveals nothing about the character traits that actually predict success.

**Skillset must match target-account complexity, not just "general sales ability."** A rep skilled at single-stakeholder, single-department selling will often fail badly moved into multi-stakeholder, multi-level, multi-department accounts (different departmental decision criteria, different messaging needs per level, the need to build multiple Champions across departments, harder budgeting/cost-justification alignment across units). McMahon's cautionary tale: a company that hired reps skilled at simple single-buyer HR-software sales to sell a complex multi-stakeholder BI platform failed repeatedly and was eventually sold for parts.

**The five character traits McMahon weighs most heavily (illustrated by star rep Carlo Carpanelli, hired despite an unconventional presentation, on the strength of these traits alone):**
- **Intelligence** — the capacity to absorb and keep updating product, competitive, and market knowledge as it evolves.
- **PHD (Persistence, Heart, and Desire)** — skill only compounds through many repetitions; a national billiards champion's line: "the losers practice until they get it right, the winners practice until they never get it wrong."
- **Coachability and adaptability** — two distinct failure modes exist: not coachable (won't accept feedback at all) and coachable-but-never-adapts (accepts feedback repeatedly but never actually changes behavior). Both are disqualifying in a fast-changing company.
- **Integrity** — dishonest dealing breaks team trust in a way that often can't be repaired.
- **Curiosity** — see "urgent curiosity" (Section 1); the honest, non-defensive desire to understand *why*, which also makes a person harder to offend and therefore more resilient under pressure.

**The hard rule on character:** "if their mother and father couldn't change them by twenty years old, then you can't change them." IQ, persistence, adaptability, and curiosity are treated as largely fixed traits to select for, not train into someone — whereas knowledge and skill are trainable.

**Ownership of attrition.** If a leader loses a rep they hired, that is the leader's failure, not the rep's — either the wrong person was hired, or the right person wasn't developed. The diagnostic questions for a leader to ask themselves: did I recruit the wrong person; is this a training/knowledge gap on my part; or do I lack the intimate understanding of this person's strengths, weaknesses, and motivations needed to actually inspire them?

## 13. Sales Management: Coaching, Accountability, and Forecasting Strategy

**Competence vs. commitment — the two-axis diagnostic for any performance issue.** Before addressing underperformance, determine which axis is actually broken:
- *Competence* (a "speak to their head" / fact-finding conversation) — do they know WHAT to do and HOW to do it? Questions: at what specific step/stage are they failing, and why; what knowledge or skill is missing; is there a training class or a peer/manager who can develop the skill; are you yourself competent enough in that area to help them?
- *Commitment* (a "speak to their heart" / feeling-finding conversation) — do they have the WILL and motivation? Questions: why do they feel performance is low; what's changed if they were previously committed; has something shifted personally; are you actually motivating them; are they bored and need a new challenge; is something or someone demotivating them; are they coachable and willing to learn new material?

Managers tell people what to do by speaking to the head. Leaders motivate people to do it by speaking to the heart. Most real performance problems are a mix of both, and diagnosing which is which — without bias or emotion — is a prerequisite to fixing it or making a fair termination decision.

**Where to actually spend coaching time.** A real example from the book: a manager was spending 80% of her time on two chronic underperformers (each running ~$600K/year vs. a $1.2M target) and only 20% on four strong performers (each already at $1.2M/year). The math: a 20% lift on the four performers is worth ~$240K/quarter combined; the same 20% lift on the two underperformers, even if achievable (it usually isn't — chronic underperformance is often a character/adaptability issue, not a coaching-access issue), is worth only ~$60K/quarter. Default coaching-time allocation toward underperformers is usually the wrong economic bet, and it also starves your best people of the development and attention that keeps them from leaving.

**Non-negotiables (minimum operating standards).** A short, explicit list every rep is expected to meet without being reminded — analogous to a family's basic household rules. McMahon's example list: show up to meetings on time; always be prepared; show respect to teammates; give full effort consistently; update CRM forecasts by a fixed weekly deadline; maintain cold command of top competitive differentiators, top competitor weaknesses, top customer pain points, several dollar-quantified success stories, and your own top value drivers; and be able to deliver a corporate overview, a 30-second elevator pitch, a whiteboard pitch, answers to the ten most common objections, and your top-ten Discovery and Scoping questions — all from memory.

**Performance standards need three explicit tiers** — exceptional, acceptable, unacceptable — and leaders must actually hold people accountable at the bottom tier, not just celebrate the top tier. Failing to hold consistent non-performers accountable is itself a tax on the top performers, who notice the absence of consequences and start asking why they should keep overachieving. Consequences don't have to be termination — reallocating territory or accounts is often sufficient and sends the signal.

**"Comfortable being uncomfortable."** New sales managers frequently try to stay likeable and avoid unpopular decisions. McMahon's corrective: the job is not a popularity contest — it's about recruiting, training, holding people accountable, and building a cohesive team, and some of that will make you unpopular with some people some of the time. If your team believes you have their genuine best interest at heart and are actively making them more competent, popularity is not what keeps them from leaving.

**Caring means competence, not perks or hugs.** Ping-pong tables, free beer, and even literal hugs from leadership don't reduce attrition on their own — a company doing all of that can still post very high attrition if it never trains or develops people. Pride — in growing competence, in the team, in the product, in the company — is what people actually want, and pride only follows from winning, which only follows from being made competent. Caring, operationally, means investing real time in making people better at the job.

**Forecasting strategy — several concrete techniques:**
- *Push unqualified deals off the forecast.* If a rep exhausts your qualifying questions and still can't articulate the deal's real status, remove it. This isn't punitive — it does two useful things: it pressures the rep to surface more real information, and it creates a quota gap that forces a reconciliation exercise (below), which is itself a training moment.
- *Forecast reconciliation.* When closed-plus-committed falls short of quota, the shortfall should not simply be waved through. Build a reconciliation target of roughly **4x the gap** (assuming a realistic ~25% close probability on net-new pipeline), broken down both by sales-process stage and by specific account with a stated activity plan for each. Doing this in week one of the quarter, not week eleven, is what actually creates enough runway to close the gap.
- *Pipeline is oxygen, not an afterthought.* Weak pipeline forces reps into "vending" — skipping steps, discounting to force a fit inside your quarter instead of the customer's actual buying timeline, "stealing from the future." Consistent, disciplined pipeline generation is unglamorous, high-effort weekly work, and a large enough qualified pipeline "masks all sales issues" — meaning a manager can't diagnose real qualification or skill gaps in a rep whose sheer pipeline volume keeps bailing them out.
- *"Take me to your Champion."* After the standard forecast review, the single highest-value additional verification step is for the manager to personally meet the Champion behind each forecasted deal, early in the quarter (within the first month) — not the last two weeks. This directly tests whether a real Champion (not a coach) exists and whether the rep is actually in control, using the criteria in Section 6.
- *Forecast accuracy is a trend, not a single data point.* One accurate quarter could be luck; three consecutive accurate quarters reflect genuine intimacy with your people and their accounts — which is what actually allows a business to scale hiring and spending decisions with confidence.
