# The Sales Acceleration Formula — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing sales hiring/training/demand-generation processes, or advising on scaling a sales team.*
*Book: "The Sales Acceleration Formula" — Mark Roberge*

---

## 1. The Four Formulae and the Mission of Scalable, Predictable Revenue Growth

Roberge's framing premise is that sales scaling is an engineering problem, not an art form. He came to the head-of-sales seat at HubSpot as an MIT-trained engineer who had written code and never worked in sales, and he treated the mission — "scalable, predictable revenue growth" — as something to be engineered. Seven years later the company crossed a $100M run-rate, 10,000 customers across more than 60 countries, and a 450-person sales, services, account management, and support organization.

The mission decomposes into four commitments, each of which becomes a formula:

1. **The Sales Hiring Formula** — "Hire the same successful salesperson every time."
2. **The Sales Training Formula** — "Train every salesperson in the same way."
3. **The Sales Management Formula** — "Hold our salespeople accountable to the same sales process."
4. **The Demand Generation Formula** — "Provide our salespeople with the same quality and quantity of leads every month."

"Formula" is not used algebraically. It means a collection of repeatable processes, metrics, and calculations applied to a domain that is usually run on instinct. For each of the four, the method is the same: devise a repeatable process, lean into metrics, run the correlations, and iterate on the model as data accumulates.

The prioritization argument that sits underneath all four: **world-class sales hiring is the most important driver of sales success.** Scaling a team generates an endless to-do list (hiring, training, coaching, pipeline reviews, forecasting, enterprise deal support, leadership development, cross-functional communication) and a start-up has the resources for only a fraction of it. Roberge's calculation was that world-class execution across all components would require a 150-hour workweek against a personal capacity of about 80 hours, so corners had to be cut deliberately. He chose to be world-class at hiring first, on the reasoning that being world-class at training, managing, coaching, and forecasting cannot offset a team of mediocre salespeople, while a team of top performers finds a way to win under any circumstances. Closing the next big customer wins the battle; finding a salesperson who will bring in hundreds of big customers for years wins the war.

## 2. Engineering the Sales Hiring Formula

The central and deliberately unhelpful finding: **the ideal sales hiring formula is different for every company, but the process to engineer the formula is the same.** Every salesperson has unique strengths (consultative selling, activity volume, presentation delivery, networking, making customers feel like family) and every company has a unique sales context (marketers vs. IT buyers, transactional vs. complex, established vs. evangelistic value proposition). Performance comes from the fit between the two. Roberge's earliest mistake was recruiting the top performers out of large, established sales forces — high-activity phone sellers from transactional processes with well-understood value propositions — into an evangelistic sale where prospects on the first call did not know what the company did or what the category was. Top-dog status in one context did not transfer.

The four-step engineering process:

**Step 1: Establish a theory of the ideal sales characteristics.** List the characteristics believed to correlate with sales success. For each one, document a clear definition — what "intelligence" means, what "aggressive" means — and define what a score of 1 versus 5 versus 10 represents on that characteristic. Each candidate is scored 1 to 10 per characteristic and summarized on an **Interview Scorecard**.

**Step 2: Define an evaluation strategy for each characteristic.** Decide in advance, per characteristic, what behavioral questions to ask, whether to use role plays, whether to set a pre-interview exercise for the candidate, and how to use reference checks.

**Step 3: Score candidates against the ideal sales characteristics.** Fill out the Interview Scorecard after every interview. The tooling is irrelevant (the original ran in Microsoft Excel); the key to the process is discipline, not sophisticated technology. Document findings and learnings as you go.

**Step 4: Learn and iterate on the model.** Once a handful of hires are on board and differentiating in performance, go back to the Interview Scorecards of the top performers and ask: which characteristics do these top performers have in common, and are those characteristics actually predictors of success here (increase their weight); which characteristics do not predict success (decrease their weight or eliminate them); and what is missing from the scorecard entirely (add it and start rating candidates on it). Repeat the same exercise on the salespeople progressing more slowly. Reflecting on as few as two or three hires is already compelling.

Once hiring volume accumulates — roughly a year of hiring, in Roberge's case — the model graduates from judgment to **formal regression analysis correlating the hiring characteristics with post-hire sales success**, which removes most of the remaining subjectivity. The regression is rerun every 6 to 12 months, both to absorb new data as the team expands and to account for shifts in buyer context caused by product evolution, changing buyer preferences, and changes to the competitive landscape. The finished formula also functions as a blueprint for future hiring managers, who then know exactly which characteristics to look for and how to evaluate each one instead of driving in the dark.

The headline result of the first regression: **the characteristics traditionally associated with salespeople — aggression and strong objection-handling ability — had the worst correlation with success.** Roberge reads this as a statistical fingerprint of the internet shifting power from the salesperson to the buyer. Statistics suggest salespeople who are intelligent and helpful, rather than aggressive and high-pressure, are the most successful with today's empowered buyer.

## 3. The Five Traits That Predicted Sales Success

Five characteristics correlated most significantly with sales success in HubSpot's buyer context, and the Interview Scorecard weights were set to the strength of each correlation. The set is presented as probably relevant to any company operating in a rapidly evolving market, but never as a universal mold.

1. **Coachability** — the ability to absorb and apply coaching. The single most significant influencer of the hiring decision, and the trait that most stood out in the interviews of the people who became rock stars. Evaluating it consumed the majority of the interview.
2. **Curiosity** — the ability to understand a potential customer's context through effective questioning and listening.
3. **Prior success** — a history of top performance or remarkable achievement. The most objectively measurable of the big five.
4. **Intelligence** — the ability to learn complex concepts quickly and communicate those concepts in an easy-to-understand manner.
5. **Work ethic** — proactively pursuing the company mission with a high degree of energy and daily activity.

Two context caveats attached to the list. Intelligence is not universally predictive: in a commoditized buyer context, work ethic is the superior predictor. Intelligence mattered at HubSpot because the industry was reshaping itself annually (Twitter was a garage project when they started selling and a $25 billion company seven years later), and salespeople had to absorb new concepts and translate them into marketing strategy implications faster than the market did. Most early-stage companies operate in rapidly evolving industries, so intelligence is expected to predict there too.

## 4. Interviewing for the Five Traits

**Coachability — the three-step role-play protocol.** After brief rapport-building, set up a verbal role play that models your actual buyer context: state who you are playing (title, company type, company size, team size), how the candidate came to be talking to you (inbound lead assigned to them, downloaded a specific asset last night), and the goal of the call (light discovery plus setting an appointment). Then:

- *Step 1: Set up a role-playing exercise that models your buyer context.* Give the scenario, invite questions, and let them begin.
- *Step 2: Evaluate the candidate's ability to self-diagnose.* Ask "How do you think you did?" The answer is the first coachability data point. "I did great" is a bad sign. You want reflection and analysis — specifics on what went well and what could improve. Then build on one of their own observations: "You mentioned you could have handled my question on SEO better. If we could rewind to that section, what would you do differently?"
- *Step 3: Evaluate the candidate's ability to absorb and apply coaching.* State explicitly that you give one area of positive feedback and one area of improvement in every interview. Lead with the praise — offering only improvements risks the candidate believing they are bombing and freezing up, which destroys your ability to assess them. Then coach for real (Roberge would go to the whiteboard), watching whether the candidate is glassy-eyed or taking notes and asking good follow-up questions. Then have them redo the role play applying the coaching.

**Absorb and apply are the two actions that constitute coachability.** Some candidates cannot absorb the coaching (poor listeners, or they do not value feedback); others absorb but cannot apply (less adaptable, weaker thinking on their feet). Calibration for the second pass: most candidates badly mishandle it, so look for effort, not perfection. Across well over 1,000 interviews in six years, perhaps five candidates absolutely crushed the second attempt, and every one of them became a rock star. If you witness perfection, hire at all costs. The underlying logic: you just watched meaningful improvement inside ten minutes, so extrapolate to a day, a week, a month.

**Curiosity — two tests.** The first happens in the lobby, at hello. Does the candidate open with a question? Do they ask about your day? Did they research your background and reference a finding from it? Do they follow your answers with smart open-ended questions? The second happens inside the role play, where the diagnostic is whether they lead with questions or "show up and throw up." The failing version answers "I have one minute" with an all-in-one platform pitch, a customer name-drop, and an ask for a ten-minute call. The passing version answers it with "I noticed you downloaded our eBook about generating leads on Facebook. What specific questions did you have about Facebook marketing?" and then keeps pulling the thread — "Have you run any Facebook campaigns yet?" / "How did they go?" / "Okay? What do you mean?" / "What types of people are qualified for your service? What types of people did the campaign attract?"

What curiosity looks like as a skill: great salespeople ask great questions, listen intently, and probe into points of interest without the prospect feeling interrogated; they educate through the questions they ask, which are thought-provoking enough to elicit "nobody has ever asked me that before"; they build trust quickly enough to earn the right to ask personal questions and get honest answers; and they seek customer goals, aspirations, fears, and struggles through tactical questioning. The prescribed practice drill: at a wedding reception, networking event, or party, approach a stranger and ask them questions, seeing how long you can go without mentioning yourself. If they walk away feeling interrogated, you need more practice. If they walk away thinking "that was a really smart and interesting person," you are on your way.

**Prior success — measure the rank, then verify it.** The interview line is mechanical: how many people held your role at the company, where did you rank, what metric is that rank based on (bookings, attainment), is it last quarter or all of last year, and will your references verify that performance. **The bar is top 10 percent**; a candidate outside that range has to score extremely high on the other traits to earn an offer. When the candidate did not come from a reasonably sized sales force, or from sales at all, evaluate prior success through other domains: academic performance and class rank, standardized test scores, standout or captain status on a varsity team, championship contribution, student government or leadership of an extracurricular organization, and — for non-sales candidates — how they differentiated themselves from peers in their current role. The team included an Olympic gold medalist, a cello player from the Portland Symphony, and a former comedian featured on Comedy Central; people who pursue a life passion with exceptional vigor at a top percentile bring the same drive to sales.

**Intelligence — start sales training during the interview process.** Expose candidates to new information early, then test absorption and retransmission later. At the end of the first phone screen, send training materials on the core concepts of your domain and ask them to learn the material before the next interview. Then reference that material inside the next role play by asking them to explain one of the concepts back to you as if advising you. Two things are being assessed: how well they understood the concepts they were exposed to, and how simply they can communicate them back. Keep asking follow-up questions until the candidate is stumped — the deeper you get before their answers degrade, the better they scored.

**Work ethic — three techniques.** (1) *Observations during the interview process*: how quickly did they return calls, how quickly did they turn around deliverables (resume, assessments, interview feedback), did they push the pace of the process or did you. (2) *Reference checks*: do not ask "did the candidate work hard?" Instead give the referee the trait list — coachability, curiosity, intelligence, work ethic — ask them to rank those from strongest to weakest for the candidate, and then ask why they ranked them that way. (3) *Behavioral questions* probing the rigor of their self-management: "Please tell me about your typical work day or work week. What are some of your must-do activities?"

## 5. Sourcing: Finding Top-Performing Salespeople

Finding great salespeople is the hardest part of the hiring process, harder than evaluating them. The founding data point: posting ads across every job board available produced hundreds of applications, about 50 phone screens, dozens of in-person interviews, and **zero hires**. Across hundreds of subsequent sales hires, not one came from a job board or was actively seeking a new role.

The principle: **great salespeople never need to apply for a job, so finding great salespeople requires a passive recruiting strategy.** Truly great salespeople have multiple standing offers at all times, and their old bosses call them roughly quarterly with ball-game invitations and open invitations to rejoin.

**Build a recruiting agency within your company.** External agencies charged 15 to 20 percent of base salary on a success-based fee and demanded exclusivity; Roberge ignored the exclusivity request and always ran two or three firms at once, cutting any firm whose candidates failed to clear the early stages. Results were average, but the real objection was structural: dependence on outside resources for the single most important driver of sales success, with no way to predictably triple hiring pace. The advice he took was: *don't hire a recruiting agency, don't build a corporate recruiting team, build a recruiting agency within your corporation.* The reasoning is a comp-and-incentive argument. Agency recruiters work hard, source passive candidates, and are paid performance-based variable packages that push them to crush the phones — but they are not working exclusively for you, and a rational currency-seeking recruiter pitches whichever company generates the highest commission. Internal corporate recruiters, generically, value quality of life, work nine to five, are not interested in cold-sourcing, are paid flat base salaries with no commission, and are better at launching job ads and ushering inbound resumes through the process — which is why internal recruiters so often hire agencies to do the cold-outreach work for them. The fix is to hire a talented agency recruiter who is thinking about starting her own firm and have her start the firm inside the company: paid as an agency recruiter (lower base, meaningful performance bonuses, higher overall earning potential), with bonuses based on **fill rates, timing, and long-term success of the hires made**, prohibited from using outside agencies, and measured on a sales-style funnel — outbound candidates touched this week, touches to connections, connections to phone screens, phone screens to hiring-manager interviews, interviews to hires.

**Sourcing passive candidates on LinkedIn — four steps.** All four were achievable on the free version at the time of writing. Note the sequencing constraint: the first recruiter was not approved until the sales team reached 10 salespeople, so sourcing the initial team fell entirely on the head of sales.

- *Step 1: Search for a list of qualified candidates.* Productive filters are zip code (geography of the hire), job title ("sales," "account executive" to filter to people already in the function), school (a proxy for intelligence where intelligence is a predictor), and company (local firms with large sales teams and quality training programs). The highest-yield search variant: pick a head of sales you have a relationship with, search for salespeople who worked at her company and have since left, build the list in about 15 minutes, email it to her, and ask which of them were standout performers. This converted roughly 99 percent of the time, and contacts would usually volunteer additional names once they understood the profile.
- *Step 2: Screen the results from the profile.* Look for indicators of sales excellence (team rankings, consistent quota attainment, President's Club); longevity at employers known to have high-performing teams (a poor salesperson survives a year, a mediocre one two years, so three, four, or five-plus years in a high-performing environment is a strong signal); alignment between their current buyer context and yours (enterprise vs. SMB, commodity vs. complex, relationship-oriented vs. transactional) as a read on learning curve rather than a showstopper; school, major, and academic performance as proxies for intelligence and prior success — with the observation that **second-tier schools produce the best candidates**, because elite-school hires often grow bored inside a sales organization and want career progression faster than the company can accommodate; and profile quality, which matters mostly at the extremes (a weak, photo-less profile is a major red flag in an era of social selling; a professional photo, 500-plus connections, and executive recommendations make a strongly positive impression).
- *Step 3: Engage the prescreened candidates.* A second-degree connection through a friend or employee is an enormous advantage and almost always produces access, so always ask the mutual contact for the introduction. With no mutual connection, guess the corporate email format and send a direct email rather than using InMail, on the logic that the inbox is more valuable real estate. The template: subject line is just `[Current employer]/[Undergraduate school]`; body is four lines — congratulations on their success, who you are, the fact that the current team cannot keep up with inbound lead flow so the team is expanding, that their background resembles your current top performers, and then the ask: "Are there any folks in your network who are in the job market and have a background similar to yours?" It works for three reasons: the subject line's only job is to get the email opened and that construction is irresistible; the body is brief, because at introduction you do not need to sell product, culture, or team, only make a candidate who is not in the job market wonder whether they are missing a life-changing opportunity; and **the ask is not guilt-inducing** — asking for a referral rather than going in for the kill reads as far less confrontational, makes the recipient feel less dirty about responding, and is genuine, since the friend with similar success who *is* looking is equally interesting. If no reply, follow up with a phone call the next day: salespeople all have phones and usually pick up inbound calls, so getting a connection is not extraordinarily hard.
- *Step 4: The "forced referral."* The best technique used. Connect on LinkedIn with all of your salespeople including recent hires. One to two months in, once they are comfortable, ask for referrals — but not by announcing a $2,500 referral bounty and asking if they know anyone. Instead: "I am going to set a 20-minute meeting with you tomorrow. Tonight I will go through your 275 connections on LinkedIn and look for salespeople in this city you are connected to who look like a fit for our team." Show up the next day with the 18 names that fit, and have them tell you which are top performers and which they are comfortable introducing. More work up front, beautifully effective, and hard to run until the team starts scaling.

**Understand the sales talent pool in your area.** Build a list of every company with an inside sales team in your recruiting market and interview at least one person from each, deliberately taking interviews with mediocre-looking candidates purely for intelligence. Questions to ask: how much does the company pay salespeople and how are the comp plans structured; what is the buyer context (transactional or complex, enterprise or SMB, mostly outbound or inbound leads); how many reps, what roles, how is the team structured; what is the sales training like (formal methodology, outside training or full-time staff); were there recent changes that would push top performers to consider leaving (commission plan change, leadership change); and who are the top salespeople there. On the last one, the tactic is to ask whether they are the top performer — salespeople who say no often blame the territory, which lets you ask which territory the best performer covers and then network to her. Roberge flags this last one as one he never used himself and labels it apply-at-your-own-risk; the point is creative information gathering to find top talent.

## 6. The Ideal First Sales Hire

A teaching case with four late-stage candidates for a start-up's first sales hire, ranked from least to most desirable.

**Candidate 1: The SVP of Sales** (25 years' experience, ran a 500-person team and $2B in revenue at the Fortune 1000 incumbent you want to disrupt). *Least favorite*, and the one founders are typically fixated on. Pros: a Rolodex of executive-level relationships with your target buyers, and industry knowledge giving strong strategic instincts on go-to-market, methodology, and value proposition. Both are easy dimensions to screen for and both are overrated — genuinely few deals are still done on golf courses. The Rolodex becomes materially more interesting only when the addressable market is tiny and concentrated (the top 10 telecom companies in the country). Cons: hesitancy to roll up his sleeves (a decade spent learning to delegate, arrives asking "where is my assistant?"); lack of recent front-line experience, when the first hire must be in the trenches talking to customers constantly; and low pace, unsuited to the high-energy, cut-corners tempo of an early-stage company.

**Candidate 2: The #1 Salesperson** (currently top rep on that 500-person team, three years in frontline sales). A step up, but still not advisable — the only case where it works is a founder/CEO with a sales management background willing to invest the time to coach the hire properly. Pros: industry knowledge at the front-line level (how to connect with the buyer, speak their language, find their current priorities) and world-class sales fundamentals, natural ability, work ethic, and competitive spirit. Cons: unproven ability to succeed in an unstructured environment — he joined his current employer into weeks of training, a finished pitch deck, an established methodology, and sales tools, all of which the first hire at a start-up must build from scratch; and he is the only candidate with no leadership experience, which matters because the ideal first sales hire should lead to many more hires.

**Candidate 3: The Entrepreneur** (until recently CEO of her own two-year-old start-up that ran out of capital; before that a salesperson at a large company known for breeding excellent fundamentals; little experience with your buyer context). *Most desirable.* Pros: entrepreneurial instinct — most likely of the four to accelerate the company toward the right product/market fit, and likely the part of the job she is most passionate about; she will dig in with prospects on their challenges, opportunities, perspectives, and priorities, and has the instinct to step back and help the CEO and product team see the patterns and decide where to pivot. Sales fundamentals from formal training at a large organization, enough to engineer an appropriate methodology and structure it for scale. Leadership potential from having run a company. Cons: no sales management fundamentals (she has probably never hired or managed a salesperson), which is an acceptable bet because you can monitor her closely once product/market fit is established; and the same industry learning curve as the sales manager.

**Candidate 4: The Sales Manager** (top rep promoted to manager six months ago, no experience with your buyer). Liked, but not the ideal fit. Pros: willingness to roll up her sleeves, since she was recently selling on the front line; leadership experience as a new manager, enough to build the methodology, hire, implement CRM, and grow into coaching an eight-plus person team, with off-the-charts motivation because the role fast-tracks her career; and a proven track record evidenced by the promotion. Cons: an industry learning curve; and, critically, weaker entrepreneurial instinct — she will ask the CEO for the vision on value proposition and target buyer, pitch that vision to buyers, fail to get traction, and be left scratching her head, because she needs to learn the target customer's biggest challenges *without being told*, understand how they are already thinking about solving them, take the pulse of their honest reaction to the value proposition, and then see the patterns and iterate on both target customer and value proposition.

The governing principle: **when faced with the first sales hire decision, many founders put the most weight on senior leadership experience and industry domain knowledge — do not fall into this trap. The most critical value from your first sales hire comes not from the first customers or revenue she generates, but from her ability to accelerate the company toward product/market fit.**

## 7. Why Ride-Along Training Fails: The Superpowers Problem

The industry-standard onboarding is the ride-along: the new hire shadows the #1 performer for a few weeks. Roberge's objection is not that shadowing is unpleasant but that the conclusions drawn from it are wrong and unpredictable.

**Superpowers** — every top-performing salesperson succeeds in her own unique way, excelling in one particular aspect of the selling process while ranging from "average" to "very good" across the rest. Two long-tenured top performers illustrate it. Betty was the best rapport-builder on the team; half of a product demonstration with her would be spent catching up on kids, pets, food, or music, and her prospects loved her and bought from her. Bob had no equal on activity volume, running roughly **25 percent more sales calls than anyone else on the team**, multitasking across ten open CRM tabs, on the phone and sending email in harmony. Each was average-to-very-good everywhere else.

The failure mode: if Betty had trained Bob, Bob would have concluded that succeeding on the job is about rapport building, known that schmoozing was not his strength, and become concerned about his ability to succeed at all. If Bob had trained Betty, Betty would have concluded that success is about activity volume and reached the same despair from the opposite direction. The same dynamic is illustrated with the ACME story, where a disciplined, preparation-driven new hire shadows a top performer who is late to appointments, does no research on prospects' companies, takes no interest in their strategy or priorities, and shows up with a pitch deck and starts talking — yet closes customer after customer on personal relationship alone. The new hire's rational conclusion is that he should abandon what made him successful and become a relationship seller.

Two summary principles: **every top-performing salesperson succeeds in her own unique way, and heavy reliance on ride-alongs during the training process jeopardizes a new hire's ability to shine using her unique strengths.** And separately: **a ride-along sales training strategy is neither scalable nor predictable** — how many new hires can shadow each top performer, how much of a distraction is that to the top performer, how do you quantify the program's success, and how do you audit and improve it.

What replaces it is exposure to a best-practices blueprint for the *entire* sales cycle — a sales methodology — combined with the flexibility for each salesperson to apply her own superpower to that process.

## 8. The Three Elements of the Sales Methodology

**Defining the sales methodology is what enables the sales training formula to be scalable and predictable.** It has three elements, and they must be built in this order.

**The buyer journey** — the general steps through which a company progresses as it purchases a product. A typical journey: the buyer discovers a problem he would like to solve; educates himself about the problem and the available solutions; creates a short list of solutions to evaluate; pilots one of them; assembles an ROI analysis on cost and benefits. Starting the methodology design here, rather than with your own process, is deliberate: it increases the likelihood that the buyer's needs stay front and center through the entire selling process, and it forces the team to step back and ask how the buying journey itself can be accelerated or streamlined.

**The sales process** — the seller-side steps that support the customer along his buying journey. If a potential customer requests information, the salesperson emails the relevant information and calls to learn more about the questions behind the request; that activity is *prospecting*. A portion of it produces a *connect* call, where the salesperson introduces the company and establishes some rapport. A successful connect call produces a *discovery call* on goals and objectives, which may produce a *presentation* or *demo*.

Two design constraints on the sales process:

- **Alignment with the buying journey.** Stages should map to the buyer's stages, so that the next step between buyer and salesperson is always in sync and the salesperson is perceived as helpful through the process.
- **Inspect-able stages.** Stages must let both salesperson and manager see the true status of each opportunity. "Influencer bought in" is a terrible stage: too subjective, defined differently in different salespeople's heads, and impossible for a manager to inspect. **"Discovery verified" is the model of a good stage** — it means the salesperson emailed the prospect a summary of the discovery call and the customer responded affirmatively. The salesperson knows unambiguously whether an opportunity is there, management can inspect it trivially, and the buyer is aligned by the explicit confirmation of his own goals and solution vision, leaving him ready to progress.

**The qualifying matrix** — the information needed from a potential buyer to establish both whether you can help him and whether he wants help. The information is gathered across various stages of the sales process and rarely in the same order twice. The long-standing default is **BANT**: *Budget* means the salesperson has validated that the value generated by the solution exceeds the cost and that budget to cover the cost is accessible; *Authority* means those budget criteria were validated with the person actually in charge of the budget; *Need* means the salesperson understands the goal the buyer is trying to achieve or problem he is trying to solve, can quantify that need, and understands the implications of not achieving the desired outcome; *Timing* means a specific calendar date by which the customer wants the need addressed. BANT is old-school but a good place to start; iterate past it as you understand your buyer context more deeply, keeping the criteria short, simple, and easy to understand.

## 9. Building the Curriculum and Making Training Predictable

**Curriculum construction.** Once the methodology is defined, structure the curriculum by introducing its elements in the same order they were created. Start with a session on the buying journey, diving deeply into the questions buyers are exploring at each stage so new hires get inside the buyer's head. Then run a session introducing the sales process and the qualifying matrix. Then run separate sessions that go deep on each individual stage — one on prospecting, one on the connect call, one on the discovery call, and so on. As the team grows, **have top salespeople teach the class that matches their superpower**: the head of sales pre-approves the content, the work is delegated, and the top performers get a professional development opportunity they value. This is the structural inverse of the ride-along, matching superpowers to specific training topics rather than letting one superpower define the whole job.

**Exams and certifications add predictability to the sales training formula, and provide the platform to learn from and iterate on it.**

- **The exam** tests factual information, primarily product knowledge. A **100-question exam** at the end of training, treated as a real gate: new hires crammed for it like a college final, and on some occasions trainees were let go for poor performance on it.
- **Certifications** test qualitative skills — the ability to navigate specific stages of the sales process. To graduate training, a new hire had to be certified on the discovery stage: given a scenario where a prospect has reached discovery, the trainee runs the discovery call as a role play against a sales trainer, who then completes a certification evaluation specific to that stage. The rubric names the specific behaviors being observed (for discovery: opening with open-ended questions, listening and digging deeper into areas of interest, addressing the various aspects of the qualifying matrix) and defines what performance merits **scores of 1, 5, 7, or 10**. The trainer does not say "good job here, bad job here" — the certification produces a quantifiable result. **The rubric is shared with trainees in advance** so expectations are explicit.

**The evaluator must not be the trainee's hiring manager.** Hiring managers are accountable for the decision to hire each trainee, so grading their own hires creates a conflict of interest. Sales trainers carry no such bias, and their responsibility is to ensure the company has an accurate read on each new hire's performance out of training. The second-order effect is the useful one: with grading removed from their hands, hiring managers are strongly incented to engage with new hires early in training and work hard to help them ace the certifications, because a new hire's evaluation performance reflects not only her own talent but the hiring manager's ability to find and develop talent.

## 10. Iterating on the Sales Training Formula

The structure exists partly to create a baseline from which to measure and iterate; the training formula must evolve with the business exactly as the hiring formula does. Three instruments:

1. **Six-month feedback forms.** At six months on the job, the hire completes a review of the training program. The timing is chosen deliberately: by then the salesperson has floor experience and can judge which parts of training were actually useful. The two highest-value questions are to rank the training sessions from most to least valuable, and to name subjects missing from the curriculum.
2. **Correlations between training performance and sales performance.** Run the same analysis used on hiring characteristics: did a high product-exam score predict field success, did a low score predict failure, did the sales certifications predict success. If the correlations do not exist, the training formula itself is in question. Where they do, lean into the aspects of training that are influencing sales success. The observed result: **training scores turned out to be an even better predictor of success than the hiring scores.**
3. **Desired behavior changes in the sales team.** New product developments, new addressable markets, or observed problems in the funnel all require retraining the existing team and adjusting the formula. Having a structured training formula already in place is what makes it straightforward to see where and how to adjust it as the business evolves.

## 11. Manufacturing Helpful Salespeople Buyers Trust

The premise is a power shift: buyers can find the top vendors in a space online, compare price and functionality, find current and former customers in social media, often try the product free, and sometimes buy it online without a seller at all. Which raises the question of why salespeople are needed. The answer is that they must prove their worth by adding value to the process — sales is no longer about memorizing the call script, the price book, and the top ten objections, it is about being a genuine consultant and trusted advisor.

The orienting inversion: **buyers should not be asked to understand the salesperson's solution and how it can help with their own goals; instead, the salesperson should understand the buyers' goals and how his own solution can help achieve those goals.**

**Train salespeople by making them live the buyer's day-to-day job.** Salespeople need enough business acumen to transform generic product messaging into a customized story that resonates with the buyer, addresses his needs, and uses his terminology. The questions a salesperson must be able to answer about the buyer's role: what do they do all day, what is easy about the job, what is hard, what causes stress, what do people in this role like to do, what do their bosses want them to do, how is success measured. **The best-trained salespeople have experienced the day-to-day job of their potential customers** — they have stood at both the starting gate and the finish line, which is what lets them advise rather than pitch.

The implementation: with marketing professionals as the target buyer, new sales hires did not spend their first weeks memorizing scripts and rehearsing objections. They spent them building their own website, writing their own blog, and creating their own social media presence — publishing articles, setting up landing pages, running A/B tests, segmenting leads, creating email nurturing campaigns, and analyzing visitor-to-lead-to-customer conversion, all in the company's own product. By graduation they often ranked at the top of Google for dozens of keywords and had built social followings of hundreds. By their first prospecting call they knew more about the discipline than 90 percent of the buyers they were calling. Two deliberate design choices: **the websites should not be relevant to your business** — Roberge preferred they were not, and had salespeople blog about chinchillas, the New England Patriots, and secret eateries — because authentic interest meant many kept updating them after training ended, and as the product evolved they would test new features on their own sites first and stay ahead of customers on feature fluency. The payoff shows up as a specific credibility move with skeptical buyers: *most people are skeptical when they first hear about this, I was too, I sold insurance before this and knew nothing about it, but look at this blog I built in training — search "best Italian food in Boston," that first listing is mine. You can do this. I can help you do this.*

**Modern selling feels less like a seller/buyer relationship and more like a doctor/patient relationship.** When a doctor asks whether you smoke or whether heart disease runs in your family, you do not lie — you see the diploma on the wall and tell the truth, because you know she is there to diagnose and fix the problem. When she prescribes, you do not say "let me think about it" or ask for 20 percent off. You take the pills. The illustrative case: a Fortune 500 VP of sales asks for help on marketing strategy, insists on traveling to Roberge rather than hosting him, arrives with a deck framing the problem and proposed solution built on twelve months of their own funnel data, spends 90 minutes taking diligent notes while he shares industry benchmarks, confirms what is compelling, challenges what is weak, and tells stories of customers with the same problem — then picks up the check and signs as a customer with no additional meetings. That status was not conferred by a job title; it was built by using the internet to share views and help others.

**Personal brand as a sales asset.** Social media presents an opportunity for every salesperson to be perceived as a trusted advisor by their buyers, and **salespeople should take time normally spent prospecting and reallocate it to social media participation, because the rewards are greater.** The reallocation is explicit and small: if you attend a chamber of commerce meeting twice a month, go once and spend the saved time in online conversations with your buyers; if you plan to cold-call ten hours this week, cold-call eight and use the two hours to write a blog article answering a question your buyers ask often. Four tactics to experiment with:

1. Find the people on Twitter your prospects follow — journalists, thought leaders, executives at complementary companies. Retweet their posts; many will follow back. Direct message them, introduce yourself, set up a call, ask how you can help, then help. Next time you publish, ask if they would promote it — but only if the content is genuinely relevant to their audience.
2. Find the LinkedIn groups your prospects are active in. Answer questions there, pose your own and engage with the responses, include links to topical content you have. If no group exists, start one.
3. Find the blogs your prospects read. Read them 15 minutes a day. Retweet one or two and promote them in your status. Comment on the blog with your name linked back to your profile or company — bloggers love comments and good ones respond, so comment back, build the relationship, and ask about submitting a guest piece.
4. Participate in your company's blog, and source the topics from your own Sent Items folder. A question you keep fielding from multiple prospects is an industry pain point, and therefore excellent blog content — if it comes up routinely, many people want the answer.

## 12. Metrics-Driven Sales Coaching

**Effective sales coaching by sales managers is the most important lever to drive sales productivity**, and Roberge's stated preference is to retitle every sales manager a "sales coach." Maximizing the time managers spend coaching is the highest-leverage management decision available.

The diagnostic analogy is golf instruction. The weak coach watches one swing and then delivers a stack of simultaneous corrections — new grip, lean back, more weight on the back foot, one o'clock not two o'clock on the backswing, turn the wrists over sooner — and loses the student entirely. The effective coach watches one swing, changes one thing ("try this grip, take 100 swings like that"), checks in twenty minutes later, and only then adds the next change ("now put more weight on your back foot, take another 100 swings").

**A common sales management mistake is to overwhelm the salesperson with coaching too many skills simultaneously. Pick one skill and focus.** The failure concentrates on new hires straight out of training, where the manager sees an enormous gap between current and desired performance and responds with pages of feedback; attempting to develop a salesperson across a spectrum of skills at once results in no skill being developed at all. The best managers identify the one skill that, if improved, produces the largest improvement in that salesperson's performance, then customize a coaching plan around developing it. The best of those **use metrics to diagnose which skill to prioritize** — that diagnosis-by-metrics step is what makes it metrics-driven sales coaching.

**Cascading the coaching culture through the organization.** Easy at one leader and eight salespeople; hard at 15-plus managers with director and VP layers. The mechanism is a monthly cadence built backward from the top inspection:

- *Second afternoon of every month*: the head of sales meets each director (each overseeing roughly 50 salespeople) to inspect their monthly coaching plans, asking three questions per salesperson — (1) what skill will you work on this month with this salesperson, (2) how did you decide on that skill, (3) what is the customized coaching plan you will use to develop the skill.
- *Second morning of every month*: directors meet their managers to inspect coaching plans, asking the same three questions they are about to be asked that afternoon.
- *First afternoon of every month*: each sales manager sits down with each salesperson, reviews their metrics, and builds the personalized coaching plan together.
- *First morning of every month*: each manager and salesperson review the performance metrics.

The whole organization is therefore accountable to a monthly cadence of sales coaching preparation, driven by the inspection above it rather than by exhortation.

**Build the coaching plan with the salesperson, not for them.** The manager does not open with "I reviewed your performance, here is what I saw, here is what we will work on and how" — that creates no buy-in, does not empower the salesperson, and squanders the meeting as a learning opportunity. Instead the manager walks the salesperson through diagnosing his own skill deficiency via a question sequence: "How do you think you did last month?" → "Qualitatively, what do you think you did well and what can you improve on?" → "Let's review the numbers. Here are the call activity metrics for the entire team. What are your observations about your performance on this chart?" → "Let's move to connect rates. What do you see here?" — continuing through all key metrics, and diving in wherever something interesting appears with "Why do you think your performance here was so strong (or so weak) relative to your peers in this area of the funnel?" The meeting closes on the two-part question that matters most: **"Reflecting on your qualitative observations and the metrics we ran through, which skill do you think we should work on this month, and what's the best way I can help you with that skill?"**

The manager usually already knows which skill she wants to work on, but should be willing to adjust based on the salesperson's contributions — the flexibility is what maximizes buy-in and teaches salespeople to become their own coach, reflecting on their own deficiencies and customizing their own improvement plans.

**Book the follow-up coaching sessions inside that same meeting.** The pattern: agree the skill ("developing a sense of urgency with your early-stage opportunities"), accept the salesperson's own proposed method (recording two discovery calls to review together), and put both sessions on the calendar on the spot with a stated prerequisite ("please have a recording of a qualifying call prepared for each of these meetings"). This converts coaching from a reactive activity into a scheduled one, so managers spend their time on the skills with the greatest impact on team performance instead of reacting to issues as they surface.

On metric selection, the guidance is deliberately modest: the right metrics vary company to company, so keep the first pass relatively simple and start with the high-level metrics already being tracked.

## 13. Metric Diagnosis Patterns and "Peeling Back the Onion"

The working instrument is a single chart comparing a basic funnel — **leads created, leads worked, demos delivered, customers closed** — across every salesperson on the team for the prior month. It shows where mediocre performers deviate from top performers and where the largest conversion leakage sits for each individual. Always ask the salesperson directly for his own read first, then offer your own diagnosis. Three recurring shapes and their prescribed diagnoses:

**Shape 1: fewest leads worked.** (a) *Overinvestment in unqualified opportunities* — not qualifying properly at the start of the buyer journey, so demo time is spent on opportunities unlikely to close; evidenced by a large number of discovery calls and demos coupled with a low close rate on those demos; coaching plan is a daily pipeline review of newly advanced opportunities focused on qualification. (b) *Time management* — too much research on very early-stage opportunities, weak CRM skills, self-created administrative work; evidenced by below-average volumes across the entire funnel; coaching plan is daily and weekly activity goals, calendar time-blocking per activity type, and regular reporting back. (c) *Personal motivation* — the average rep puts in a solid 50 hours a week and this one is investing 30; also evidenced by low volume throughout the funnel; coaching plan is a personal conversation about why he comes to work (how much does he want to earn, what would he do with the money, by when), connecting goal achievement to daily activity volume, then daily progress review. (d) *Call reluctance* — fear of picking up the phone; very hard to fix, probably indicates a failure in the recruiting funnel, and may mean the person is in the wrong profession.

**Shape 2: good leads worked, lowest demos and lowest customers.** (a) *Prospecting depth* — only touching leads once or twice before moving on; diagnose by reviewing CRM prospecting logs; coach on the right prospecting cadence and CRM use so nothing falls through the cracks. (b) *Lack of prospecting personalization* — calling deeply but sending the same email and leaving the same voicemail each time; diagnose by reading the content of her emails; each touch must add personalized context and build on the prior message, delivering the most helpful information at the most helpful time. (c) *Trust development on the connect call* — getting people on the phone but leading with a boring elevator pitch instead of using the call to learn; coaching plan is two 90-minute shadowing sessions on her prospecting and connect calls, role play the improvements, then shadow her next calls applying them.

**Shape 3: high leads worked and high demos, very low customers closed.** (a) *Lack of urgency* — **the most common issue Roberge encounters anywhere**: the prospect is excited, says he plans to buy, calls back the next day with a trade show or deadline excuse, asks for a call in a month, and barely remembers your name a month later. Surface it by reviewing "closed lost" reasons on late-stage opportunities; coach via role play on developing urgency and by recording and critiquing sales calls. The key questions: why does this prospect need our product today, what are the implications if they don't buy today, imagine it is six months from now and the problem is still not solved — what happens, will it really be that bad? If the answer is no, urgency was never developed and the business probably will not be won. (b) *Lack of decision-maker access* — if a prospective buyer says he is the decision maker, he probably isn't, and true decision makers often deflect ownership to someone else. Diagnose by checking how long it has been since the salesperson directly engaged the person responsible for signing key documents; if that turns out to be the CEO and no one has spoken to her, there is an issue. Coach by role-playing specific opportunities. Best-in-class salespeople appreciate the difference between why the end user wants the product and why the decision maker wants it, and alter how they communicate value to each party. (c) *Digging below the surface pain* — when asked about an opportunity's pain point the salesperson says "he needs more leads," which is everybody. Probe: why do they need more leads, how many do they generate today, how many do they need, how did they arrive at that goal, is this a "must-hit" goal or an "ideal world" goal.

**"Peeling back the onion"** — the practice of decomposing a high-level funnel ratio into its component ratios to isolate the actual skill deficiency, on the principle that the numbers rarely lie. Worked example: a weak leads-worked-to-demos-booked ratio splits into *leads-worked-to-connects* and *connects-to-demos-booked*. A low first ratio means she cannot get people on the phone, so dive into prospecting frequency and personalization. A low second ratio means she cannot pique interest once connected, so listen to the calls. Peeling back the onion saves time isolating deficiencies and gives confidence you are working the right area.

**Measuring the coaching itself.** Use the same funnel chart, but plotted month over month for one individual rather than across the team for one month. That lets you return to prior months' coaching plans, identify the metric each plan was meant to move, and verify whether it actually moved.

## 14. Sales Compensation Plans as the Primary Strategy Lever

**The sales compensation plan is one of the most effective tools for the CEO and VP of sales to drive business strategy.** Most of HubSpot's critical strategic shifts were executed through changes to the sales compensation plan, and each plan change produced measurable behavior change plus a side effect. **There is no perfect sales compensation plan; the appropriate plan depends on the stage of the business.** Three plans in the first six years:

**Plan 1: The Hunting Plan.** Context: 100 customers, an annual run rate of barely $300K, and a need to acquire customers fast to accelerate toward product/market fit. Structure: **$2 upfront for every $1 of monthly recurring revenue** brought on, so a $500-per-month subscription paid a one-time $1,000 commission. Churn protection: a **four-month claw-back** — if the customer churned inside four months the entire commission was taken back out of the salesperson's next month of earned commissions; past four months the commission was kept regardless of later cancellation. Result: HubSpot scaled from **100 to 1,000 customers in fewer than six months**, growing the run rate to **$3 million**. Side effect: churn exploded to an unsustainable level, concentrated in **month five** — immediately after the claw-back expired.

**Plan 2: The Customer Success Plan.** Diagnosis first. The initial theory was that post-sale consultants varied in quality, but churn analyzed by post-sale consultant was similar across the team. Churn analyzed by *salesperson* showed **more than a 10-fold difference between the lowest and highest churn rates**. The conclusion: not an onboarding problem but a sales problem, since retention was determined by which customers salespeople chose to target and what expectations they set. Roberge shared the per-salesperson churn analysis with the whole team, taught them why retention mattered, and announced the plan change a quarter in advance. Structure: stack-rank the team by churn rate and segment into quartiles. **Top quartile: $4 per $1 of MRR (double). Second quartile: $3 per $1 (a 50 percent increase). Third quartile: unchanged at $2 per $1. Bottom quartile: cut to $1 per $1**, with the explicit reason stated — their customers were not succeeding, were on average unprofitable for the company, and were having their money wasted by poor expectation setting — paired with training on expectation setting and an offer of help. Result: **churn dropped 70 percent within six months.**

**Plan 3: The Customer Commitment Plan.** About 18 months later Plan 2 had run its course: "poor expectation setting from sales" had nearly vanished from the churn-reason list, remaining churn was driven by causes outside the salesperson's control (customers acquired by larger companies, isolated product bugs), and so the best performers on the churn list were no longer the best expectation setters — they were simply lucky. The design question Roberge asked: **"What criterion is 100 percent in the control of the salesperson and highly correlated with customer success?"** The answer in that business was advanced payment terms: month-to-month customers were less committed and far more likely to churn, annual prepayers were more committed and more successful. Structure: **$2 per $1 of MRR, paid out 50 percent on the first month's payment, 25 percent on the sixth month's payment, 25 percent on the twelfth month's payment.** A month-to-month customer meant waiting a full year for the whole commission; a year prepaid meant earning all of it immediately. Result: the average prepayment commitment jumped from **2.5 months to seven months**, churn stayed in check and in fact improved, and salespeople felt in control of their destiny.

## 15. Designing and Rolling Out a Commission Plan

**Evaluate a sales compensation design through the lens of three factors: Simple. Aligned. Immediate.**

- **Simple** — salespeople should not need a spreadsheet to calculate their plan. Too many variables and they become confused about which behaviors drive the largest check, throw the plan aside, and sell the way they already know, forfeiting the whole opportunity to drive behavior. It should be extraordinarily clear which outcomes are being rewarded.
- **Aligned** — look ahead a year and ask what the single most important goal the company needs to achieve is (customer count, profitability, customer success, market share, new product distribution, new market penetration), then ask how the compensation plan can be aligned to it. If the majority of revenue comes from a sales team, aligning the comp plan is the most impactful tool available, regardless of how much you tweak training, redesign marketing materials, or attend customer conferences.
- **Immediate** — when salespeople succeed they should see it in their paycheck immediately, and when they fail they should feel it immediately. Any delay between the behavior and the financial outcome decreases the plan's impact.

**Involve the sales team in the design, without making it a democracy.** The rollout mechanism was a **"town meeting"** — optional but well attended — where Roberge explained why the plan was changing and what the new structure's goals were, then opened the floor to structural ideas, shared the structures under consideration, and took feedback. That was followed by a **company wiki page** restating the reasons, the goals, and the candidate structures, where the conversation continued asynchronously with Roberge responding to most comments. Throughout both, he was explicit that **the design was not a democratic process and would not be put to a vote** — transparency and involvement must not be confused with an invitation to design the plan around individual needs, because the company's interests, not the maximization of commission checks, are what is being protected. Outcomes: informing the team of the reasons and goals up front prepared them psychologically for the change; most appreciated the transparency even when the change hurt them individually; **every commission plan change included at least one structural element originating from a salesperson** during these discussions, each one legitimately useful rather than accepted to appease; and because even rejected ideas had been discussed openly, the team understood why the final structure was chosen and why their own ideas were not included.

## 16. Promotion Tiers

The second element of the compensation structure, alongside the commission plan, is a formalized career growth plan. Salespeople's goals vary — some want leadership skills, some want more ownership of entrepreneurial aspects of the job, and some have no desire to manage or change products and simply want to grow as individual contributors and hone their craft. The common default career goal is moving from inside sales to outside sales, which was unavailable at HubSpot because the company was deliberately focused on the large untapped SMB market through an exclusively inside sales team. The standard alternative — an annual review with a **traditional 2 to 4 percent salary increase** based on performance — was rejected as too subjective for a function as measurable as sales.

**Promotion tiers** replace it. Each tier specifies three things: the title at that level; the base pay, variable pay, and additional equity earned at that level; and the criteria required to reach the next level. In the worked example, the entry-level title is "sales associate," and promotion to senior sales associate requires all three of: an install base of **$60K in monthly recurring revenue**, an average of **$5K new MRR per month**, and signing new customers with an average of **six months paid up front**. On promotion the quota increases, but earnings per dollar closed increase as well, producing a higher commission rate and higher OTE, plus **an additional 10,000 stock options**.

Why it works: salespeople are competitive, financially motivated, and always looking to achieve goals, so they take the tiers seriously and constantly push for the next level — which makes the promotion criteria a second lever (alongside commission) for aligning compensation with desired behaviors. It is also better culturally: no annual reviews to manage and no arbitrary compensation increases, with subjectivity and politics removed and salespeople empowered to ascend as fast as they can, on weekly and monthly feedback rather than an annual cycle.

**Tenure is deliberately not a promotion criterion.** Some top performers hit the promotion in **as few as seven months**; for others it took **over two years**, which Roberge considered fine. Sales is a measurable, meritocratic function, so tenure was simply left out.

## 17. Sales Contests

**Sales contests are an effective tool to drive short-term behaviors and build team culture within the sales organization.** The stated relationship to compensation: if the compensation plan is Batman, the sales contest is Robin — almost as effective at motivating the team and driving desired behavior, with the added advantages that contests are fun, temporary, and short-term focused in a way commission plans cannot be. Roberge ran a contest almost every month, especially in the early years. Six design best practices:

1. **Align the contest with a short-term behavior change desired for the majority of the team.** Example: fearing a summer slump and wanting to boost activity in June is difficult to accomplish through the commission plan, but an activity-based June contest does it.
2. **Make the contest team-based.** With 12 people, form four teams of three and have the teams compete rather than every man for himself. **For the first three years every contest was a team contest**, with remarkable effects on culture: high performers helped lagging teammates, and laggards worked late to avoid letting their teams down. The first individual-performance contest, run after three years, immediately produced accusations of cheating and backstabbing on the floor, and they returned to team contests.
3. **Make the prize team-based.** Choose a reward the team experiences together — a limo to the casino, a golf outing, a day sailing. They win together *and* experience the reward together, return with photos, feel good about their colleagues, and are motivated to win again next month.
4. **Send out updated contest standings every night.** Publish at least once a day to the entire sales team, if not the entire company. Without daily updates, contest effectiveness drops precipitously — compile and post manually if necessary.
5. **Choose the time frame wisely.** Long enough to drive the behavior change, short enough to hold engagement. Daily is too short, weekly is the briefer end of acceptable, quarterly is probably too long, **monthly is ideal**.
6. **Avoid contest fever.** Overlapping contests dilute each other; run one contest at a time for a given group.

**The forecasting contest.** Problem: as the business matured, forecasting mattered more, and the team was both bad at it and not taking it seriously. Design: split everyone into four teams; every time a salesperson ran a product demo he had to estimate by end of day whether that prospect would close by month end; if yes, he wrote the prospect's name on a whiteboard with a **confidence score between 0 and 100**; at month end, confidence scores for prospects who became customers were added to his and his team's score, and confidence scores for prospects who did not were **subtracted**. **The winning team's score was negative 70.** The contest revealed that the team had "happy ears" after discovery calls and demos, assuming every prospect would buy, and had been skipping steps in discovery and qualification, producing sloppy pipeline management. Rerunning the contest alongside forecasting training improved both behavior and scores.

## 18. Developing Sales Leaders from Within

The received wisdom Roberge starts from is "don't promote your best salespeople to sales management," which he grants has merit: of all functions, **sales has the largest variance between the characteristics conducive to front-line success and those conducive to management success**, and really good salespeople are sometimes selfish, egotistical, and competitive by nature, which does not translate. But promoting the worst salespeople obviously fails too — how can a salesperson respect coaching from a manager who could not do the job himself? And hiring experienced managers externally failed in his context: he could not find managers who had led teams selling a similar value proposition to a similar buyer with a similar playbook, and the managers he did meet ran what he calls a **"sweatshop"** — drilling daily required activity metrics, telling reps to "just get me on the phone with a qualified prospect" and closing the business for them, spending most of the day inspecting forecasts and pipelines, not great coaches, not very analytical, not relating well to their people. So the answer was to train and promote from within, because those people already knew the buyer persona, the value proposition, and the sales system.

**Focus on leadership skills, rather than general sales management skills, when developing future managers internally.** The reframe came from listing the skills actually needed — coaching, negative feedback delivery, team motivation, conflict management — and realizing those are leadership skills, not sales management skills. The result was a **12-week sales leadership course**, and the recommendation is to focus on the skill categories rather than the specific resources: (1) Defining and Developing Your Leadership Style; (2) Providing Positive and Negative Feedback to Subordinates; (3) Successful Mentoring and Coaching; (4) Managing Conflict (with direct reports, with peers, with your boss); (5) Managing through Change; (6) Building and Developing Your Team; (7) Active Listening.

**Develop your leadership bench by using a formal leadership curriculum for your salespeople who earn the opportunity.** Each week ran in three steps: (1) the candidate completes a **preparation document** on that week's topic *before* any reading — for conflict management, representative conflict scenarios with the candidate explaining how he would handle each — which tests natural instincts before exposure to best practices; (2) the candidate completes the assigned reading, connecting general best practices to scenarios he will actually face; (3) the candidate meets with Roberge (later a full-time leadership trainer) to **role-play the scenarios in person**, which evaluates how well he absorbed and applied the material. Sample role-play prompts, issued in advance: *Providing negative feedback effectively* — you just shadowed a demo that was awful, the salesperson vomited product features the whole time, showed inferior product knowledge, and asked no qualifying questions. *Managing conflict with direct reports* — one of your salespeople signed two customers who both cancelled almost immediately, both commissions were clawed back, expectation-setting issues clearly occurred in both sales processes, but the salesperson is aggressively blaming the post-sale account manager for mismanaging onboarding. *Building team spirit and pride* — the team has missed goal for the second quarter running, fewer than 50 percent of your salespeople are making quota, and you have heard some are interviewing elsewhere. *Active listening* — a salesperson grabs you in the hallway asking to change teams, and you have told him you will meet immediately after your current meeting.

**Prerequisites for leadership consideration.** Tenure grants nothing; entry is earned on three evaluated areas. *Performance* is easiest — "exceed the sales targets for six months in a row"; being the top performer is unnecessary but consistent goal attainment is a must. *Sales skills* means **well-roundedness**, and this is where the superpowers finding bites: an activity hound who is mediocre at presentation skills could never develop a rep struggling with discovery calls, and would only succeed as a manager by finding eight salespeople whose strengths exactly matched his own, which is near-impossible and does not scale. Leaders need a well-rounded grasp of the whole methodology so they can diagnose a specific issue and customize a plan for it. The assessment reuses the new-hire skill certifications with **a more advanced level created for each skill area**, and the candidate must score at or above the advanced level on all of them. *Leadership potential* is demonstrated through contribution to the team while still an individual contributor: insightful questions and comments in team meetings, proactively mentoring a new salesperson on the floor, owning a class in new-hire training, or helping administer new skill training for the existing team.

**From the classroom to the real world.** **Before formal promotion, let qualified leadership candidates hire, train, and manage one new salesperson while still carrying their individual quota responsibilities.** Candidates who finished the course interviewed sales candidates, reported their assessments back to management, and named the hire they would make; once the hire was made they mentored that person through training and managed them for their **first two months** on the floor, with management as a support layer. This gives a real taste of the day-to-day role so candidates can judge for themselves before leaping, and creates a relatively safe environment for final skill development: a mistake costs credibility with one salesperson rather than fumbling early with an eight-person team. Roberge explicitly rejects the common **"team lead"** alternative — a salesperson permanently managing a small team while carrying an individual quota — because the team lead cannot balance both and it is usually the management component that suffers; his version works because it is limited in both duration and scope. The bonus is time-management practice: a promoted manager with an eight-person team realistically has only **three to four hours per week** per salesperson, and juggling a quota with one mentee is how a candidate learns to coach inside that constraint.

## 19. Common Potholes from New Sales Managers

**Avoid the common pitfalls of new managers: exhibiting weak time management around coaching, acting as a glorified salesperson, and giving up on new hires too early.**

**Effective time management.** The pattern is a star individual contributor — organized, well-rested, a pick-me-up for the office — who two months after promotion is harried, red-eyed, and burned out, saying there are not enough hours in the day. New managers hold an unrealistic perception of how much coaching they can actually do per team member; success as a manager is about the **efficiency** of their coaching, meaning the ability to diagnose issues, customize a plan, and execute it in a time-efficient manner. The prescribed exercise: have the new manager write on a whiteboard every category of professional work she does each week (coaching reps, management meetings, team meetings, deal involvement, email) with approximate hours per week for each, then total it. In the worked case the total was **102 hours**. Then walk the list: the team meetings have to happen, email cannot pile up, the time allocated to overseeing the team's opportunities is about right, so the only variable that can actually move is coaching time — which she had budgeted at **six hours per week per person across an eight-person team**. The conclusion is not to coach less but to coach more efficiently. **New sales managers need to appreciate how in-demand their coaching time is, and must be efficient with their coaching execution.**

**The manager as a glorified salesperson.** Almost all managers were individual contributors who controlled their own destiny — a bad quarter was solved by increasing their own activity. Management removes that direct control, since goals are now achieved through other people, which is a frustrating transition. The failure response is taking over the important calls: "just set me up with your next demo, I'll run it, I'll get the deal done." This smothers and spoils the team: salespeople stop closing deals themselves, lose confidence in their own abilities, and grow apathetic — "if I can just bring my manager on every call, she'll get me to quota or fail, either way it's not my fault." It also does not scale. Managers must be patient; it is painful to hear a salesperson mishandle an objection and stay silent, but it is essential to development. The salesperson **needs to skin his knees**, with the coaching coming afterward, and must be taught how to get himself out of trouble without hand-holding.

**Giving up on a salesperson too early.** Managers reported new hires "just weren't working out," and the more inexperienced the manager, the sooner the report arrived. When the company stayed patient and gave those supposedly weak salespeople **another six months**, many became rock stars. The prescribed test is a coachability probe rather than a time rule: pick one deficiency in the salesperson's process, coach it, and check in the next day. If there is improvement and the improvement sticks, that is a promising sign — the salesperson is demonstrating coachability and should evolve into a productive contributor even if it takes work. If a manager coaches a simple deficiency and the salesperson does not apply it, that is a bad sign, and it is probably best for both parties to part ways so the individual can find a buyer context better suited to his strengths.

## 20. The Demand Generation Formula: Inbound over Outbound

The opening diagnostic is a four-question audience survey Roberge has run on hundreds of audiences (MBA students, doctors and lawyers, tech entrepreneurs, realtors) with identical results every time: have you been cold-called in the past six months, did you enjoy it, did you buy; have you received unsolicited direct mail or email, did you open it, did you buy; have you Googled to research a product, did you enjoy it, did you purchase; have you heard about a product in social media from people you trust, did you look into it, did you buy. **Very few hands go up for cold calls or unsolicited email instigating a purchase; almost all hands go up for a Google search or social media discussion influencing one.**

**Outbound marketing** — interruptive tactics (cold calling, direct mail, advertising, trade shows). Buyers dislike it enough to invest in technology specifically to keep it out of their lives: the Do Not Call Registry, DVRs to skip commercials, spam blockers. **Inbound marketing** — the channels through which buyers empower themselves, principally Google and social media, where a buyer can be bored at home on a Saturday night researching the problems he faces at work, which is the true start of the modern sales and marketing funnel. **Today's buyer is empowered by the Internet, so a modern demand generation strategy means less focus on interruptive outbound marketing and more focus on inbound marketing.** The paradox Roberge highlights: asked the same questions as executives about where their companies spend, many are embarrassed by their outbound spend and are spending next to nothing on SEO, social media participation, and blogging — companies watch outbound effectiveness decline and inbound effectiveness rise, and keep pouring the majority of demand generation into outbound anyway.

**How search ranking actually works.** First-generation engines (Alta Vista, Excite) crawled **metadata** — meta-keywords, meta-descriptions, page titles — and ranked on that, which marketers learned to game with **"black hat"** tactics such as stuffing high-traffic words like "baseball" into meta-keywords, degrading result relevance until the core value of search engines was compromised. Google's design question was "what attribute of a website can we use to automatically determine the website's relevance and authority?" and its answer was **inbound links** — a hyperlink on another site pointing back to yours — on the reasoning that many people linking to a site makes it important, and that it is very hard to start a site and immediately convince thousands of people to link to it. Google also weights the authority of the linking site, so a link from the Wall Street Journal is thousands of times more impactful than one from a teenager's personal blog. The rise of social media added **social media influence** to the algorithm for the same anti-gaming reason: retweets of your articles, followers on your company Twitter account, and fans of your Facebook page are hard to fake, so heavy following and sharing signals thought leadership on a topic.

**Successful inbound marketing comes from two tactics: (1) continual quality content production (blogs, ebooks, webinars) on a frequent basis, and (2) frequent online participation in social media where your target buyers are already conversing.** That is the whole strategy. You cannot fake your way through it; the sites must be built authentically.

**This does not happen overnight.** The characteristic failure is lack of commitment: set up a blog and social accounts, write three articles, promote them, see nothing happen, and conclude inbound does not work for your business. The analogy is weight loss — you do not hit the gym three times in the first week and lose ten pounds, you probably stay the same weight, but three times a week for a few months and the weight comes off, the routine becomes ingrained, and you can no longer imagine a week without it. Same with inbound: a few times a week for a few months and results appear, the routine becomes part of the broader marketing process, and your marketing is changed for as long as you maintain it.

## 21. The Content Production Process and Long-Tail Topic Selection

The executive failure mode is taking it on personally: the CEO who leaves a talk resolved to blog twice a week will not keep it up, because executives, salespeople, and CEOs are busy, get pulled in many directions, may manage a week or two, and then a new priority halts production. **Do not overburden valuable resources at your company with inbound marketing responsibilities. Hire a journalist. Form a thought leadership committee. Put the two together to create a continuous stream of high-quality content.** Building the content production team is the executive's actual job here; once the team is in place the hard part is over.

**The journalist** is the one key resource, and developing journalistic capability inside the company is the hardest but most important part of the journey. The options run along a spectrum: hire a full-time journalist (many are extraordinarily gifted and their traditional opportunities are shrinking as newspapers and magazines struggle); hire an intern from the best journalism program near the office to come in a half day every Friday morning to write, potentially paid through course credit; use a freelancer, since the journalism industry is very open to freelance lifestyles; or repurpose an office administrator, who traditionally has exceptional written communication skills, by eliminating a mundane five-hour weekly task to free the time. **Do not obsess about domain experience when hiring the journalist** — deep knowledge of your product, industry, or buyer is helpful but far less important than great journalism skills, because a great journalist can sit with a PhD neuroscientist, pick her brain for an hour, and write a beautifully interesting piece.

**The thought leadership committee** supplies the journalist with a continual source of domain knowledge. Anyone who understands the industry, the product value proposition, and customer needs is a candidate: certainly the executive team; engineers if the product is technical; partners and external thought leaders; and above all **salespeople**, who hear the questions buyers ask at the beginning of the journey, have well-rehearsed answers, and know which answers resonate. Those question-and-answer pairs make excellent blog articles — and as with the personal-brand advice, **check the "Sent Items" folders on your salespeople's email**, because the canned responses they send repeatedly are exceptional blog material.

**The content production process** puts the two together. With ten people on the committee: **every Tuesday at 9 a.m., one committee member sits with the journalist for a one-hour interview**, on a niche subject — never the product, but an industry trend, a question buyers have early in their journey, or a phrase likely to resonate with someone the business can help — after which that member is done for **ten weeks** while the others cycle through. From that single hour the journalist produces a **three- to five-page ebook** on the topic, **three or four short blog posts** on niche subjects within the ebook, and **dozens of social media messages** for Twitter, LinkedIn, and Facebook drawn from the quotes, stats, and trends in each article. Created within a day or two, the output is **scheduled for release across an entire month**: each day one social message publishes, linking to its blog article; the end of each article carries a call to action ("Did you like this blog article on XYZ? Perhaps you will like the ebook we published on the same subject"); the click leads to a landing page offering the ebook free in exchange for name, email, phone number, and company URL. Repeat weekly, or twice weekly, or daily. The result is a stream of high-quality content produced on minimal budget and minimal executive time, extracting the company's brainpower onto the digital page, compounding into social following and inbound links, which drives exponential growth in buyers finding you through Google, with a high percentage self-identifying in exchange for the content.

**The before-and-after math.** Start with 10,000 website visitors per month and a single "Contact Us" call to action, converting **0.5 percent of visitors into leads, or 50 leads per month** — described as a very common reality for a small or mid-sized business. After a few months of inbound tactics it is not uncommon for traffic to grow by multiples; assume **three times, to 30,000 visitors**, and for the visitor-to-lead conversion rate to rise **from 0.5 percent to 3 percent** on the strength of useful ebook offers. That yields **900 leads per month, up from 50.**

**Complement content production with social media participation.** Social media is like a live conference happening every second of every day — set up your booth and join the conversation. On blogs your buyers read: read them too, add value with an intelligent comment, sign it with your name hyperlinked to your blog; leaving the first comment on an article that goes viral beats any advertisement you can buy; great bloggers love comments, often respond, and mentally note who leaves insightful ones, so reach out by email after being active for a while, invite them to guest blog on yours, ask to submit a guest post for theirs — which promotes your thought leadership to a new audience of target customers with the endorsement of someone they already trust. On Twitter: find who your buyers follow, follow them, retweet what is relevant, and many will follow back; build the relationship by email, then ask them to promote your content — if you have scratched their back a few times and your content is high quality, almost all will, putting your business in front of their 5,000 followers with a trusted thought leader's stamp of approval. In LinkedIn groups where prospects congregate: read the questions, answer the ones relevant to your value proposition, and **do not promote your product — don't even mention it, and don't worry about promoting your content either.** Simply answer with smart, helpful, knowledgeable responses; people will click through to your profile, notice your company, check it out, and subscribe to your content. The governing rule: social media participation is not a one-way promotional stream, which is egotistical, selfish, and not networking — the best networkers at an event meet people, ask questions, and add value. **As a rule of thumb, one-third of social media messages should be about your company and two-thirds about other people.**

**Long-tail theory** (from Christopher Anderson's *The Long Tail*) — within a given population, a large percentage of the overall data is represented by the multitude of small batches scattered far down the curve. With products on the x-axis ranked by popularity on the y-axis, the area under the **"head"** is what brick-and-mortar bookstores had to live on, because physical inventory limits meant only stocking best sellers; Amazon differentiated itself early by doing business in the **"long tail,"** where in many industries the area under the tail is actually greater than the area under the head and, critically, **less competitive**. The same pattern appears as Netflix versus Blockbuster, YouTube versus cable TV, and Spotify versus Best Buy. **Focus your content on "long-tail" topics: they are less competitive and more likely to attract your target buyer.** Applied to topic selection for an IT services business: do not chase "IT consultant" or "information technology," which sit at the head — millions of searches a month, extraordinarily competitive to rank for, and only a small portion of visitors actually qualified. Chase "Sharepoint implementations," "IT for pharmaceutical companies," or "hosted VOIP implementations" instead: not millions of searches, but still hundreds, and the searchers are far more qualified. Each piece of content targets a different slice of the tail and attracts hundreds of highly qualified buyers, and the more you publish along this strategy the larger the portion of the tail you own.

## 22. Marketing's Role in Converting Inbound Interest: The Buyer Matrix

The recurring symptom Roberge was called in to fix, at least five times a month: a customer six months into inbound has gone from a few dozen leads a month to over 500, and the salespeople hate the leads and believe quality is really low. The causes split between marketing's handling and sales' handling of those leads.

**The most common mistake: don't pass all the leads to Sales.** The inversion between the two models is the key concept. **Outbound** starts with a purchased list that is presumably a good **"fit"** — the Fortune 5000 telecom CEOs if that is your target — and then Sales and Marketing work the list as aggressively as possible with direct mail, email spam, targeted advertising, and cold calls, hoping **1 percent** respond; those who do respond probably have some **"pain"** that triggered the response. **Inbound is the inverse**: most inbound leads have a pain that needs solving (why else conduct the search, read the article, download the ebook?), but **not all of them are a good fit** — some are executives from exactly your target industry, which are beautiful leads, and some are PhD students from Asia doing dissertation research, who will never buy. Unqualified inbound leads do not mean inbound is failing; the situation just needs managing. Take the extreme case where only **10 percent, or 50 of the 500 leads**, are properly qualified. Those 50 are perfect-fit companies with a pain you can solve, and **they will probably close at twice the rate in half the time** compared with traditional outbound leads. But if Marketing passes all 500 — the 50 great and the 450 weak — Sales will end up hating the entire batch, because they must crawl through ten leads to find one good one. Filter and pass only the 50, and Sales will think they have the best marketing team on the planet and beg the executive team to invest more in marketing. Early on the filtering need not be sophisticated; someone on the marketing team can simply screen and pass the best ones through, since lead flow is low enough for a manual process.

**Avoid the lead scoring trap.** Lead scoring is a smart move in general but is usually implemented so as to cause more harm than good, because the algorithm becomes too complicated. "Once a lead exceeds a lead score of 50, the lead will be passed to sales" sounds harmless, but consider the underlying points: providing an email adds two, viewing the pricing page adds seven, requesting a demo adds ten, downloading an ebook adds five, additional ebook downloads add two each. There are so many permutations to reach or miss 50 that there is no reason to believe the threshold is the right call in all cases: **a start-up intern who downloads 20 ebooks on a Saturday night gets passed to Sales, while an important individual who visits one page and requests a demo does not.**

**The Buyer Persona/Buyer Journey matrix ("buyer matrix")** is the replacement. The y-axis is buyer personas, defined by **primarily static attributes that will not change** — business size, industry, role — for example Small Business, Mid-Market, Enterprise. The x-axis is the stages of the buyer journey, which **will change, hopefully as quickly as possible** — for example Problem Education, Solution Research, Solution Selection. A three-by-three matrix yields **nine unique "buyer states,"** each one a specific persona at a specific journey stage, and each one lets you customize marketing, sales, product, and customer support to that state. Worked contrast: a buyer in **Small Business/Problem Education** should be greeted on the website with a call to action to download an ebook on small business marketing trends, and referenced to educational collateral built for early-journey buyers when Sales connects; a buyer in **Small Business/Solution Selection** should get a call to action to read a case study about a small business customer in his industry, and be referenced to ROI reports summarizing similar customers' success. When first setting up the matrix, do not attempt a highly customized, thoroughly tested experience for every state — implement something basic per box, use your gut, form theories — then instrument it: how many buyers enter each state monthly, how many move on, how many get stuck or exit, where they get stuck and after how long. Then optimize **one buyer state at a time**: pick the worst-performing state with the best improvement opportunity, study the buyers progressing quickly through it (what content they consumed, what actions they took, what the company did to accelerate them) and the buyers stuck in it — **calling the stuck buyers directly to diagnose the blockers** — form theories, run experiments, then move to the next state.

**Use the buyer matrix to decide when to pass leads to Sales.** Start with a theory per persona. Enterprise leads pass at **Problem Education**, because the revenue potential is large enough that a Fortune 500 employee merely touching the website, subscribing to the blog, or mentioning you in social media warrants immediate follow-up. Mid-Market passes at **Solution Research**, with Marketing nurturing them through Problem Education until behavior (downloading product information, requesting a demo) signals the next stage. Small Business passes at **Solution Selection**, because budget and revenue potential are limited but volume is high, so Marketing nurtures through two stages until the buyer is, say, actively using the free trial. Then measure: **if lead conversion is remarkably low for a persona, move the pass-to-Sales threshold right in the matrix and require more nurturing; if remarkably high, move it left and pass sooner.**

**The worked analysis, run at the end of Q3 on Q1-generated leads** so they have had time to work through the journey. Enterprise: **1,500** leads reached Problem Education and passed immediately; **3 percent** converted, for **45 customers** at an average annual contract of **$700,000**. Mid-Market: **7,000** leads nurtured and passed; **6 percent** converted, for **420 customers** at **$200,000** average. Small Business: **11,000** leads nurtured and passed; **20 percent** converted, for **2,200 customers** at **$40,000** average. The 20 percent Small Business conversion rate suggests they may have waited too long and missed closable prospects, so the hypothesis is tested by passing Small Business leads at Problem Education instead: **30,000** leads passed, only **5 percent** converted, for **1,500 customers** at the same **$40,000**. **The experiment failed on revenue: 2,200 × $40,000 = $88 million under the original threshold versus 1,500 × $40,000 = $60 million under the experiment** — passing at the earlier stage overwhelmed the team with volume, wasted time on lesser-qualified opportunities, and drove down revenue productivity.

**Microsegmentation.** Start simple, but as expertise grows the simple model cannot accommodate real behavior — Enterprise deals involve multiple individuals (finance, marketing, IT) each with unique interests, and a Small Business segment may span technology, finance, and health care, each with distinct perspectives. The technique is to take one row of the matrix and explode it into a new matrix: for multi-influencer Enterprise, the new y-axis becomes the influencer roles (marketing end user, IT, finance) with the buyer journey unchanged on the x-axis, so different content can be shown to the IT manager and the marketing end user, each adjusted to their journey stage.

## 23. Sales' Role in Converting Inbound Interest

Difficulty handling inbound leads stems from classic sales training that does not apply to them and can actively hurt the sale. Three legacy behaviors do the most damage.

**Scrap the elevator pitch — lead with context.** The failing pattern is a voicemail sequence that is a carbon copy of the company elevator pitch, repeated across Tuesday, Thursday, and Monday with no new information ("Would you like to reach more decision makers... state-of-the-art technology that maximizes the accuracy of our data... please reach out to me today"). This is inappropriate in any modern prospecting situation and **the kiss of death for an inbound lead specifically**, because Marketing has already invested significant time and money attracting that buyer with educational content relevant to his context, the buyer has had a great experience and perceives the company as smart, helpful, and relevant — and one classically trained voicemail throws all of it out the window. The contextual version adds new information at every touch and ties each message to the buyer's actual interactions: *Tuesday* — I noticed you downloaded our ebook on Facebook marketing best practices, I looked at your company's Facebook page and had a few suggestions for improving it, I'll email those now; *Thursday* — I found a customer of ours in your industry who had enormous success with their Facebook strategy, sending that case study now so you can see the specific tactics and the results you should expect; *Monday* — I ran that customer through our grader tool and compared their social presence to yours, they scored an 87 and you scored a 54, sending those reports now, it turns out there is a lot more opportunity outside of Facebook in the broader social area for you. **Treat the voicemail and email sequence as a dialogue**: buyers do not always call back, but they are usually listening, so add new information each time and align each message to specific interactions with your company. Where the prospect's pain points are not publicly visible, infer context from how they found you, which article they read, and which ebooks they downloaded; share content related to those interests, tailored to business size, industry, or role; and instead of proposing a product demo as the next step, propose **a free consultation** on whatever topic interests them, sending an internal expert's bio and offering to connect them.

**The "going negative" voicemail** — whether the sequence is three voicemails or twelve, the final message should always be this one, credited to Rick Roberge: "I left you a few voicemails with suggestions and best practices on Facebook marketing. I have not heard back from you. I am going to assume that Facebook marketing is no longer a priority for you this year. Give me a call if it ever becomes a priority again." **It has the highest callback rate of any message in the sequence** — provided you have genuinely added value through the contextual prospecting, because the prospect does not want the flow of useful information to end.

**Call low, then call high.** The classically trained instinct is "call high with the elevator pitch" — find a decision maker and lead with a pitch about profit margin, accelerated growth, or decreased COGS. Applied to an inbound lead from a perfect-fit company where the contact is a middle manager, a frontline worker, or an intern, that pitch produces "Huh?" and a salesperson declaring the leads suck. The reframe: **ask why the intern is doing this research.** A decision maker almost certainly told him to, because the problem is important to the business right now — which means your product addresses something top of mind for a decision maker. Two strategies follow. Option one is to ignore the inbound contact and call the decision maker directly, leveraging the contact's context and assuming the decision maker shares it ("We have been receiving a number of inquiries from your team about generating leads through social media. I reviewed your Facebook page and had some ideas on how it can be a better lead generator for you"). Option two, which Roberge prefers, is **"call low, then call high"**: call the lead contact first with a value proposition relevant to *him* (not the one built for the decision maker), give as many free tips and as much free consulting as possible, and use each exchange to build trust and earn the right to ask about the organization's needs and where they originate — why did you decide to research this topic; was there someone at the company who instructed you to; who do you report to and what have they been asking of you lately; what is on your CEO's mind these days and what did she talk about at the last company meeting; can you tell me more about your CMO's areas of focus. Done well, this converts the inbound contact into a **"coach."** **Do not confuse a coach with a champion**: the inbound contact usually lacks the authority to be a champion, meaning he cannot influence the organization enough to compel the purchase, but as a coach he supplies internal context that lets the salesperson help the organization more effectively. Only then call high, using what the coach gave you: "I have been working with members of your team on the company's Facebook lead generation strategy. I am aware that the company has recently decided to expand the sales team by 20 percent in Q4 and is working aggressively to identify new sources of qualified leads for the new hires. One of our customers in your industry generated leads through social media and far exceeded the 20 percent lift you are targeting. Would you have a few minutes to walk through the details of their strategy?"

**Prioritize prospecting by level of engagement, not alphabetical order.** Sorting leads alphabetically is common and a bad sign. A touch cadence — calling company ABC today because that is when it is due based on last touch — is better and does ensure nothing falls through the cracks, but still has exceptions. The test case: a lead called yesterday who just visited your website two minutes ago, versus a lead called three days ago and now overdue for follow-up. Call the first one, quickly. **Engagement is the best criterion for prioritizing prospecting calls**, and examples of engagement include: a lead visited my website; opened my sales email; opened an email from my drip marketing campaign; mentioned a key phrase relevant to my business on social media; mentioned our company or our competition on social media; followed our CEO on Twitter; downloaded one of our ebooks. Salespeople need to know in real time when these occur and organize their activity accordingly — forget alphabetical order and deprioritize touch cadences.

**Specialize sales by inbound versus outbound.** Selling to an inbound lead requires a different skill set, which is one reason to specialize. The second reason is behavioral: **if a salesperson receives 50 inbound leads a month and is also asked to supplement his pipeline with cold calling, he will not make the cold calls**, because salespeople always take the path of least resistance to goal — whereas a salesperson given no inbound leads and told to hit quota by cold calling will find a way. HubSpot's structure through the $0 to $100M journey was one team calling inbound leads exclusively, which got very good at inbound-specific tactics while the company got good at sizing that team to optimize lead flow per salesperson and time spent per lead; and a second team hitting goal by cold calling, supported by **business development reps (BDRs)** who made the cold calls and set appointments for outbound closing specialists, with the advantage of targeting perfect-fit companies but the added burden of educating cold prospects and creating pain points. For a company transitioning from an all-cold-calling team to a hybrid structure, the recommended move is to **take your top performers and rotate the inbound leads between them**, so they develop the inbound tactical skills while you experiment with the right lead volume per salesperson, then scale that team. The pitfall to avoid is the inbound team excusing a miss on marketing's underdelivery; the inbound team must be able to hit goal regardless, achieved either by (1) keeping closers as generalists and specializing only the appointment setters by inbound versus outbound, so closers work all opportunities and allocate time to wherever the action is, or (2) giving each inbound salesperson a set of named accounts to work alongside the inbound flow.

## 24. The SMarketing SLA

**The dysfunctional relationship between Sales and Marketing is the kiss of death in a buyer-driven world.** The caricature: Marketing in one corner believing Sales is a group of overpaid, self-centered brats who miss the big-picture strategy; Sales in another believing Marketing sits around doing arts and crafts all day with no idea what a qualified lead looks like; both retreating to their corners, Sales cranking cold calls and Marketing running trade shows and branding exercises. It is fatal now because buyers begin their journey online, so Marketing must own the initial engagement, nurture through the early phases, and pass to Sales at the most appropriate and helpful moment, with Sales picking up where Marketing left off to create continuity in the buyer's experience. The remedy is borrowed from IT, where service level agreements quantify acceptable system availability (99.999 percent uptime per month): **use the Sales and Marketing SLA to replace the subjective and qualitative aspects of the Sales/Marketing relationship with well-defined targets and quantified goals.**

**The Marketing SLA, step one: define lead quality.** The decision of when a lead is qualified to pass comes from the buyer matrix, depending on the type of company and its engagement level. Worked example: Mid-Market persona leads pass at the Solution Research stage, where Mid-Market means a company with **1,000 to 10,000 employees** and reaching Solution Research means the lead downloaded product information or requested a demonstration. **If Marketing delivers a lead meeting those criteria, the lead "does not suck" and Sales should engage it** — and if Sales keeps resisting such leads, either Sales needs training on engaging them or the SLA needs revising to better define what earns Sales' attention.

**Step two: define lead quantity from the sales funnel backward.** Assume the optimal flow for a mid-market salesperson is **150 qualified leads per month**, at which he connects with **50 percent** of them, creates **25 qualified opportunities**, moves **15** to the presentation stage, and converts **five** presentations into paying customers each month, thereby hitting his revenue goal. With **10 salespeople on the mid-market team at 150 leads each, Marketing owes 1,500 qualified leads per month.** Roberge notes that companies reaching this level of precision rank in the **top 5 percent** of Sales and Marketing alignment excellence.

**Step three: fix the flaw that precision alone does not solve.** Under a raw lead-count SLA, a VP of marketing downloading product collateral and a VP of marketing active in a free trial both generate one qualified lead, yet **active free trials closed at many times the rate of product collateral downloads**, being at Solution Selection rather than Solution Research. Product collateral was also the easier lead for Marketing to generate. So the SLA structure quietly pushed Marketing toward the lead type Sales valued least: collateral-download calls to action appeared more often on the website, and emergency demand-generation emails focused on collateral downloads. The teams were not aligned. The fix is to shift from raw lead counts to **implied dollar value of leads**, engineered in three steps: (1) for each buyer state, calculate the average rate at which those leads convert to customers; (2) for each segment, calculate the average purchase price of customers generated from that class of lead; (3) multiply conversion rate by average purchase price to get the dollar value of a lead in that Buyer Persona/Buyer Journey segment. The SLA then becomes an aggregate implied value: Marketing owed not 1,500 mid-market leads but **$12 million of mid-market implied lead value**, achievable through 1,000 demo requests, 3,000 product collateral downloads, or any appropriate combination — statistically, Sales would have enough qualified leads either way. **The Marketing SLA provides a framework to put Marketing on a revenue quota, similar to Sales.** Result: within one week of the change, website calls to action shifted from collateral downloads to free trials, Sales was happy with lead quality, and Marketing got proper credit.

**The Sales SLA** — the agreement is bidirectional, and Sales cannot sit back saying "more, more, more." **Sales is accountable to Marketing just like Marketing is accountable to Sales; the Sales SLA defines a series of behaviors expected of the Sales team to ensure each lead is worked effectively.** First component, speed: market data and Roberge's own validated analysis both show a lead called within minutes rather than hours or days converts at an **exponentially higher** rate, so one clause reads "every lead from the website should be called within one hour of converting." Second component, call volume per lead, derived rather than asserted. The analysis plots **50,000 sales leads** with the number of call attempts on the x-axis (some were called only once, some twelve times) and the **profitability** of calling a lead that many times on the y-axis, so the peak identifies the ideal per-lead call volume — balancing the higher connect probability of more attempts against the organizational time each attempt costs. The conclusions: **the optimal number of call attempts is five for a small business lead, eight for mid-market, and twelve for enterprise.** These patterns were then programmed into the CRM so salespeople did not have to think about them, with a daily dashboard so no leads slipped through the cracks — accepted by the team because salespeople prefer to think about the hard parts like breaking the ice and building rapport, and like having a mechanism that backs them up, provided a data-driven culture was built from the start.

**Daily reporting on both sides.** **Send daily reports updating the entire Sales and Marketing team on the Sales and Marketing SLA, and manage the Sales and Marketing machine on a daily basis.** For Marketing, the report plots **ideal lead value generated from the first to the last day of the month against actual lead value generated each day**, and the goal is to track the ideal line as closely as possible, because deviation in *either* direction creates inefficiency: a slow three weeks followed by a monster final week leaves Sales twiddling their thumbs and then drowning; crushing week one and trickling thereafter means Sales cannot keep up with the early volume, well-qualified leads get lost to slow follow-up, and then lead flow dries up — even if Marketing limps to its SLA number. For Sales, the enforcement tool is the **"Do Not Be on It" dashboard**, an example of keeping it simple in sales: being on the dashboard means you are violating the Sales SLA. Its charts showed new leads not touched within one hour of converting through the website, free trials one week old called fewer than three times, and demo requests three days old touched fewer than two times. Program your own SLA measures into it and distribute daily.

## 25. Sales Technology: Sell Faster, Sell Better

**Historically, sales technology has been built for the sales leader, not the salesperson. This technology does not work for salespeople; instead, it creates work for salespeople** — admin tasks, time off the phones, time away from selling. It has been ineffective enough that many salespeople refuse to use it, producing adoption problems that then compromise the very value propositions the software was bought for, such as easy pipeline review or forecast analysis, because the data is incomplete or sloppily kept. The response is not to avoid sales technology or let salespeople do their thing, but to approach it correctly. **Companies should strive to adopt sales technology that enables better buying for customers and faster selling for salespeople:**

1. **Sell faster** — accelerate the current sales process by eliminating admin work and mundane tasks for salespeople.
2. **Sell better** — create a better buying experience by capturing buyer context and making it available to salespeople wherever they are, so they engage buyers with the most helpful information at the most helpful time.

**Accelerating lead sourcing.** Lead sourcing (finding qualified buyers to call) is one of the most time-consuming steps. Purchased lists of supposedly qualified leads typically yield bad prospects, many no longer even employed at the listed companies; data-source subscriptions leave salespeople filtering massive contact sets where poor accuracy and comprehensiveness make the filtering an extremely inefficient use of time. HubSpot's team made both mistakes before resorting to plain Google searches for phrases that would surface good-fit companies. That worked but cost too much time, because the process ran: search Google; click the first interesting company; scan the website to verify qualification; search the CRM to see whether the company is already owned by another salesperson; if unowned, research online to find the executives involved in the purchase decision; research further for industry, revenue, territory, and contact information; type all of it into the CRM. **Ten clicks. Ten minutes. One lead sourced.** The engineered version: search Google; click the first interesting company, at which point all the data needed to evaluate it appears in a **browser sidebar** next to the company website — company summary, territory, revenue, key executives, existing CRM records, account ownership, and contact history — addable to the CRM with one click, with a list of **similar companies** at the bottom of the panel on the logic that a salesperson who likes this lead will probably like five more like it, each viewable and addable with one click. **Ten clicks. Ten minutes. Ten leads sourced.** In elapsed terms: sourcing 50 new leads took a full business day under the old process and **less than one hour** with the tooling. Once a company is in the CRM, the technology enriches the record with buyer context automatically — prior website visits, marketing email opens, ebook downloads, recent social media activity — so **sales technology creates better buying experiences for customers by capturing customer context and making that context readily available to salespeople.**

**Accelerating sales prospecting.** The weak pattern is sorting the list by last activity date or alphabetical order and personalizing only the contact and company name, so every touch is the same elevator pitch. Better salespeople establish a strategic cadence based on lead quality and personalize with company news such as a funding round or an industry award. Roberge's challenge to even that: company news does not actually help usher a company along its buyer journey, whereas knowing that someone from the company visited your website, opened an email from your marketing department, opened an email from you, or mentioned your competitor in social media — **as the events are occurring** — is more relevant and lets the salesperson engage with the most helpful information at the most helpful time, yielding many more connects. The second half of the problem is activity logging: failing to log means no historical trail of engagement per lead and no evidence for the manager that the salesperson has been working, yet traditional technology makes logging painful. The typical end-to-end prospecting process runs fourteen steps — click through to the lead record; research company news to reference; dial and leave the voicemail; create a task in the CRM; categorize it as a voicemail; save it; compose the email; send it; create another task; categorize it as an email send; copy and paste the email into the task; save it; create a new task to follow up in two days; save it — and this **might be conducted more than 50 times per day.** The automated alternative: the salesperson tells the system he is ready to prospect, and the system cues the first lead record, displays the full context for quick absorption, dials the phone, automatically logs the voicemail if no connect occurs, suggests and auto-personalizes the follow-up email, logs the send, and schedules the next follow-up task according to the optimal cadence for that lead type. **Sales technology enables faster selling for salespeople by eliminating admin tasks and automating data capture.**

**Accelerating lead engagement.** Traditional salespeople force every prospect through a prefabricated process, sharing the same content in the same sequence, with stages driven by the salesperson's behavior and perception rather than the buyer's actions ("yes, I gave them a demo," "yes, I asked if they have budget"). That yields a seemingly full pipeline that gets stuck and does not convert, because the pipeline quality is fabricated. The alternative: **opportunity stages should be driven by actions the buyer takes, not actions the salesperson takes** — did the buyer confirm the discovery call summary was accurate, did the buyer open the proposal, did a VP- or C-level executive attend a product overview, has someone in Finance reviewed the ROI study. With those buyer actions captured by technology, opportunities move forward and backward automatically as buyer action or inaction registers, **eliminating the salesperson's subjectivity and gut feel**, which is what usually contaminates pipeline and forecast accuracy. Aligning opportunity stages with the buying journey also keeps the team in lockstep with the buyer, and technology can support that by recommending the right collateral for a given buyer state: which blog article, ebook, or webinar fits a mid-sized health care company investigating compliance with a new FDA mandate, or which case study fits an enterprise manufacturer looking to support its distribution channel with a digital marketing budget.

**Automated reporting as the byproduct.** Because the technology actually helps the salesperson, adoption rises and data integrity improves, which automates collection of the data needed to run the organization. **Sales technology developed for the salesperson in the end benefits the sales manager with more accurate reporting to run the salesforce and the business.** Sales executives gain visibility into: whether the pipeline is positioned to achieve the quarter's target; the total revenue forecast for the quarter; whether top-of-funnel activity is on par with what next quarter requires; where each salesperson ranks in total funnel activity (leads sourced, voicemails, connects, discovery calls, presentations) by day, week, and month; how a salesperson's current activity compares with her own historic performance; whether the team is working each newly generated lead in accordance with the Sales SLA; and whether any leads, especially high-priority ones like demo requests or free trials, are slipping through the cracks. Marketers gain visibility into whether their leads are being worked per the SLA, which lead types are accelerating through the buyer journey and which are not, which salespeople most effectively leverage available collateral, which collateral produces the highest prospect engagement (down to which slides or pages are popular or ignored), and whether the team is using the latest collateral. Salespeople gain visibility into which prospects recently engaged with their emails and collateral, how their activity volume compares with the team, whether they are completing enough activity to hit their goals, and whether any leads or opportunities are falling through the cracks. All of it without additional burden on the salesperson, because capture is automated.

## 26. Running Sales Experiments

**Great teams have a core philosophy of continual improvement, and a key ingredient to the sales acceleration formula is fostering a culture of experimentation.** The prompt came in year two from board member Gail Goodman, CEO of ConstantContact: "Great year, but you guys are not experimenting enough. You have a very good model. It is working. But there must be ways to do it better. You are a young company. Try some things. Don't be afraid to fail." The governing question for any major change: **why make a major change without first testing it on a smaller scale?**

**The bottom-up innovation funnel.** The most groundbreaking ideas came not from executives or directors but from the front line — the people talking to prospects, going head to head with competitors, and working with customers daily, who see the patterns and generate trajectory-changing ideas. **A key role of the executive team is to set up a culture around innovation, rather than generate all of the big ideas themselves.** Three mechanisms sustained it.

The **internal "hackathon"**: framed around a specific problem relevant at the time (slow customer adoption of a new feature, a competitor's new offering, an internal culture issue), announced company-wide with an open invitation, usually held after hours with pizza and beer, sometimes drawing hundreds of people. Format: the organizer frames the problem and sets the agenda; anyone with an idea pitches it for a minute or two; all ideas go on a whiteboard; after roughly **30 minutes** of idea generation the crowd is polled on interest; the **top 10 or so** are selected and small breakout teams form around each; teams spend about **an hour** discussing their idea and designing an experiment to test it; then each team presents details to the broader group. Simple, low-investment ideas are executed by whoever owns the relevant business function. Promising ideas needing significant investment are presented to the executive team at a dedicated **experiment board meeting**, which decides whether to fund them; funded ideas enter the **innovation pipeline**, monitored monthly, with teams delivering status updates so the board can provide guidance and decide whether to continue, add resources, or discontinue a project to make room for others.

**Organizational transparency**: a successful innovation culture requires all employees to feel like the "CEO" of their functional area, and being CEO requires a full picture of the business. So all monthly financials were available to every employee, reviewed at company meetings and posted on the wiki; the tactical priorities of the business and of each executive were published on the wiki and reviewed at company meetings; monthly progress against the operational plan was reported company-wide; customer happiness was measured with a **monthly NPS survey** and employee sentiment with a **quarterly employee NPS**, with raw data and summaries from both shared with everyone. The wiki itself was extremely active, with the executive team responding to questions and comments, flattening the steep communication pyramid of more archaic organizations.

**An "innovation" career track.** Three tracks existed: the **leadership track** (functional expertise established, then leadership training and management opportunities), the **functional expert track** (the career salesperson who wants to grow as an individual contributor, the career engineer who wants to grow as a code ninja, with well-defined attractive paths built proactively), and the **innovation track** for employees most passionate about breaking new ground, who were heavy participants in hackathons and wiki threads and were often eventually selected to lead an experiment. **Being able to point to successful leaders who reached their positions via the innovation track was key to the culture's credibility.**

**Follow a specific formula for experiment execution so that you can be confident your experiments are efficient and effective.** Five steps:

1. **Define a clear goal and measure of success.** Obvious but routinely skipped. Without it, three weeks in the team is in the weeds and has lost sight of the original intention, and at the end there is no consensus on whether the results indicate success. Set a clear objective, treat it as a thesis, define what success and failure look like, and be absolutely disciplined about quantifying success.
2. **Design the experiment execution.** Find a way to test the thesis in the least time for the least investment, because time and cost determine the experiment's attractiveness: an experiment that runs in a day for under $100 and could triple the business gets run in a heartbeat, while one taking a year and hundreds of thousands of dollars needs an extraordinarily high potential return to even consider. **Finding the lowest-cost, shortest path to experimentation is critical.**
3. **Choose a leader.** Ideally the experiment came out of an innovation process and the employee who conceived it has the skills, passion, and time to lead it, since personal attachment generates exponentially higher motivation than an inherited project. Great experiment leaders have passion for the idea, knowledge of the functional areas being tested, and professional goals the experiment helps them achieve.
4. **Assemble the team.** Assign **at least two people** to every experiment, and choose **top performers rather than average performers**, because what the first phase is looking for is a **"true negative"**: if two top performers cannot make it work, the idea probably was not feasible and certainly should not be rolled out broadly; whereas if one average salesperson fails, you have learned nothing, since you cannot tell whether the idea or the person was the problem.
5. **Establish routine check-ins.** Set expectations on evaluation frequency. HubSpot held a **monthly experiment meeting** with senior leadership where the experiment leader and team had **30 minutes** to present progress and field questions, after which weak progress could mean discontinued funding and apparent success could mean additional funding or expansion plans.

## 27. Two Worked Experiments: The VAR Program and GPCT

**The Value Added Reseller (VAR) program.** Context: the fourth salesperson hired, Pete Caputa, was a nontraditional hire — an engineer by training, coming from a small start-up where he had been CEO, with some solid sales training. A few months in he became passionate about building a VAR channel targeting the small marketing agencies, web design shops, and SEO consultants already following the company, up to which point 100 percent of revenue came from direct sales on inbound leads.

The standing advice was against it, and Roberge preserves the reasoning as a general caution: most start-ups find reselling attractive because someone else builds the sales force for you, but for most early start-ups it fails. SaaS executives on the advisory board warned that these partnerships take significant effort to establish, especially with resellers of any scale; that getting an agreement in place is only half the battle, since you must then capture the mindshare of the partner's sales team and train them, and your product is often lost among dozens or hundreds of others they are expected to resell. **The deeper objection is the lost feedback cycle.** It is rare for a start-up to nail product-market fit out of the gate, and the most valuable output of an early direct sales muscle is **not the early customers and revenue but the learnings from prospective buyers** — the more effectively the team understands the addressable market's needs and preferences and communicates them back so the company can iterate on product, pricing, and packaging, the more likely it is to reach product-market fit and accelerate. Partner salespeople are neither physically nor emotionally connected to your company and have little incentive to capture and relay that feedback.

The experiment design honored both the objection and the innovation culture. The gate: **if Pete hit 120 percent of his quarterly goal, he could start experimenting with VAR.** He did. The next quarter he kept full quota responsibility, received a bit of funding, and ran the experiment nights and weekends. The goal: explore whether VARs could acquire new customers at an attractive cost who would then be successful using the software. **Success was defined as five new customers acquired through one or more VARs, with all five having the software set up and logging into the platform at least once per week for the first month.** He hit it. The following quarter VAR became his full-time job with a marketing budget and a new set of goals; he hit those, and the budget was increased along with funding for two dedicated salespeople. The channel was then measured like the rest of the business — **LTV/CAC, payback period, salesperson productivity, customer retention**, and other metrics. **Six years later Pete oversaw a VAR-focused team of 100 cross-functional employees** responsible for a significant share of monthly new revenue. The lesson drawn: a simple, contained, relatively cheap bet with the potential for enormous gains.

**GPCT — iterating the qualifying matrix.** Diagnosis: with BANT in place, the **"N" (Need) had become the most important component of discovery** in that context, and salespeople who established a strong Need had very high lead-to-customer conversion rates. A well-developed Need sounded like: "The buyer is adding two salespeople in Q4 and needs to increase lead flow by 20 percent by the start of the quarter to support the expansion. If they do not, the new salespeople will resort to cold calling, which has been unsuccessful for the team in the past, and the expanded team will likely fail at accelerating customer acquisition." A poorly developed Need sounded like "the buyer needs more leads, just like everyone else" — to which the diagnostic questions are why, how many, how did they arrive at that number, what happens if they do not generate more leads, and where does increasing lead flow sit on their overall priority list. Not having those answers was a bad sign.

The replacement matrix, **GPCT (Goal, Plan, Challenges, Timeline)**, was built to describe what the most successful salespeople were already doing:

- **Goal** — the business goals the prospect's company is rallying around. Per Roberge's mentor John McMahon, **"a well-developed goal is quantified and implicated."** *Quantified* means a number is attached (increase lead flow by 20 percent). *Implicated* means you understand the consequence of missing it (new salespeople with no leads to call, the same potential revenue spread across a larger team, decreased salesperson productivity, likely failure to grow the business). Goal establishes how much impact you can have and how important achievement is to the buyer.
- **Plan** — the business plan in place to achieve the goal, which in that context meant the marketing strategy for increasing lead flow: more trade show presence, a direct mail campaign, more advertising spend, starting a blog, ramping cold calling. Uncovering the plan assesses whether it is realistic and whether you can help. Where the plan would work and you could help, opportunities converted well. Where the plan was likely to fail — the common example being buying a list of cold prospects and cold emailing them, which yields few leads *and* raises the likelihood the company's email lands in spam folders, damaging future sales and marketing — the salesperson had to educate the buyer on the danger and redirect them, a harder opportunity to navigate. The stated principle: customers could technically execute the bad plan using the software, but **selling on those grounds would produce a failed campaign and a bad customer experience**, so educating and redirecting was better for the customer.
- **Challenges** — the challenges of implementing the buyer's plan: does the company have the right staff, the necessary budget, gaps in software functionality or vendor capabilities that will hurt the plan. Understanding them establishes how you can most effectively help; where challenges remain that you cannot address directly, you can at least help find alternative solutions in the spirit of the relationship.
- **Timeline** — the date by which the buyer needs to achieve the goal, plus how the buyer arrived at that date, the consequences of missing it, and how your solution can accelerate achievement and how to communicate that effectively.

**The rollout was itself run as an experiment**, in deliberate contrast to the standard approach of announcing a new matrix at the annual sales meeting with a few hours of classroom training — after which, Roberge estimates, **20 percent of the team would still be using it six months later** and the rest would have settled back into old ways. The experiment's goal was to understand whether moving from BANT to GPCT would improve the discovery stage and thereby increase lead-to-customer conversion rates, salesperson productivity, and customer success. **Phase one taught GPCT to the five top salespeople, led by the head of sales training, Andrew Quinn, the best coach in the company, with success defined as a 10 percent increase in lead-to-customer conversion rates, salesperson productivity, and customer success within six months.** The team was deliberately star-studded to find a **true negative**: if that team under that coach could not improve with GPCT, rolling it out broadly was a bad idea; if it worked, it was worth testing on the next tier. For six months the group met a few evenings per week to discuss GPCT, **watched film of one another using it on live discovery calls**, reviewed their pipelines through the GPCT lens, established clear guidelines for defining the key elements of each stage, and developed the best questions for uncovering each element.

They beat the success metrics, and produced an unanticipated second outcome: **organic adoption**. The phase one salespeople were physically spread across the inside sales floor, so their neighbors overheard the qualifying calls, started asking about the new model, and word spread about GPCT, the evening sessions, and the team's results, with other salespeople asking the top performers to help them adopt it. **By the time GPCT was formally announced to the entire sales team, everyone had heard of it and 80 percent of the floor had already bought in and were using it.** Two payoffs versus the annual-kickoff rollout: a safe, low-risk way to test the framework's value, and a far more efficient path to floor-wide buy-in. Roberge notes both of these were among many experiments, with an even greater number of failures — the point of the culture being to let employees challenge the norm and fail without operational catastrophe.
