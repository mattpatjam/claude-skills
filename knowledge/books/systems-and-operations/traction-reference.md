# Traction — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing operating systems/processes, or advising on business systemization.*
*Book: "Traction: Get a Grip on Your Business" — Gino Wickman*

---

## 1. The Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS) and the Six Key Components

EOS is built on the premise that every organization, regardless of industry or size, is made of the same six underlying components. Strengthen all six and the common frustrations of running a business (lack of control, people problems, insufficient profit, a growth ceiling, and initiatives that never stick) fall away. Weaken any one of them and the whole system underperforms, because the six are interdependent rather than a menu to pick from.

The Six Key Components:

- **Vision** — Getting everyone in the organization 100% on the same page with where the business is going and how it will get there.
- **People** — Surrounding yourself with the right people (core value fit) in the right seats (skill and capacity fit).
- **Data** — Replacing subjective opinion, ego, and gut feel with a handful of objective numbers that give a weekly pulse on the business.
- **Issues** — Building the organizational discipline to identify, discuss, and permanently solve the obstacles blocking the vision.
- **Process** — Identifying and standardizing the core processes that make up "the way" the company does business, so results are consistent regardless of who executes.
- **Traction** — Bringing discipline and accountability so the vision actually gets executed, through 90-day priorities (Rocks) and a structured meeting rhythm (Meeting Pulse).

Each component has a companion tool: the Vision/Traction Organizer (Vision), the Accountability Chart plus People Analyzer plus GWC (People), the Scorecard (Data), the Issues List plus Issues Solving Track (Issues), the Three-Step Process Documenter (Process), and Rocks plus the Level 10 Meeting (Traction).

A core operating principle repeated throughout: **less is more**. Every tool in the system pushes toward fewer priorities, fewer numbers, fewer core values, fewer process steps — the discipline of the system is subtraction, not addition. "When everything is important, nothing is important."

The **Organizational Checkup** is a 20-statement, 1–5 self-rating questionnaire covering all six components (clarity of vision, core values, core focus, 10-year target, target market, differentiators, proven process, right people, Accountability Chart, right seats, leadership trust, Rocks, weekly meetings, meeting discipline, issue-solving, documented processes, customer/employee feedback systems, Scorecard, individual accountability numbers, and budget monitoring). Score bands: 20–34% ("This book will change your life"), 35–49% (normal, not great), 50–64% (above average), 65–79% (well above average), 80–100% (the target state — a "well-oiled machine"). The checkup should be retaken at least twice a year; the goal is steady incremental progress, not perfection. Nearly all organizations run below 50%; even 80% is considered excellent, since the work of running an EOS-based company is never fully complete.

## 2. Letting Go of the Vine — Preconditions for Change

Before any tool works, leadership has to accept four fundamental beliefs. This is framed as a leap of faith a founder/owner must take before the rest of the system can function:

1. **You must build and maintain a true leadership team.** A dictatorship (one person making every decision) is exhausting and caps growth — one person can only solve so many problems, and the organization is designed to crumble the moment that person steps aside. The alternative is a cohesive leadership team with clear accountabilities, open and honest debate, and a united front to the rest of the company. As the leadership team goes, so goes the company.
2. **Hitting the ceiling is inevitable.** Growth happens in a series of ceilings — organizational, departmental, and individual. An organization hits this because its existing structure, systems, or people reach capacity. Failure statistics on small businesses are cited (roughly half fail within five years by various studies) largely because they can't survive these growing pains.
3. **You can only run your business on one operating system.** Multiple frameworks, vocabularies, and planning rhythms running simultaneously (one person's "objectives" vs. another's "goals," monthly vs weekly priority-setting) create friction and waste. Pick one system and commit the whole organization to it.
4. **You must be open-minded, growth-oriented, and vulnerable.** Willingness to admit you don't know something, invite challenge, and let go of the need to always be right is a precondition — teams that aren't vulnerable or growth-oriented can't successfully do this work, no matter how good the tools are.

### The Five Leadership Abilities (needed to break through each ceiling)

1. **Simplify** — Streamline rules, processes, systems, and messaging. Use models, visuals, acronyms, and checklists. Complexity compounds as an organization grows, so leaders must actively fight it. ("No further progress and growth is possible for an organization until a new state of simplicity is created" — Dan Sullivan.)
2. **Delegate and elevate** — Hand off responsibilities you've personally outgrown to people who have the skill and enthusiasm to own them, freeing yourself to operate at your highest-value contribution (your "Unique Ability"). Growth under you becomes growth of the company once you let go.
3. **Predict** — Both long-term (90 days and beyond: 10-year target, 3-year picture, 1-year plan, next-quarter priorities, built by "climbing the tree" to see where the business needs to go) and short-term (the daily/weekly problems that, if not resolved permanently, get patched with "duct tape and twine" and eventually cause implosion).
4. **Systemize** — Identify the handful of core processes that make the organization function (a marketing process, sales process, operating process, customer-retention process, accounting process, HR process, etc.), name them consistently, and integrate them into one functioning machine — "your Way."
5. **Structure** — Organize the company to reduce complexity and create accountability, using a proper Accountability Chart rather than a structure shaped by ego, personality, or fear of losing people.

## 3. The Vision Component and the Vision/Traction Organizer (V/TO)

The core failure mode this component addresses: leaders can see their own vision clearly but wrongly assume everyone else in the organization sees the same picture. Clarifying vision is the starting point for every other component, because clear vision drives better decisions on people, process, finances, strategy, and customers.

The **Vision/Traction Organizer (V/TO)** is a two-page tool that distills a company's entire vision and short-term plan into eight questions. It deliberately rejects long-form business plans in favor of extreme simplicity (the book traces this lineage through the "Life Business Management Plan," Verne Harnish's "One-Page Strategic Plan," and Jim Horan's "One Page Business Plan" — all built on the insight that useful planning documents get shorter, not longer, as they mature).

### The Eight Questions

1. **What are your core values?** A small set (3–7) of timeless guiding principles that define culture and identity. Process for finding them: (1) each leadership member names three people who, if cloned, would create market domination; (2) list the characteristics those people embody; (3) narrow the long list to 5–15 by circling/striking/combining; (4) debate down to the final 3–7. Once defined, core values must be actively used to hire, fire, review, reward, and recognize — otherwise they're decoration, not culture. A recommended discipline: let the first draft list "simmer" for 30 days before finalizing. Values should be written and delivered as a "core values speech" with 3–5 concrete supporting stories per value, and repeated at every hiring conversation, quarterly all-company meeting, and performance review.

2. **What is your core focus?** (Also called mission, niche, sweet spot, hedgehog concept, unique ability, "the zone," or "voice" depending on the source theory cited.) Composed of two parts:
   - **Purpose/cause/passion** — why the organization exists, stated in 3–7 words, simple, bold, emotionally resonant, not about money, and portable across industries (it shouldn't reveal what business you're in).
   - **Niche** — the one thing the organization does better than anyone; a filtering mechanism for every future decision ("Do one thing and do it better than anyone").
   Distraction from core focus ("shiny stuff") is one of the most common causes of stalled growth; the antidote is ruthless focus on the existing "sweet spot," which the book likens to the sweet spot on a golf club face — hit it consistently and results compound.

3. **What is your 10-year target?** A single, specific, measurable long-range goal (akin to a BHAG — Big Hairy Audacious Goal, per Collins & Porras) that everyone in the organization is working toward. Ten years is the most common horizon EOS clients choose, but 5–20 years is acceptable. Rule of thumb: once you're three years from hitting it, retire it into the three-year picture and set a new one.

4. **What is your marketing strategy?** Four elements:
   - **Target Market ("The List")** — a specific, filtered definition of the ideal customer by geographic, demographic, and psychographic traits, converted into an actual named/contactable prospect list. Replaces a "shotgun" approach with a "rifle" approach.
   - **Three Uniques** — the combination of three differentiators that, together, no competitor replicates. Individual uniques can overlap with competitors; the specific combination of three cannot.
   - **Proven Process** — the company's own named, visually illustrated, repeatable process for doing business with a customer (typically 3–7 major steps), used as a sales tool to build prospect confidence and internally to clarify how each role affects the customer.
   - **Guarantee** — a customer-facing commitment addressing the industry's biggest service/quality fear, backed by a tangible penalty if not honored (e.g., "four hours or it's free"). Not every business needs one — roughly half of EOS clients don't have one — but a strong guarantee closes more business and forces internal excellence to back it up.

5. **What is your three-year picture?** A concrete, specific snapshot (revenue, profit, a measurable indicator of scale, plus 10–20 bullet points describing what the organization looks like) painted three years out. Purpose: let people "see" the destination and decide if they want to be part of it, and make one-year planning easier by working backward from a fixed point.

6. **What is your one-year plan?** Revenue/profit/measurable targets plus three to seven specific, measurable, attainable priorities for the year, tied to a real budget. "Sales" is not a specific goal; "$1 million in new sales" is.

7. **What are your quarterly Rocks?** The organization's top three-to-seven priorities for the next 90 days (full mechanics in Section 8, Traction Component).

8. **What are your issues?** A running list of everything currently in the way of the vision — organizational, personal, strategic, or tactical — captured honestly rather than solved on the spot (solving happens via the Issues Solving Track, Section 6).

### Sharing the Vision ("Shared by All")

A completed V/TO is worthless if only the leadership team has seen it. Communicate it via:

- A company kickoff meeting unveiling the V/TO and delivering the core values speech, with time for questions.
- A quarterly (≤45-minute) state-of-the-company meeting with a fixed three-part agenda: where you've been, where you are, where you're going — plus newly set company Rocks.
- A full V/TO review each quarter as part of setting new Rocks.

Cited research (Harris Interactive/FranklinCovey poll of 23,000+ employees): 37% didn't understand their company's priorities; only 1 in 5 was enthusiastic about the organization's goals or saw a clear line between their own tasks and those goals. Rule of thumb: **people need to hear the vision seven times before they truly internalize it** — repetition that feels redundant to leadership is usually still below the threshold for the rest of the org.

## 4. The People Component: Right People, Right Seats

Formula: **Core Values + People Analyzer = Right People.** **Unique Ability + Accountability Chart = Right Seats.**

- **Right People** = people who share the company's core values, fit the culture, and are enjoyable to work with — independent of skill.
- **Right Seat** = a person operating inside their Unique Ability (the intersection of skill and passion), matched to a role defined by a properly structured Accountability Chart.

Two failure patterns to distinguish, each requiring a different fix:
- **Right person, wrong seat** — someone who shares core values but is in a role too big, too small, or misaligned with their strengths (often because they were promoted for tenure/loyalty rather than fit). Fix: move them to the right seat; if none exists, this becomes one of the hardest but necessary decisions.
- **Wrong person, right seat** — someone highly skilled and productive who does not share core values. They quietly damage the organization through politics, dishonesty, or corrosive behavior. Fix: they must go regardless of their output, because the long-term cultural cost outweighs short-term performance.
- (A third, simpler case: wrong person, wrong seat — an unambiguous termination.)

### The People Analyzer

A grid tool: list people down the left column, core values across the top, and rate each person's adherence to each value using three grades: **+** (exhibits it most of the time), **+/-** (inconsistent), **-** (doesn't exhibit it most of the time). The organization sets "the bar" — the minimum acceptable combination (a commonly cited example: three pluses, two plus/minuses, never a minus, for five core values). Anyone at or above the bar is a "right person."

**The Three-Strike Rule** for people below the bar:
- Strike One — discuss the gap and expectations; give 30 days to improve.
- Strike Two — if no improvement, repeat with another 30 days.
- Strike Three — termination, since the pattern shows the person won't change.

In practice, most people who don't fit self-select out once core values are made explicit and visible (via the values speech, quarterly meetings, reviews) — they rarely make it to strike three.

**36 Hours of Pain** — a naming convention for the emotional cost of a necessary termination: the anguish leading up to and including the conversation is real but brief; the ongoing cost of *not* acting (a full year of tension, in the book's example) is far larger. "It's less important what you decide than that you decide" applies here too.

### The Accountability Chart

Not a conventional org chart — it starts from the belief that every organization, regardless of size or industry, runs on **three major functions**: Sales/Marketing, Operations, and Finance/Administration. Rules:

- All three functions must be strong; weakness in any one undermines the whole (illustrated through three scenarios: strong sales + weak ops = customers won through the front door and lost out the back; strong sales + strong ops + weak finance = money flows in and right back out uncontrolled; strong ops/finance + weak sales = idle capability waiting for demand that never arrives).
- **Only one person can be accountable for each major function.** Two names in one box means no one is actually accountable.
- A fourth function usually emerges above these three: the **Integrator** — the person who resolves the natural friction between the three functions and runs day-to-day operations, profit/loss, and special projects. Operates more on logic.
- About half of organizations also have a **Visionary** function above the integrator — typically the founder/owner, driven by ideas (roughly "10 ideas a week, one of them great"), culture, and major relationships. Operates more on emotion. In partnerships, one partner is commonly the visionary and the other the integrator; naming this dynamic explicitly (rather than leaving it as unspoken tension) is described as transformative for founder relationships.
- Functions typically expand to 3–10 boxes depending on organization size (sales/marketing may split into distinct sales and marketing seats; operations into delivery/project management/customer service; finance/admin into finance, admin, IT, HR). No EOS client example in the book exceeds seven major functions on the front line.
- Each function/seat is defined by roughly **five major roles** — bulleted responsibilities, not a narrative job description.
- **LMA** (Lead, Manage, hold Accountable) is the responsibility carried by anyone with direct reports.
- The chart evolves as the company grows — a company growing at ~20%/year should expect to revise it roughly every 90 days. It is also **scalable**: where multiple people hold the same seat (e.g., 8 customer service reps), list the headcount rather than drawing multiple boxes.
- One name can occupy two seats (common in small/early-stage companies); two names cannot occupy one seat.
- Confirm a completed Accountability Chart by asking three questions: (1) Is this the right structure to get us to the next level? (2) Are the right people in the right seats? (3) Does everyone have enough time to do the job well? A "yes" on all three signals 100%.

### GWC — Get it, Want it, Capacity to do it

The filter for confirming someone is in the right seat, applied as strict yes/no (not graded like core values):

- **Get it** — genuinely understands the role, culture, systems, pace, and how the job fits together.
- **Want it** — authentically wants to do this job, not out of obligation, ego, or a manager's wishful thinking.
- **Capacity** — has the time, and the mental/physical/emotional bandwidth, and the requisite skill/intellect/emotional intelligence to do it well. Related to the Peter Principle (people promoted to their level of incompetence).

A "no" on any one of the three means it is not the right seat for that person, regardless of how much you like them or how long they've been there. Roughly 80% of client leadership teams change within the first two years of the process — meaning the team you start with is rarely the team you end up with.

**Delegate and Elevate** operationally: everyone has 100% of their available working capacity (which varies by person — 40 hours for some, 70 for others). When someone is required to operate above 100%, something must be delegated to a person in the seat below, freeing the first person to move closer to their Unique Ability. Referenced concept: "the monkey" (from *The One Minute Manager Meets the Monkey*) — when a direct report brings you their problem, don't let them leave it in your office; they should walk out still owning it.

## 5. The Data Component: The Scorecard

Core metaphor: a pilot flying blind with broken gauges but staying "optimistic" — most businesses are run this way, on subjective impression rather than data. A profit & loss statement is a **trailing indicator**; by the time it shows a problem, it's too late to prevent it. The goal of this component is to replace gut-feel management with a small set of forward-looking, activity-based numbers reviewed weekly.

### The Scorecard

A weekly report of **5–15 numbers** (aim closer to 5) that together give an accurate pulse on the business. Construction process:
1. Leadership team brainstorms, as if isolated with no other information source, every number needed to know the business is healthy (weekly revenue, cash balance, sales activity, customer satisfaction/problems, AR/AP, project/production status, etc.).
2. For each number, name the single person accountable for delivering it (the function head, not the person who merely enters data).
3. Set a weekly goal figure for each number, tied to the one-year plan.
4. Track dates going forward; roll the sheet so 13 weeks (one quarter) are visible at a time, preserving history as older weeks roll off.
5. Assign someone to collect and compile the Scorecard weekly.
6. Review it every week without fail.

**Three Scorecard rules of thumb:**
1. Numbers must be **weekly, activity-based leading indicators**, not high-level lagging P&L figures. To find them, trace your sales or service process back to its earliest measurable step (leads → contacts → appointments → proposals → closes) so problems show up before they hit revenue.
2. The Scorecard is proactive, not a replacement for reviewing monthly/quarterly financials and a budget.
3. **Red-flag** any number that misses its weekly goal (visually, e.g. shading it red) to create focus and urgency in the weekly meeting.

A Scorecard typically needs about three months of iteration before it's ~100% right; an 85%-correct first draft is considered a good starting point.

### Everyone Has a Number

Complete mastery of the Data Component means every individual — not just departments — has one meaningful, manageable number they own weekly (the book's example: a receptionist's number was "two," as in "two rings good, three rings bad"). Eight cited advantages of giving everyone a number:

1. Cuts through vague, subjective communication between manager and report.
2. Creates clear accountability (a number leaves no gray area).
3. Right people in the right seats *appreciate* clarity and being held to numbers — resistance to measurement is itself often a signal of a wrong-person or wrong-seat problem.
4. Creates clarity and genuine commitment (versus vague, unmeasurable expectations).
5. Creates healthy competition.
6. Produces results (what gets watched improves).
7. Creates teamwork and peer accountability.
8. Lets you solve problems faster, because an off-track leading number is caught while there's still time to act.

A good source of candidate numbers: the Accountability Chart. Of the ~five roles listed per seat, one to three are usually directly measurable.

## 6. The Issues Component: The Issues List and the Issues Solving Track (IDS)

Premise: strengthening Vision, People, and Data creates organizational transparency — issues that were previously hidden get smoked out. The differentiator between successful and unsuccessful organizations isn't the *number* of issues (every organization has largely the same recurring handful, historically), it's the discipline to solve them permanently rather than let them fester ("problems are like mushrooms: in the dark, they multiply").

### Prerequisite: an open, honest culture

Trust is the foundation issue-solving depends on. Leadership sets the tone by admitting its own mistakes first; if the leadership team isn't open and honest, the organization underneath it never will be.

### Three types of Issues Lists, by scope and time horizon

1. **The V/TO Issues List** — company-level issues that can wait beyond 90 days (new product ideas, HR policy gaps, capital needs, etc.), addressed in quarterly/annual sessions.
2. **The weekly leadership team Issues List** — strategic issues for the current week/quarter (off-track Rocks, a bad Scorecard number, key employee issues, major client problems), resolved in the weekly Level 10 Meeting.
3. **The departmental Issues List** — local, tactical issues resolved in departmental weekly meetings; anything that can be solved at this level should not be escalated up.

Note also three *types* of individual issues that surface: (1) a true problem to solve, (2) information that needs to be communicated/agreed on, (3) an idea/opportunity needing feedback or a go/no-go decision. Naming which type it is up front makes resolution faster.

### The Issues Solving Track (IDS)

Three steps, always taken in this order, and always tackled **in priority order** (not top-to-bottom on the list, since solving the top issue often makes several lower ones vanish as symptoms):

1. **Identify** — Dig past the stated symptom to the real root cause. This step often consumes the most time; once the real issue is named, discussing and solving it can take minutes. (Illustrative example: a "customers are too demanding" complaint traced down through communication, then process, ultimately to one employee being in the wrong seat.)
2. **Discuss** — Get every opinion, fact, and concern on the table once each (saying the same thing twice is "politicking," not discussion). Watch for tangents — a common failure mode where teams drift entirely off-topic; a suggested discipline is calling "Tangent Alert!" the moment it happens and parking any real-but-irrelevant issue on the list for its proper turn.
3. **Solve** — Land on a decision, typically converted into a To-Do (action item with a single owner and a short deadline). A clear "solution statement" is spoken aloud so the whole team can confirm agreement before moving on.

### The Ten Commandments of Solving Issues

1. Thou shalt not rule by consensus — someone must make the final call; unanimous agreement on major decisions is rare and consensus-seeking becomes paralysis.
2. Thou shalt not be a weenie — solutions are usually simple, just not easy; have the resolve to act.
3. Thou shalt be decisive — speed of decision matters as much as, or more than, being right every time.
4. Thou shalt not rely on secondhand information — if an issue involves people not in the room, convene them (a "pow-wow") before deciding.
5. Thou shalt fight for the greater good — set aside ego, department turf, and personal preference.
6. Thou shalt not try to solve them all — take issues one at a time in priority order; total resolution of every issue every week is not the goal.
7. Thou shalt live with it, end it, or change it — the only three options for any issue; if you can't end or change it, stop complaining and consciously choose to live with it.
8. Thou shalt choose short-term pain and suffering — resolve now rather than let pain compound (see "36 hours of pain").
9. Thou shalt enter the danger — the issue you fear discussing most is the one most needing discussion.
10. Thou shalt take a shot — propose a solution rather than waiting for someone else to; the team will correct you if you're wrong.

### The Personal Issues Solving Session

For interpersonal friction blocking team health: (1) each person shares what they believe are the other's three greatest strengths and three greatest weaknesses; (2) list and solve all issues between them; (3) capture action items; (4) reconvene in 30 days to confirm follow-through. Resolves the tension roughly 9 times out of 10; when it doesn't, one person may need to leave the team for the group's health.

## 7. The Process Component: Documenting and Following "The Way"

Central idea: consistency is the prerequisite for everything else — "nothing can be fine-tuned until it's first consistent." Process is described as the most neglected of the six components, largely because it's invisible until documented, and entrepreneurs assume that keeping it in their heads is sufficient. A company typically runs on **about seven core processes**: HR, marketing, sales, one-to-three operations processes (varies by business), accounting, and customer retention.

### Stage 1: Identify your core processes

Done together as a leadership team (not delegated to one person) so everyone agrees on the same names for the same processes — skipping this step and documenting ad hoc procedures first (as one client did, producing 100 disconnected documents) wastes effort that reorganizing around named core processes later would have saved.

### Stage 2: Document each core process

Owned by whoever is accountable for that function per the Accountability Chart. Apply the **20/80 rule**: document the 20% of steps that produce 80% of the result, at a high level (major steps, each with 2–5 bullet sub-points), not an exhaustive procedural manual — a fully detailed process might run 30 pages; the useful 20/80 version runs about 6. The actual failure mode being solved is people *skipping steps*, not lacking exhaustive documentation. Turn steps into checklists wherever possible, since checklists (cited: pilots, healthcare) measurably increase consistency and quality control. Apply technology deliberately — only where it improves the process, not for its own sake (illustrated by a cautionary "$45,000 Mistake": custom software built to solve a problem later solved by a $500 off-the-shelf tool).

### Stage 3: Package it

Compile all documented processes into one binder or intranet resource, titled "[Company Name] Way," with the process names forming the table of contents.

### Followed by All

Documentation alone changes nothing; the process must be trained and enforced. This requires the leadership team to actually follow it themselves first ("do as I say, not as I do" fails here more than anywhere else in the system) and to make the value visible to employees — e.g. via a visual model (the book cites a "Circle of Life" illustration showing how each person's adherence to their step affects everyone downstream, and how skipped steps ripple as damage).

Payoff: a fully documented and followed set of processes increases the company's actual sale value (illustrated by acquirers repeatedly describing well-systemized EOS companies as "the best-run small company we've ever seen") and gives the owner real optionality — grow it, step back from it, sell it, or franchise/duplicate it.

## 8. The Traction Component: Rocks and the Meeting Pulse

Vision without traction is "merely hallucination." This is the component that converts a clear vision into daily execution, via two disciplines: **Rocks** (90-day priorities) and a **Meeting Pulse** (a structured weekly/quarterly/annual meeting rhythm). Both require full leadership commitment — half-measures fail.

### Rocks: the 90-Day World

Human beings, by nature, lose focus roughly every 90 days — this is presented as the reason for the quarter-length cadence, not an arbitrary business convention. A Rock is the equivalent of "picking to the stick" — breaking an overwhelming long-range goal into a visible, reachable interim target.

Rules for Rocks:
- **Three to seven company Rocks per quarter** (fewer is better); everyone below leadership level should carry only **one to three**.
- A Rock must be **specific, measurable, and attainable** — "Close three core accounts" is a Rock; "Start working on the Customer Service Process" is not (too vague to know when it's done).
- **One owner per Rock.** Shared ownership means no ownership.
- It takes roughly **two quarters to master** Rock-setting; expect early Rocks to be set too ambitiously and completion rates around 50% before the team calibrates to a sustainable ~80%.

**Process for establishing Rocks** (repeated every quarter):
1. List everything that could conceivably need doing in the next 90 days (commonly 10–20+ items).
2. Debate and cut: keep, kill, or combine each item until only 3–7 company Rocks remain.
3. Set a hard due date (end of quarter) and define each Rock so there's zero ambiguity about "done" vs. "not done."
4. Assign a single owner per Rock.
5. Each leadership member sets their own 3–7 individual Rocks, incorporating any company Rocks they own.
6. Compile into a single-page **Rock Sheet** (company Rocks at top, individual Rocks below) used at every weekly meeting. Once set for the quarter, no new priorities may be added mid-quarter — new ideas go on the Issues List for next quarter's consideration.
7. Share company Rocks with the whole organization at the quarterly state-of-the-company meeting.
8. Roll the same process down to every department, and eventually every individual employee.

**Rock traps:** garbage in/garbage out (a poorly-set Rock wastes the whole quarter); rushing the two-quarter learning curve; "commitment fizzle" (dropping the discipline once initial enthusiasm fades); giving non-leadership employees too many Rocks (overload).

### The Meeting Pulse

The organization's heartbeat — a fixed, non-negotiable meeting rhythm at three altitudes: annual, quarterly, weekly.

**Annual Meeting Pulse** — leadership team, off-site, two days, once a year.
- *Day One:* Segue (three greatest company wins + one personal win + expectations for the session) → Review previous year's numbers and goals in strict done/not-done terms → Team health-building exercise (the book's recommended version, "One Thing": each person receives peer feedback on their single greatest strength and single greatest weakness, then commits to one change) → SWOT analysis, converted into that session's Issues List → Full V/TO review through the one-year plan, with the three-year picture rebuilt from scratch each year (not merely edited) since a year's worth of learning should sharpen the vision.
- *Day Two:* Establish next quarter's Rocks → Tackle key issues (via IDS) → Next steps → Conclude (feedback + meeting rating out of 10, target 8+).

**Quarterly Meeting Pulse** — leadership team, off-site, eight hours, every 90 days.
- Agenda: Segue → Review previous quarter (numbers + Rock completion, stated only as done/not done, target ≥80%) → Review the V/TO (30 min–2 hrs, resolve any disagreement on the spot) → Establish next quarter's Rocks → Tackle key issues (IDS, using remaining time) → Next steps → Conclude (rating out of 10).
- The "buildup" effect: prescheduling the quarterly meeting causes people to unconsciously prepare for it over the preceding weeks, which is part of why it works — springing a full-day session on people without advance notice is markedly less effective.
- For incomplete Rocks, only three explanations are considered legitimate: carry it forward, treat the remaining 5% as a to-do, or reassign it — the two root causes to diagnose are over-committing (poor prediction) or someone not giving their full effort (an accountability issue for the Issues List).

**Weekly Meeting Pulse — the Level 10 Meeting.** Leadership team, same conference room, **90 minutes**, same day and same time every single week (the only acceptable reasons to skip: vacation or death — the meeting still happens without an absent member).

Fixed agenda:
- Segue — 5 min (share good news, disconnect from the week)
- Scorecard — 5 min (report only; anything off-track drops to IDS, no discussion here)
- Rock Review — 5 min ("on track" / "off track" only, per Rock; anything off-track or needing discussion drops to IDS)
- Customer/Employee Headlines — 5 min (brief good/bad news; anything requiring discussion drops to IDS)
- To-Do List — 5 min (done/not done review of prior week's 7-day action items; an item should not survive on this list more than two weeks)
- **IDS — 60 minutes** (the bulk of the meeting; work the Issues List in priority order using Identify-Discuss-Solve, producing new to-dos)
- Conclude — 5 min (recap new to-dos; decide what needs communicating to the wider org and how; rate the meeting 1–10, target 8+)

Two required meeting roles: someone runs the meeting (keeps the group moving through the agenda), and someone manages the agenda document (keeps the Scorecard, Rock Sheet, To-Do List, and Issues List current and in front of everyone).

**The Five Points of a healthy Weekly Meeting Pulse** — meetings must (1) be the same day each week, (2) the same time each week, (3) use the same printed agenda, (4) start on time, and (5) end on time. Starting late is called out specifically because the time lost always comes out of the IDS segment — the part of the meeting that matters most.

Departmental weekly meetings follow once the leadership team has mastered its own rhythm (commonly ~3 months), typically running 30–60 minutes, with at least half the time still reserved for issue-solving.

## 9. Implementation Sequence — Rolling EOS Out

The book explicitly separates the *order in which concepts are taught* (Vision → People → Data → Issues → Process → Traction, for comprehension) from the *order in which tools should actually be implemented* (traction and accountability first, vision last), because building accountability before vision-setting produces sharper, more honest strategic conversations later. Recommended implementation order, always starting with the leadership team alone before rolling to the rest of the company:

1. **Accountability Chart** (with People Analyzer and GWC) — establishes who owns what before anything else is layered on, surfacing structural and personnel issues immediately.
2. **Rocks** — focuses the newly-accountable team on the next 90 days.
3. **Meeting Pulse** (including IDS, the Level 10 Meeting, quarterlies, annuals) — the weekly Level 10 Meeting is typically uncomfortable for 4–8 weeks before it becomes habitual.
4. **Scorecard** — takes one to three months to mature into a genuinely predictive tool.
5. **V/TO** (core values, core focus, 10-year target, marketing strategy, three-year picture, one-year plan) — deliberately built on top of an already-accountable, already-disciplined team, so the vision work is grounded rather than aspirational fluff.
6. **Three-Step Process Documenter** — typically a 6–12 month effort to document and fully train the organization's core processes; can start earlier if the leadership team has spare capacity.
7. **Everyone Has a Number** — usually the last tool to land well, since it depends on the accountability and management discipline built by the first six.

The **first five tools** (Accountability Chart, Rocks, Meeting Pulse, Scorecard, V/TO) are called the **EOS Foundational Tools**, said to produce roughly 80% of the total results of the system.

Rollout guidance:
- Introduce tools **one organizational tier at a time**, starting with direct reports of the leadership team, expanding outward.
- Every organization moves at its own pace; company size and number of hierarchical tiers both slow the process (a cited range: fastest full rollout in 6 months for a 50-person company, slowest in over 3 years for a 70-person company). A rough average is about one year for the foundational tools to be fully absorbed top to bottom.
- Multi-location businesses need on-site leadership fully bought in at each location before pushing further down that location's tiers.
- A recognizable "click" moment tends to occur somewhere between 6 and 36 months in, where the leadership team suddenly sees how all six components interlock as one system rather than a set of disconnected exercises.

## 10. Recurring Operating Disciplines and Cautions

A set of practices that don't belong to a single component but recur throughout as maintenance disciplines for a mature EOS company:

- **Same-page meetings** — for partnerships or visionary/integrator pairs specifically: a recurring (e.g. monthly) private meeting to surface and resolve tension before it reaches employees, since a misaligned leadership pair is sensed by the organization even when unspoken. If irreconcilable after genuine effort (cited as roughly 5% of cases), the healthiest long-term outcome may be formally parting ways.
- **Clarity breaks** — regularly scheduled solo thinking time, away from the office, with no "catch-up" busywork allowed — used to review the V/TO, think strategically, or simply sit with a blank pad. Framed as time that pays for itself many times over in decision quality.
- **Compartmentalizing** — a discipline for keeping the whole system simple in practice: anything longer-term than 90 days goes on the V/TO Issues List; anything due this year is a goal; anything due this quarter needing weeks/months is a Rock; anything arising this week needing resolution goes on the weekly Issues List; anything due within 1–2 weeks is a to-do. Every input has exactly one correct home.
- **Guarding against "shiny stuff"** — the recurring temptation (especially for visionary personality types) to chase new markets, products, or ventures outside the defined core focus. Two sanctioned outlets: (1) channel restlessness into deepening the existing core focus (new applications of it, client/employee interviews, testing current offerings), or (2) if a new idea is genuinely outside core focus and worth pursuing, spin it out as a fully separate company with its own dedicated resources rather than diluting the current one (illustrated by a company that nearly stalled trying to run two different business models inside one structure, then fixed it by separating them and installing a separate integrator for each).
- **Bigger isn't always better** — growth for growth's sake is treated as a common strategic mistake; the book explicitly frames profitability and fit with the owners' Unique Ability as more important than scale (posing the comparison: a $10M company at 20% margin nets the same as a $100M company at 2% margin, with far less complexity).
- **"Garbage in, garbage out"** applies system-wide, not just to Rocks — every EOS tool only returns value proportional to the honesty and effort put into it.
- **Kanter's Law**, cited directly: "Everything can look like a failure in the middle" — a caution against abandoning the system during the disorienting middle stretch where old problems are surfacing faster than they're being resolved.
