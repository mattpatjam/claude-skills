# Drive — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing hiring processes, or advising on team-building.*
*Book: "Drive: The Surprising Truth About What Motivates Us" — Daniel H. Pink*

---

## 1. The Three Drives and the Motivation Operating Systems (1.0, 2.0, 3.0)

Pink frames motivation as a societal "operating system" built on assumptions about human behavior. Three drives power behavior:

- **Drive 1 — Biological:** hunger, thirst, sex. Survival. The basis of **Motivation 1.0** (worked for early humans, inadequate once complex societies formed and drives had to be restrained).
- **Drive 2 — Reward and punishment:** seek reward, avoid punishment. The basis of **Motivation 2.0**: reward the behavior you want, punish what you don't. Codified by Frederick Taylor's "scientific management" (workers as parts in a machine, responding rationally to external forces). It powered the Industrial Revolution and still dominates how organizations run.
- **Drive 3 — Intrinsic motivation:** the inherent satisfaction of the activity itself. Discovered by Harry Harlow (1949: eight rhesus monkeys solved mechanical puzzles unprompted, with focus and apparent enjoyment; solving two-thirds of them in under 60 seconds by day 13-14; introducing raisins as a reward *disrupted* performance, producing more errors and fewer solutions). Confirmed by Edward Deci (1969 Soma cube experiments: the group paid $1 per configuration played hardest during the paid session, then in the unpaid third session played *less* than they had before ever being paid; unpaid controls kept playing). Deci's conclusion: "When money is used as an external reward for some activity, the subjects lose intrinsic interest for the activity." Rewards give a caffeine-like short-term jolt, then wear off and reduce longer-term motivation. This third drive is the basis of **Motivation 3.0**.

Motivation 2.0 got a minor patch (call it 2.1) via Maslow's humanistic psychology and McGregor's Theory Y (relaxed dress codes, flexible schedules, "empowerment"), but Pink argues those refinements are a modest improvement, not an upgrade. Motivation 3.0 is the full upgrade: built on autonomy, mastery, and purpose.

Underlying theory: Deci and Ryan's **self-determination theory (SDT)**. It starts from universal human *needs*, not tendencies: three innate psychological needs — competence, autonomy, and relatedness. When satisfied, people are motivated, productive, and happy. When thwarted, motivation, productivity, and happiness plummet. The third drive is universal but fragile: whether it emerges depends on whether conditions support it. Ryan on the standard management response to underperformance: rewards/punishments are used instead of "the hard work of diagnosing what the problem is." Deci: rewards are necessary in workplaces, but "the less salient they are made, the better."

## 2. Why Motivation 2.0 Is Breaking: Three Incompatibility Problems

Carrot-and-stick logic is increasingly incompatible with modern work in three ways:

- **How we organize what we do.** Open source runs on intrinsic motivation: Wikipedia beat Microsoft's paid, professionally managed Encarta (Encarta shut down Oct 31, 2009; Wikipedia hit 13M articles in ~260 languages within 8 years). Firefox 150M+ users, Linux one in four corporate servers, Apache 52% of the corporate web server market — all volunteer-built. Lakhani and Wolf's survey of 684 open-source developers: "enjoyment-based intrinsic motivation," how creative a person feels on the project, is the strongest and most pervasive driver; most reported frequently reaching flow. New legal forms — L3Cs (low-profit limited liability corporations, first in Vermont 2008), Yunus's "social businesses," for-benefit organizations (Mozilla is one), B Corporations — are **purpose maximizers**, which flout Motivation 2.0's core principles.
- **How we think about what we do.** Behavioral economics (Kahneman's 2002 Nobel, Ariely) killed the rational wealth-maximizing calculator: in ultimatum-game replications worldwide, most people reject offers of $2 and below even though $2 > $0. Extrinsic incentives assume rational response; humans routinely don't deliver it. Bruno Frey's extension (*Homo Oeconomicus Maturus*): "It is inconceivable that people are motivated solely or even mainly by external incentives." If we're predictably irrational, we can also be predictably purpose-seeking.
- **How we do what we do.** Work splits into **algorithmic** tasks (follow established instructions down a single path to one conclusion) and **heuristic** tasks (no algorithm exists; you must experiment and devise a novel solution). Routine algorithmic work — including white-collar work in accounting, law, programming — is being offshored and automated. McKinsey estimate: only 30% of US job growth now comes from algorithmic work, 70% from heuristic work. Amabile's intrinsic motivation principle of creativity: intrinsic motivation is conducive to creativity, controlling extrinsic motivation is detrimental to it — so Motivation 2.0's central tenets can impair exactly the work modern economies depend on. Add flatter organizations, 18M+ US non-employer businesses, and tens of millions of telecommuters: nobody is watching, so work now depends on self-direction. One business leader's hiring line: "If you need me to motivate you, I probably don't want to hire you."

## 3. The Sawyer Effect and Baseline Rewards

Two foundational concepts that run through everything else:

- **Baseline rewards first.** Salary, contract payments, benefits, and a few perks are the floor. If baseline rewards aren't adequate or *equitable*, the person's focus goes to the unfairness of the situation and the anxiety of the circumstance — you get neither extrinsic nor intrinsic motivation, you get very little motivation at all. Pay well, pay fairly, then take money off the table.
- **The Sawyer Effect** (from Tom Sawyer's fence-whitewashing): rewards perform behavioral alchemy in both directions. Negative side: external rewards turn play into work. Positive side: the current can run in reverse — focusing on mastery, variety, and self-chosen challenge turns work into play.

Key mechanism: only **contingent, expected, "if-then" rewards** ("if you do this, then you get that") do the damage, because they require people to forfeit some autonomy, draining the activity of enjoyment. Classic evidence: Lepper/Greene/Nisbett preschool drawing study — children promised a "Good Player" certificate for drawing showed much less interest in drawing two weeks later; unexpected-reward and no-reward children drew as much as before. Deci's 1999 reanalysis of 128 experiments confirmed that tangible rewards tend to have a substantially negative effect on intrinsic motivation, and that institutions opting for short-term behavioral control do considerable long-term damage. Pink calls this one of the most robust findings in social science — and one of the most ignored.

## 4. The Seven Deadly Flaws of Carrots and Sticks

If-then rewards, applied to interesting or creative work, reliably backfire. The seven flaws, each backed by research:

1. **They can extinguish intrinsic motivation** (Deci's Soma experiments; the preschool drawing study).
2. **They can diminish performance.** Ariely's Madurai, India study (87 participants; rewards of 4, 40, or 400 rupees — a day's, two weeks', and nearly five months' local pay): the medium group did no better than the small, and the super-incentivized 400-rupee group fared worst of all. "In eight of the nine tasks we examined across the three experiments, higher incentives led to worse performance." 2009 LSE review of 51 corporate pay-for-performance studies: financial incentives "can result in a negative impact on overall performance."
3. **They can crush creativity.** Glucksberg's candle problem (a heuristic task requiring overcoming functional fixedness): incentivized participants took on average ~3.5 minutes *longer*, because rewards narrow focus and blinker the peripheral thinking novel solutions need. Amabile: blind expert panels rated commissioned artwork significantly less creative than noncommissioned (equal technical quality); a 20-year follow-up of Art Institute of Chicago students found the *least* extrinsically motivated artists had the most professional success — "It is those who are least motivated to pursue extrinsic rewards who eventually receive them."
4. **They can crowd out good behavior.** Swedish blood-donation field experiment (153 women): 52% donated when unpaid; offering ~50 kronor cut donation to 30% (payment tainted an altruistic act); adding the option to donate the fee to charity restored it to 53%. Contrast: Italy giving donors paid time off *increased* donations — it removed an obstacle to altruism rather than pricing the act.
5. **They can encourage cheating, shortcuts, and unethical behavior.** Goals imposed by others narrow focus so much that some people take the low road (Sears auto-repair quotas → overcharging; Enron revenue targets; the Ford Pinto). The goals-gone-wild scholars: goal setting should be "prescribed selectively, presented with a warning label, and closely monitored." Self-set mastery goals are usually healthy; imposed sales targets, quarterly numbers, and test scores carry the side effects. When the reward is the activity itself, there are no shortcuts. Sticks fail symmetrically: the Haifa day-care study — introducing a fine for late pickup produced a steady *increase* in lateness, settling at almost twice the original rate, because the fine converted a moral obligation (be fair to the teachers) into a purchasable transaction.
6. **They can become addictive.** Suvorov's principal-agent model: offering a reward signals the task is undesirable; once offered, the principal "is doomed to give it again," and the reward soon feels like the status quo, forcing escalation. Neuroscience (Knutson, fMRI): anticipating monetary reward sends dopamine to the nucleus accumbens — the same basic physiological process as cocaine, nicotine, or amphetamine addiction — and that activation predicts risky choices and risk-seeking mistakes (why casinos surround guests with reward cues).
7. **They can foster short-term thinking.** Rewards reduce the depth as well as the breadth of thinking. People work only to the point that triggers the reward: students paid to read three books rarely pick up a fourth. Companies that spend the most time offering quarterly earnings guidance deliver significantly lower long-term growth — partly because they invest less in R&D. Paying people to exercise, quit smoking, or take medicine works until the payment stops. Pink reads the 2008-09 financial crisis as extrinsically motivated myopia at every layer of the system.

## 5. When Carrots and Sticks DO Work (and How to Use Rewards Safely)

Rewards aren't inherently evil. The decision logic (the book renders it as a flowchart):

- **First, always:** ensure baseline compensation is adequate and fair. Without that, nothing works.
- **Ask: is the task routine?** Glucksberg's second candle experiment proves the pivot: when he emptied the tacks out of the box (revealing the solution and making the task algorithmic), the incentivized group solved it *faster*. Rewards help people race down an obvious path; they hurt when the path must be invented.
- **For routine/algorithmic tasks:** if-then rewards can give a small motivational boost with few side effects — "Rewards do not undermine people's intrinsic motivation for dull tasks because there is little or no intrinsic motivation to be undermined" (Deci/Ryan/Koestner). Ariely's MIT replication: for purely mechanical tasks, higher pay = better performance; once the task required "even rudimentary cognitive skill," larger rewards led to poorer performance. First preference is still the positive Sawyer Effect (add variety, make it game-like); when that's not possible, use if-then rewards plus three practices: (1) **offer a rationale** for why the task is necessary (ties it to a larger purpose), (2) **acknowledge the task is boring** (empathy, and explains why this is a rare exception), (3) **let people complete it their own way** (state the outcome, not the method).
- **For creative/heuristic tasks:** never dangle an if-then reward — best approach is baseline fairness plus an autonomy/mastery/purpose environment, then "provide a sense of urgency and significance — and then get out of the talent's way." If rewarding at all:
  - The essential requirement: **any extrinsic reward should be unexpected and offered only after the task is complete** — shift from "if-then" to **"now that" rewards**. Amabile found the highest creativity levels among subjects who received a reward as a bonus. Ginormous caveat: repeated "now that" bonuses quickly become expected if-then entitlements.
  - **Prefer nontangible rewards:** praise and positive feedback are much less corrosive than cash and trophies; Deci found positive feedback can *enhance* intrinsic motivation.
  - **Provide useful information:** controlling motivators clobber creativity, but "informational or enabling" ones can feed it (Amabile). Make feedback specific ("great use of color") and about effort and strategy, not about hitting an outcome. Same finding from the artist commissions: commissions experienced as *enabling* (interesting work, useful feedback on ability) scored as creative as free work; commissions experienced as *constraining* did not.

## 6. Type I vs Type X Behavior

Pink's alphabet (explicit homage to Friedman/Rosenman's Type A/B and McGregor's Theory X/Y):

- **Type X behavior:** fueled more by extrinsic desires than intrinsic ones; concerns itself less with the inherent satisfaction of an activity, more with the external rewards it leads to. Runs on the second drive; the behavior Motivation 2.0 depends on and fosters.
- **Type I behavior:** fueled more by intrinsic desires; the main motivator is the freedom, challenge, and purpose of the undertaking itself — other gains welcome, but as a bonus. Runs on the third drive; what Motivation 3.0 depends on.

Key properties of Type I:

- **Made, not born.** Proclivities from circumstance, experience, and context — not fixed traits, not dependent on age, gender, or nationality. Any Type X can become a Type I in supportive settings.
- **Type I's almost always outperform Type X's in the long run.** Extrinsic focus can deliver fast short-term results but is hard to sustain and doesn't build mastery — the source of long-haul achievement. The most successful people often aren't directly pursuing conventional success.
- **Type I does not disdain money or recognition.** Below the fair baseline, everyone's motivation craters, X or I. Above it, money plays different roles: for Type I's, fair and adequate pay takes money off the table so they can focus on the work; for many Type X's, money *is* the table. Type I's value recognition as feedback, not as a goal in itself.
- **Renewable resource.** Type X is coal (works, but polluting, and if-then motivators always grow more expensive); Type I is solar: inexpensive, safe, endlessly renewable.
- **Better well-being.** SDT research: people oriented toward autonomy and intrinsic motivation have higher self-esteem, better relationships, greater general well-being. People whose core aspirations are money, fame, or beauty have poorer psychological health — and Deci found the control/extrinsic orientation correlates with the cardiac-risk Type A pattern.

Type I behavior depends on three nutrients: **autonomy, mastery, and purpose**.

## 7. Autonomy — The Four T's

Autonomy is the desire to direct our own lives — the most important of SDT's three needs, "the sun around which SDT's planets orbit." It is *not* independence or go-it-alone individualism: it means acting with volition and choice, so you can be autonomous and happily interdependent. In the 1980s Deci and Ryan shifted the key axis from intrinsic-vs-extrinsic to **autonomous vs. controlled** motivation. Control leads to compliance; autonomy leads to engagement. Our default setting is curious and self-directed (watch any one-year-old); passivity at 14 or 43 means something flipped the setting — and "management" itself is one of the forces doing the flipping. Empowerment and flex time are "control in sheep's clothing." Autonomy is a human concept, not a Western one — the autonomy/well-being link holds in Russia, Turkey, South Korea, Bangladesh.

Evidence: autonomy support from bosses (seeing the employee's viewpoint, meaningful feedback, real choice over what and how, encouragement to take on new projects) raised job satisfaction and performance at an investment bank (Deci/Ryan/Baard, 2004). Cornell study of 320 small businesses: the half granting autonomy grew at 4x the rate of the control-oriented half, with one-third the turnover.

Type I behavior emerges when people have autonomy over the **four T's**:

- **Task** — what they do. 3M's 15% "bootlegging" time under William McKnight ("Hire good people, and leave them alone") birthed Post-it notes; most of 3M's key inventions came from it. Google's 20% time produced Gmail, Google News, Google Talk, Google Translate — in a typical year over half of Google's new offerings. Atlassian's FedEx Days (24-hour quarterly ship-something sprints, results demoed to the whole company) plus permanent 20% time (48 new projects in the first year; zero engineering turnover as the CFO-facing justification).
- **Time** — when they do it. The billable hour is the anti-model for nonroutine work: it shifts focus from output (solving the problem) to input (piling up hours) and invites gaming (Rehnquist: 2,000-hour targets create "temptations to exaggerate"). ROWE (results-only work environment, Ressler and Thompson, first at Best Buy corporate): no schedules, no mandatory presence, every meeting optional, just get the work done. Best Buy results: productivity +35%, voluntary turnover 320 basis points lower than non-ROWE teams, better focus and family relationships. Gunther's Meddius ROWE: productivity rose, stress fell, and money became "a threshold motivator."
- **Technique** — how they do it. Zappos: no scripts, no call-time monitoring for service reps; reps serve the customer however they choose; minimal turnover and top-tier US customer-service rankings. (Hsieh's $2,000 post-training quit-offer is an if-then reward used to *filter out* poor fits, not to motivate.) Homeshoring (JetBlue and others): home-based reps, less monitoring, higher satisfaction and productivity, deeper talent pool, recruiting costs near zero.
- **Team** — whom they do it with. Whole Foods departments vote on hires after a 30-day trial; W. L. Gore leaders must assemble willing followers; Google "grouplets" self-organize to change the company. Research: people in self-organized teams are more satisfied than those in inherited teams, and intrinsically motivated people are better coworkers — autonomy is contagious. This is the least developed of the four T's (Atlassian's staff actually under-used 20% time to avoid letting teammates down).

Caveats: autonomy doesn't mean no accountability — Motivation 3.0 presumes people *want* to be accountable, and control over the four T's is the path there. People coming out of controlling environments need "scaffolding" (Ryan) to transition. And different individuals prize different T's — Hsieh: there's no universally most important aspect of autonomy; figure out what matters to each person.

## 8. Mastery — Flow, Goldilocks Tasks, and the Three Laws

Mastery is the desire to get better and better at something that matters. Control produces compliance; autonomy produces engagement; **only engagement produces mastery**. Stakes: Gallup — over 50% of US employees not engaged, nearly 20% actively disengaged, ~$300B/year in lost productivity; McKinsey — in some countries as little as 2-3% of the workforce is highly engaged.

**Flow (Csikszentmihalyi):** the optimal ("autotelic") experience where the activity is its own reward. Conditions: clear goals, immediate feedback, and challenge exquisitely matched to ability — a notch or two beyond current capability. In flow, time, place, and self-consciousness dissolve. People report flow *more often at work than in leisure*. Depriving people of flow (his experiment banning all "noninstrumental" enjoyable activity) produced GAD-like symptoms so fast that after two days prolonging the experiment "would have been unadvisable" — flow is the oxygen of the soul.

Organizational applications:

- **Goldilocks tasks:** neither too hard (anxiety) nor too easy (boredom); the must-do/can-do match is a core design variable. Stefan Falk at Ericsson replaced annual reviews with six one-on-ones a year (up to 90 minutes) on engagement and path to mastery; at Green Cargo he trained managers in flow and required monthly check-ins for overwhelm/underwhelm and assignment adjustment — the state-owned firm became profitable for the first time in 125 years. Supporting data: among 11,000 industrial scientists and engineers, desire for intellectual challenge was the best predictor of productivity — intrinsically motivated scientists filed significantly more patents at equal effort.
- **Positive Sawyer Effect / job crafting:** let people sculpt mundane jobs to add absorbing challenges (Wrzesniewski and Dutton's hospital cleaners who took on extra patient-facing tasks). "Even in low-autonomy jobs, employees can create new domains for mastery."

**The three laws of mastery:**

1. **Mastery is a mindset** (Dweck). Entity theory: intelligence is a fixed quantity, every encounter measures how much you have, effort signals inadequacy ("a diet of easy successes"), adversity produces the helpless response. Incremental theory: ability grows with effort, encounters are opportunities, setbacks are guideposts. **Learning goals** (be able to speak French) beat **performance goals** (get an A in French): in Dweck's studies, learning-goal students scored significantly higher on novel transfer problems, worked longer, and tried more solutions. Type X maps to entity theory + performance goals; Type I to incremental theory + learning goals.
2. **Mastery is a pain.** West Point's Beast Barracks: the best predictor of who survives isn't strength, intellect, or leadership — it's **grit**, "perseverance and passion for long-term goals," which also predicts college grades better than IQ or standardized test scores. Ericsson: traits believed innate are "actually the results of intense practice for a minimum of 10 years." Chambliss's Olympic swimmer study: "the mundanity of excellence." Flow helps twice — it identifies what's worth mastering, and it pulls you through the plateaus — but the road is mostly grind. Julius Erving: being a professional is doing the things you love on the days you don't feel like doing them.
3. **Mastery is an asymptote.** You can approach it forever, never touch it (Cézanne's endless stalking of the synthesis; Tiger Woods insisting he must get better while knowing he never fully will). Both the frustration and the allure: the joy is in the pursuit.

## 9. Purpose — The Third Leg

Autonomy and mastery are essential but need context. Purpose — working in service of something larger than yourself — provides it. Autonomous people working toward mastery perform at very high levels; those doing it for a larger cause achieve even more. Motivation 2.0 treats purpose as ornament; Motivation 3.0 places **purpose maximization** alongside profit maximization — profit becomes the catalyst or by-product, not the objective. (Demographic tailwind: in the US alone, one hundred boomers turn sixty every thirteen minutes until 2024, each reckoning with mortality and meaning; volunteerism rises while paid-work engagement falls.)

The purpose motive shows up in three organizational realms:

- **Goals:** purpose-built structures — TOMS ("a for-profit company with giving at its core," one-for-one shoes), B Corps, L3Cs, for-benefits, co-ops (worldwide membership doubled in three decades to 800M; more Americans belong to a co-op than own stock). Hewlett's research: neither boomers nor Gen Y rates money the most important form of compensation — both choose nonmonetary factors ("a great team," "the ability to give back to society through work").
- **Words:** language signals the operating system. Reich's **pronoun test**: do employees say "they" or "we" about their company? "We" companies win. Hamel: management-speak ("efficiency," "advantage," "value," "focus") lacks the power to rouse human hearts; leaders must infuse work with ideals like honor, truth, love, justice, beauty. The Harvard MBA Oath (2009, after a survey in which 56% of MBA students admitted cheating regularly): ~a quarter of the graduating class signed a purpose-maximizing pledge.
- **Policies:** ethics checklists backfire by converting intrinsic "do the right thing" into extrinsic box-ticking (Bazerman: the standard becomes checking boxes so the company doesn't get sued). Better: put autonomy in service of purpose. Two proven policies: give employees control over the company's charitable giving (Dunn/Norton/Aknin: **pro-social spending** — money spent on others or causes — raises subjective well-being more than spending on yourself, so it beats one more if-then incentive); and Mayo Clinic's trial letting physicians spend one day a week on the most meaningful part of their work, which cut burnout to *half* the rate of non-participants ("20 percent time with a purpose").

The goal-content research (Deci, Ryan, Niemiec — University of Rochester graduates tracked 1-2 years out): those with **purpose goals** (help others, learn, grow) who attained them got happier and showed low anxiety and depression. Those with **profit goals** (wealth, fame) who *attained* them were no more satisfied than in college and showed *increases* in anxiety and depression. Attaining the wrong goals contributes to ill-being — satisfaction depends on having the right goals, not just reaching goals. The failure mode: not feeling better after hitting profit goals, people scale up the same goals and drive further down the wrong road.

## 10. Type I Toolkit — Nine Strategies for Individuals

Part Three's first toolkit: awakening your own motivation.

1. **Give yourself a flow test.** Csikszentmihalyi's Experience Sampling Method, self-administered: set ~40 random alerts across a week; at each, record what you're doing, how you feel, whether you're in flow. Then mine the patterns — which activities, times of day, and people produce flow; how to restructure the day around them; and what the results say about your true source of intrinsic motivation if you're doubting your career.
2. **First, ask a big question:** "What's your sentence?" (Clare Boothe Luce to JFK: a great person is one sentence — Lincoln's was preserving the union and freeing the slaves.) Distill your purpose to a single sentence; a splintered agenda is a muddled paragraph.
3. **Then keep asking a small question:** each night, "Was I better today than yesterday?" Look for small daily improvement, not flawlessness — you don't need to be a master by day 3, and that mindset is how you become one by day 3,000.
4. **Take a Sagmeister.** Designer Stefan Sagmeister closes his studio every seven years for a full-year sabbatical — snipping years off the end-of-life retirement block and inserting them mid-career. The ideas generated in the year off often provide his income for the next seven years.
5. **Give yourself a performance review.** Annual reviews fail (feedback lands six months after the work). Instead: set mostly learning goals plus a few performance goals, both small and large; do a monthly self-appraisal — where am I falling short, what tools/information/support do I need; tie every part of the work to the larger purpose; be brutally honest (rationalizing failures wastes the exercise). Peer-based versions work if the peers will tell the truth and hold you accountable.
6. **Get unstuck by going oblique.** Eno and Schmidt's Oblique Strategies deck: random provocation cards (e.g., "What is the simplest solution?", "Repetition is a form of change") to break a mental rut under deadline pressure.
7. **Move five steps closer to mastery** — Ericsson's deliberate practice: (1) the sole objective is improving performance ("setting new goals and straining yourself to reach a bit higher each time" — weekly tennis played the same way for years improves nothing); (2) repeat, repeat, repeat (greats shoot five hundred free throws, not ten); (3) seek constant, critical feedback; (4) focus ruthlessly on weaknesses ("those who get better work on their weaknesses"); (5) expect it to be mentally and physically exhausting — that's why so few commit, and why it works.
8. **Webber's two-card exercise** (Alan Webber, *Rules of Thumb*): on one 3x5 card, answer "What gets you up in the morning?"; on the reverse, "What keeps you up at night?" One sentence each, rewritten until you can live with them. Both answers should give meaning and direction; if not, the real question is what you're going to do about it.
9. **Create your own motivational poster.** Motivation is deeply personal; make the words and images yourself rather than accepting canned workplace inspiration.

## 11. Type I Toolkit — Nine Ways for Organizations

Improving a company, office, or group:

1. **Try 20% time with training wheels.** Skittish organizations start with 10% (one afternoon per five-day week) for six months, then evaluate. An island of autonomy that converts downtime into productive time.
2. **Encourage peer-to-peer "now that" rewards.** Kimley-Horn model: any employee can award a $50 bonus to any colleague at any time, no management permission. Works because it's real-time, noncontingent, and peer-given — recognition arrives within minutes and carries deeper meaning than a boss's handout.
3. **Conduct an autonomy audit.** Anonymous survey, four questions rated 0-10: autonomy over (1) task, (2) time, (3) team, (4) technique. Tabulate the average on the 40-point scale, compare against leadership's perception, and break out per-T scores — a healthy overall average (say 27) can hide a weak spot (e.g., 8/8/8 but a 3 on time). Include a free-text field for employees' own autonomy ideas; leaders act on real data.
4. **Take three steps toward giving up control:** (1) involve people in goal-setting — engagement is far higher for self-authored goals, and people often aim higher than you would for them; (2) use noncontrolling language — "think about"/"consider" instead of "must"/"should"; (3) hold office hours — scheduled open time where anyone can bring you anything.
5. **Play "Whose purpose is it anyway?"** Everyone writes a one-sentence answer to "What is our organization's purpose?" on a card; read them aloud. Aligned answers, scattered answers, or blank stares tell you the true state of mission and culture. If people don't know why they're doing what they're doing, they can't be motivated to do it.
6. **Use Reich's pronoun test.** Listen for "we" vs "they" in hallways and at lunch, not just meetings. "They" signals disengagement or alienation; "we" signals belonging. Everybody wants autonomy, mastery, and purpose — "we" can get it, "they" can't.
7. **Design for intrinsic motivation** (Clay Shirky, from successful websites, applied equally to physical space): make participation feel good, give users autonomy, keep the system as open as possible. Ask how the built environment of the workplace promotes or inhibits autonomy, mastery, and purpose.
8. **Promote Goldilocks for groups:** begin with a diverse team (Amabile: heterogeneous backgrounds cross-fertilize ideas); make the group a no-competition zone (pitting coworkers against each other undermines intrinsic motivation — use "collaboration" and "cooperation" instead); task-shift (a bored member trains someone in their mastered skill, then takes on part of a more experienced member's work); animate with purpose, don't motivate with rewards (nothing bonds a team like a shared mission).
9. **Turn your next off-site into a FedEx Day.** Replace forced fun with a full day working on anything, however, with whomever — one rule: deliver something (idea, prototype, process improvement) the next day. Real challenges invigorate more than controlled leisure.

## 12. The Zen of Compensation — Paying People the Type I Way

Core principle: **in Motivation 3.0, the best use of money is to take the issue of money off the table.** The more prominent salary, perks, and benefits are in someone's work life, the more they can inhibit creativity and unravel performance (Deci: when organizations use rewards like money to motivate, "that's when they're most demotivating"). Get compensation right — then get it out of sight. Three techniques:

1. **Ensure internal and external fairness.** Internal: pay commensurate with colleagues of equivalent responsibility and experience. External: pay in line with similar work at similar organizations (discovering peers at comparable firms earn double produces a largely irreversible motivation dip). Fair does not mean equal — people accept a colleague earning more for a harder job or bigger contribution, because that's fair too. Equity isn't itself a motivator; it prevents money from becoming a *de*-motivator.
2. **Pay more than average.** Akerlof and Yellen's efficiency-wage finding: paying great people a little above the market attracts better talent, reduces turnover, and boosts productivity and morale — higher wages can actually reduce total costs. It elegantly bypasses if-then rewards and fairness worries. Related: a high level of base pay does more for performance and organizational commitment than an attractive bonus structure. (By definition only about half of firms can be above average — move first.)
3. **If you use performance metrics, make them wide-ranging, relevant, and hard to game.** A single quarterly sales number invites tunnel vision and corner-cutting. A basket — quarterly sales, annual sales, company revenue/profit over two years, customer satisfaction, new-product ideas, peer evaluations — points toward simply doing good work, because varied metrics are hard to finagle. Keep the payoff for hitting metrics modest, not massive: modest gains don't narrow focus or invite the low road. Done well, this converts combustible if-then rewards into something closer to "now that" recognition.

## 13. Type I Beyond the Workplace — Kids, Fitness, and the Guru Shortlist

**Parents and educators (nine ideas, selected).** All kids start as curious, self-directed Type I's; schools bribing with pizza, iPods, and cash for scores convert them to compliant Type X's. Countermeasures:

- **Three-part Type I homework test:** does the assignment offer autonomy over how/when, promote mastery via a novel engaging task (not rote reformulation), and have a purpose students understand? If any answer is no, refashion it.
- **FedEx Days for kids:** a self-chosen project day; the deliverable is reporting back what they built and learned.
- **DIY report cards:** students list their learning goals at term start, self-grade against them at term end, then compare with the teacher's card as the start of a mastery conversation.
- **Allowance + chores, never combined.** Allowance teaches money autonomy; chores teach family obligation. Linking them turns a moral/familial obligation into a commercial transaction and teaches that undesirable tasks are done only for payment.
- **Praise the right way (Dweck):** praise effort and strategy, not intelligence (kids praised for being smart avoid challenges to protect the label); make it specific; deliver it in private (praise is feedback, not an award ceremony); only when deserved (overpraise reads as dishonest and becomes another if-then reward).
- **Help kids see the big picture:** every subject should answer "Why am I learning this? How is it relevant to the world I live in now?" — then apply it outside the classroom (the fourth R: relevance).
- **Model schools:** Big Picture Learning (real work in the community, assessed like adults on performance and attitude; 95%+ of its mostly at-risk students graduate and go to college), Sudbury Valley (total student control of task/time/technique), the Tinkering School, Puget Sound Community School, and Montessori (plus Reggio Emilia and Waldorf). Also the unschooling movement, and turning students into teachers — teaching a topic is one of the best tests of mastery.

**Type I fitness plan (four tips):** set your own goals — intrinsic ones (feel good, stay healthy for family) beat extrinsic ones (slim down for an event), which produce rebound; ditch the treadmill for activities that produce flow (use the Sawyer Effect to turn workout into play); keep mastery in mind — Goldilocks-scale the difficulty upward over time for renewable energy; reward yourself the right way — no if-then self-bribes ("if I exercise four times, then a new shirt"), occasional "now that" treats are fine, and a commitment device (Stickk: money forfeited to a friend, charity, or anti-charity on failure) can help when truly stuck.

**Six business thinkers who get it (the guru shortlist):**

- **McGregor** — Theory X (people avoid effort, work for money/security, need control) vs Theory Y (work is as natural as play; initiative and creativity are widespread; committed people seek responsibility). Belief in the "mediocrity of the masses" makes mediocrity the ceiling.
- **Drucker** — self-management as the next frontier: knowledge workers must be autonomous, defining their own tasks, expected results, and deadlines.
- **Collins** — "Expending energy trying to motivate people is largely a waste of time... the real question becomes: How do you manage in such a way as not to de-motivate people?" Four practices: lead with questions, not answers; engage in dialogue and debate, not coercion; conduct autopsies without blame; build red-flag mechanisms so employees and customers can flag problems easily.
- **Ressler and Thompson** — ROWE tenets: stop all time-wasting activity, work any way you want, every meeting is optional, no work schedules. Only results matter.
- **Hamel** — management is an outdated technology, its essential tools invented by people born in the 19th century; ask "Have we committed ourselves to a purpose that is truly deserving of their initiative, imagination, and passion?"
- Plus the **fifteen-book reading list**, notably: Dweck's *Mindset* (fixed vs growth; listen for the fixed-mindset voice, reinterpret challenges as stretches, use growth language), Kohn's *Punished by Rewards* ("Do rewards motivate people? Absolutely. They motivate people to get rewards."), Carse's *Finite and Infinite Games* (finite players play within boundaries; infinite players play with boundaries), Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow*, Deci's *Why We Do What We Do* (motivation is something people do, not something done to them), Colvin's *Talent Is Overrated*, Semler's *Maverick* (radical autonomy at Semco: self-set hours, votes on big decisions, some self-set salaries — 20% annual growth for two decades), and Senge's *The Fifth Discipline* (personal mastery as lifelong learning).

**Quick recap (Pink's own one-liner):** carrots and sticks are last century; 21st-century work needs an upgrade to autonomy (the desire to direct our own lives), mastery (the urge to get better at something that matters), and purpose (the yearning to act in service of something larger than ourselves).
