# Drive — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing incentive/motivation systems, or advising on employee engagement.*
*Book: "Drive: The Surprising Truth About What Motivates Us" — Daniel H. Pink*

---

## 1. The Three Drives and the Motivation Operating Systems (1.0, 2.0, 3.0)

Pink frames motivation as a societal "operating system" built on assumptions about human behavior. Three drives power behavior:

- **Drive 1 — Biological:** hunger, thirst, sex. Survival. The basis of **Motivation 1.0** (worked for early humans, inadequate once societies formed).
- **Drive 2 — Reward and punishment:** seek reward, avoid punishment. The basis of **Motivation 2.0**: reward the behavior you want, punish what you don't. Codified by Frederick Taylor's "scientific management" (workers as machine parts). It powered the industrial economy and still dominates how organizations run.
- **Drive 3 — Intrinsic motivation:** the inherent satisfaction of the activity itself. Discovered by Harry Harlow (1949, rhesus monkeys solved puzzles for no reward, and adding raisins made performance worse) and confirmed by Edward Deci (1969 Soma cube experiments: paying people to do an interesting task killed their voluntary interest in it once payment stopped). Deci's finding: "When money is used as an external reward for some activity, the subjects lose intrinsic interest for the activity." The basis of **Motivation 3.0**.

Motivation 2.0 got a minor patch (call it 2.1) via Maslow's humanistic psychology and McGregor's Theory Y (flex time, "empowerment"), but Pink argues those are control in sheep's clothing, not an upgrade. Motivation 3.0 is the full upgrade: built on autonomy, mastery, and purpose.

Underlying theory: Deci and Ryan's **self-determination theory (SDT)**. Humans have three innate psychological needs: competence, autonomy, and relatedness. When satisfied, people are motivated, productive, and happy. When thwarted, all three collapse. The third drive is universal but fragile: it needs the right environment to survive.

## 2. Why Motivation 2.0 Is Breaking: Three Incompatibility Problems

Carrot-and-stick logic is increasingly incompatible with modern work in three ways:

- **How we organize what we do.** Open source (Wikipedia beat Microsoft's paid Encarta; Linux, Apache, Firefox) runs on intrinsic motivation: surveys of open-source developers show "enjoyment-based intrinsic motivation" and flow are the strongest drivers. New legal forms (L3Cs, B Corporations, social businesses, for-benefit organizations) are **purpose maximizers**, not profit maximizers, and don't fit the 2.0 model.
- **How we think about what we do.** Behavioral economics (Kahneman, Ariely, ultimatum game) shows people are not rational wealth-maximizing calculators. Extrinsic incentives assume rational response; humans routinely don't deliver it. If we're predictably irrational, we can also be predictably purpose-seeking (Bruno Frey: "It is inconceivable that people are motivated solely or even mainly by external incentives").
- **How we do what we do.** Work splits into **algorithmic** tasks (follow a set of instructions down one path to one conclusion) and **heuristic** tasks (no formula exists; you must experiment and invent). Routine algorithmic work is being offshored and automated. McKinsey estimate: 70% of US job growth comes from heuristic work. Amabile's research: rewards work fine for algorithmic tasks and are devastating for heuristic ones ("intrinsic motivation is conducive to creativity; controlling extrinsic motivation is detrimental to creativity"). Flatter organizations and self-employment also mean fewer managers to supervise anyone; work now depends on self-direction.

## 3. The Sawyer Effect and Baseline Rewards

Two foundational concepts that run through everything else:

- **Baseline rewards first.** Salary, benefits, and basic conditions must be adequate and *equitable* before any motivation science applies. If baseline rewards are inadequate or unfair, the person fixates on the unfairness and you get almost no motivation of any kind, intrinsic or extrinsic. Pay well, pay fairly, then take money off the table so people can focus on the work.
- **The Sawyer Effect** (from Tom Sawyer's fence): rewards can perform behavioral alchemy in both directions. Negative side: external rewards turn play into work (paying people for something they enjoy converts it into a chore). Positive side: the current can be run in reverse, turning work into play (adding variety, game elements, purpose, or self-chosen challenges to mundane tasks).

Key mechanism: only **contingent, expected, "if-then" rewards** ("if you do this, then you get that") do the damage. They require people to give up autonomy, and that drains the activity of enjoyment. Classic evidence: the Lepper/Greene/Nisbett preschool drawing study, where children promised a certificate for drawing lost interest in drawing two weeks later, while unexpected-reward and no-reward children didn't. Deci's 1999 meta-analysis of 128 experiments confirmed: tangible rewards have a substantially negative effect on intrinsic motivation.

## 4. The Seven Deadly Flaws of Carrots and Sticks

If-then rewards, applied to interesting or creative work, reliably backfire. The seven flaws, each backed by research:

1. **They can extinguish intrinsic motivation** (Deci's Soma experiments; the preschool drawing study).
2. **They can diminish performance.** Ariely's Madurai, India study: the group offered the largest bonus (five months' pay) performed worst; "in eight of the nine tasks, higher incentives led to worse performance." LSE review of 51 pay-for-performance plans: financial incentives "can result in a negative impact on overall performance."
3. **They can crush creativity.** Glucksberg's candle problem: incentivized participants took ~3.5 minutes *longer*, because rewards narrow focus and block the peripheral thinking creative problems need. Amabile: commissioned artwork rated significantly less creative than noncommissioned by blind expert panels; the artists least motivated by external rewards had the most successful long-term careers.
4. **They can crowd out good behavior.** Swedish blood-donation study: offering payment cut donation from 52% to 30% (paying tainted an altruistic act); letting donors give the fee to charity restored it to 53%.
5. **They can encourage cheating, shortcuts, and unethical behavior.** Imposed goals narrow focus so much that some people take the low road to the reward (Sears repair quotas, Enron revenue targets, Ford Pinto). When the reward is the activity itself, there are no shortcuts.
6. **They can become addictive.** Suvorov's principal-agent model: offering a reward signals the task is undesirable, and once offered, it must be offered again, and usually escalated. Neuroscience (Knutson): anticipated monetary reward triggers the same dopamine surge in the nucleus accumbens as addictive drugs, and pushes people from risk-averse toward risk-seeking choices.
7. **They can foster short-term thinking.** People work only to the point that triggers the reward and no further. Companies obsessed with quarterly earnings guidance invest less in R&D and post lower long-term growth. Paying people to exercise or quit smoking works until the payment stops.

Also on goals: goals people set themselves, aimed at mastery, are healthy. Goals imposed by others (sales targets, quarterly numbers, test scores) carry the side effects above. And sticks fail symmetrically: the Israeli day-care fine study showed that fining late parents *doubled* lateness, because the fine converted a moral obligation into a purchasable transaction, and the effect persisted even after the fine was removed.

## 5. When Carrots and Sticks DO Work (and How to Use Rewards Safely)

Rewards aren't inherently evil. The decision logic:

- **First, always:** ensure baseline compensation is adequate and fair. Without that, nothing works.
- **For routine/algorithmic tasks** (rule-based, uninteresting, little creativity required): if-then rewards can give a small motivational boost with few side effects, because "rewards do not undermine people's intrinsic motivation for dull tasks because there is little or no intrinsic motivation to be undermined" (Deci/Ryan/Koestner). Ariely confirmed: for purely mechanical tasks, higher pay = better performance; the moment "even rudimentary cognitive skill" was needed, larger rewards hurt.
- When using rewards for routine tasks, add three practices: (1) offer a **rationale** for why the task is necessary (ties it to purpose), (2) **acknowledge the task is boring** (empathy, and explains why this is an exception), (3) allow people to do it **their own way** (autonomy over technique; specify the outcome, not the method).
- **For creative/heuristic tasks:** never use if-then rewards. Instead:
  - Use **"now that" rewards**: unexpected, given only after the work is complete ("now that you've done such great work, let's celebrate"). Not experienced as the reason for doing the task, so far less damaging. Caution: repeated "now that" bonuses become expected if-then entitlements.
  - Prefer **nontangible rewards**: praise and positive feedback actually *enhance* intrinsic motivation, unlike cash and trophies.
  - Provide **useful information**: specific, informational feedback about the work ("great use of color"), focused on effort and strategy rather than outcomes. Amabile: controlling motivators clobber creativity, but "informational or enabling" ones can feed it. Similarly, artists' commissions rated creative again when the artist experienced the commission as *enabling* (interesting work, useful feedback) rather than *constraining*.

## 6. Type I vs Type X Behavior

Pink's alphabet (homage to McGregor's Theory X/Y and Friedman's Type A/B):

- **Type X behavior:** fueled more by extrinsic desires than intrinsic ones; concerned with the external rewards an activity leads to. Money and recognition *are* the point. Runs on the second drive; the behavior Motivation 2.0 depends on and fosters.
- **Type I behavior:** fueled more by intrinsic desires; concerned with the inherent satisfaction of the activity. Runs on the third drive; what Motivation 3.0 depends on.

Key properties of Type I:

- **Made, not born.** These are proclivities from circumstance and context, not fixed traits. Any Type X can become a Type I given supportive settings.
- **Type I's almost always outperform Type X's in the long run.** Extrinsic focus can deliver fast short-term results but can't be sustained and doesn't build mastery. The most successful people usually aren't chasing conventional success directly.
- **Type I does not disdain money or recognition.** Below the fair baseline, everyone's motivation craters. Above it, money matters differently: for Type I's, fair pay takes money off the table; for Type X's, money is the table. Type I's value recognition as feedback, not as a goal.
- **Renewable resource.** Type X motivation is like coal (works, but polluting and ever more expensive: if-then rewards must keep escalating). Type I is like solar: cheap, safe, endlessly renewable.
- **Better well-being.** SDT research: intrinsically oriented people have higher self-esteem, better relationships, better psychological health. People whose core aspirations are money, fame, or beauty score worse, and extrinsic orientation correlates with Type A cardiac-risk behavior.

Type I behavior depends on three nutrients: **autonomy, mastery, and purpose**.

## 7. Autonomy — The Four T's

Autonomy is the desire to direct our own lives, the most important of SDT's three needs. It is *not* independence or go-it-alone individualism; it means acting with volition and choice (you can be autonomous and happily interdependent). The real axis is **autonomous vs. controlled** motivation, not just intrinsic vs. extrinsic. Control produces compliance; autonomy produces engagement. Default human setting is curious and self-directed (watch any one-year-old); passivity is a flipped setting, often flipped by management itself.

Evidence: autonomy support from bosses (seeing the employee's viewpoint, meaningful feedback, choice over what and how, encouragement) raises satisfaction and performance; a Cornell study of 320 small businesses found autonomy-granting firms grew at 4x the rate of control-oriented firms with one-third the turnover.

Type I behavior emerges when people have autonomy over the **four T's**:

- **Task** — what they do. 3M's 15% time (birthed Post-its), Google's 20% time (Gmail, Google News), Atlassian's FedEx Days (ship something in 24 hours) and 20% time.
- **Time** — when they do it. The billable hour is the anti-model for nonroutine work (input focus destroys output focus and invites gaming). ROWE (results-only work environment, pioneered at Best Buy): no schedules, no mandatory hours, just get the work done; produced +35% productivity and much lower turnover.
- **Technique** — how they do it. Zappos: no scripts, no call-time monitoring for service reps, and top-tier customer service ratings. Homeshoring similarly. Hsieh's $2,000 quit-offer after training is an if-then reward used to *filter out* poor fits, not to motivate.
- **Team** — whom they do it with. Whole Foods teams vote on hires after trial periods; W. L. Gore leaders must recruit willing followers; Google "grouplets" self-assemble. People in self-organized teams are more satisfied; intrinsically motivated people make better coworkers.

Caveats: autonomy doesn't mean no accountability; Motivation 3.0 presumes people *want* to be accountable and that control over the four T's is the path there. Transitioning from controlling environments needs "scaffolding," and different people prize different T's, so ask each person which matters most.

## 8. Mastery — Flow, Goldilocks Tasks, and the Three Laws

Mastery is the desire to get better and better at something that matters. Only engagement (not compliance) produces it.

**Flow (Csikszentmihalyi):** the optimal experience where challenge is perfectly matched to ability. Conditions: clear goals, immediate feedback, challenge a notch or two beyond current ability. In flow, time and self-consciousness dissolve. People report flow *more often at work than in leisure*. Depriving people of flow for even 48 hours produces symptoms resembling generalized anxiety disorder; flow is "the oxygen of the soul."

Organizational applications:

- **Goldilocks tasks:** assignments neither too hard (anxiety) nor too easy (boredom). The must-do/can-do match is a core design variable; smart managers meet one-on-one frequently to check for overwhelm/underwhelm and adjust (Ericsson's flow-based management at Ericsson and Green Cargo).
- **Positive Sawyer Effect / job crafting:** let people sculpt mundane jobs to add absorbing challenges (Wrzesniewski and Dutton's hospital cleaners). "Even in low-autonomy jobs, employees can create new domains for mastery."

**The three laws of mastery:**

1. **Mastery is a mindset** (Dweck). Entity theory: intelligence is fixed, encounters measure how much you have, effort signals weakness, adversity produces helplessness. Incremental theory: ability grows with effort, encounters are opportunities, effort is the path, setbacks are guideposts. **Learning goals** (be able to speak French) beat **performance goals** (get an A in French) for transfer to novel problems and persistence. Type X maps to entity theory + performance goals; Type I to incremental theory + learning goals.
2. **Mastery is a pain.** Grit ("perseverance and passion for long-term goals") predicts success at West Point and college grades better than IQ or test scores. Ericsson's deliberate practice: ~10 years of difficult, focused effort; "the mundanity of excellence." Flow helps you identify what to master and pulls you through plateaus, but the road is mostly grind.
3. **Mastery is an asymptote.** You can approach it forever but never fully reach it (Cézanne). This is both the frustration and the allure: the joy is in the pursuit.

## 9. Purpose — The Third Leg

Autonomy and mastery are essential but need context. Purpose: the yearning to work in service of something larger than yourself. Autonomous people working toward mastery perform at very high levels; those doing it for a larger cause achieve even more. Motivation 2.0 treats purpose as ornament; Motivation 3.0 treats **purpose maximization** as a peer of profit maximization (profit becomes the catalyst or by-product, not the objective).

Purpose shows up in three organizational realms:

- **Goals:** purpose-driven structures (TOMS one-for-one, B Corps, co-ops, for-benefits). Boomers and Gen Y both rank nonmonetary factors above money in what they want from work.
- **Words:** language signals the operating system. Reich's **pronoun test**: do employees say "they" or "we" about their company? "We" companies win. Hamel: management-speak ("efficiency," "advantage") lacks the power to rouse human hearts; leaders should invoke ideals.
- **Policies:** ethics checklists backfire by converting intrinsic "do the right thing" into extrinsic box-ticking (Bazerman). Better: put autonomy in service of purpose. Examples: giving employees control over the company's charitable giving (pro-social spending raises well-being more than another bonus); Mayo Clinic letting physicians spend one day a week on the most meaningful part of their work halved burnout.

The goal-content research (Deci, Ryan, Niemiec): graduates with **purpose goals** (help others, learn, grow) who attained them got happier and less anxious. Graduates with **profit goals** (wealth, fame) who *attained* them got no happier and showed *increased* anxiety and depression. Attaining the wrong goals contributes to ill-being; satisfaction depends on having the right goals, not just reaching goals.

## 10. Practical Techniques (Type I Toolkit, Selected)

Actionable exercises from Part Three for individuals and organizations:

- **Flow test:** self-page at ~40 random moments over a week; record what you're doing and whether you're in flow. Find the patterns, restructure the day around flow-friendly times and activities.
- **The big question:** "What's your sentence?" (Clare Boothe Luce to JFK: a great person is one sentence). Distill your purpose to a single sentence.
- **The small question:** each night ask, "Was I better today than yesterday?" Small daily improvement, not perfection, is what compounds into mastery.
- **Self performance reviews:** monthly, against mostly learning goals plus a few performance goals; brutally honest; tie every part of the work to the larger purpose. Peer-based versions work too. (Annual reviews fail because feedback arrives months late.)
- **Deliberate practice, five steps:** (1) the sole objective is improving performance, not repetition of what you can do; (2) repeat, repeat, repeat; (3) seek constant critical feedback; (4) focus ruthlessly on weaknesses; (5) expect it to be exhausting, that's why it works.
- **Take a Sagmeister:** pull years out of retirement and insert them as working-life sabbaticals; the exploration funds the next cycle of work.
- **Oblique Strategies (Eno/Schmidt):** random provocation cards to break creative ruts.
- **20% time with training wheels:** organizations skittish about full 20% time can start with 10% (one afternoon a week) of self-directed project time.
- **Flowchart for rewards** (recap of Section 5): Is the task routine? If yes, if-then rewards are safe with rationale + acknowledgment + autonomy over method. If no, keep baseline pay fair, build an autonomy/mastery/purpose environment, and use only unexpected "now that" rewards, preferably praise and useful information.
