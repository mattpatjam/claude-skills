# Powerful — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing hiring processes, or advising on team-building.*
*Book: "Powerful: Building a Culture of Freedom and Responsibility" — Patty McCord*

---

## 1. The Core Premise: People Have Power, Don't Take It Away

The twentieth-century system for managing people (annual reviews, retention plans, bonus structures, engagement and empowerment programs, performance improvement plans) is not up to the pace of change in twenty-first-century business. Most of it is costly, time-consuming, and built on two false assumptions: that people must be incentivized to throw themselves into work, and that they need to be told what to do. The "best practices" built on those premises are, ironically, disincentivizing and disempowering.

- A company's job is not to empower people. People walk in the door with power; the job is to remind them of that and create the conditions for them to exercise it. Empowerment programs only exist because conventional management takes power away by overprocessing everything.
- A business leader's job, in full: create great teams that do amazing work on time. That's it. Not loyalty, retention, career progression, engagement, or happiness. Engagement is treated as the endgame when the endgame is serving customers and getting results.
- The culture was built by stripping away policies and procedures, not adding programs. Done experimentally, step by step, over years, the same way you innovate a product: remove something, watch results, reinstate only if it turns out it was needed.
- Freedom is not a free-for-all. The trade: remove policy and procedure, demand discipline about a core set of behaviors instead. Engineers hate senseless bureaucracy and stupid process; they don't mind discipline at all.
- Culture transformation is not professing values. It is identifying the behaviors you want to become consistent practices, then instilling the discipline of actually doing them, modeled first by the executive team and every manager. The Netflix Culture Deck (15M+ views) started as an internal cheat sheet: "what we expect of you and what you should expect of us."
- The core behavior set: (1) open, clear, constant communication about the work and challenges, for the team and the whole company; (2) radical honesty, truth told in a timely fashion, ideally face to face; (3) strong, fact-based opinions, debated avidly and tested rigorously; (4) actions based on what's best for the customer and company, not on proving yourself right; (5) hiring managers taking the lead on preparing their teams for the future, with a high performer in every position.
- Implementation rule: proceed incrementally. Pick the practice that best fits your group and business issue and start there; for leadership teams, start with one department. Which step you start with doesn't matter; starting does.

## 2. Treat People Like Adults: Challenge Is the Motivator

Great teams are not created with incentives, procedures, and perks. They are created by hiring talented adults who want nothing more than to tackle a challenge, then communicating clearly and continuously about what the challenge is.

- "If you do X, you'll be rewarded with Y" assumes a static system; no business today is static. The best reward is making a significant contribution to meeting a real challenge.
- Hire people who get excited about the problems you have to solve, who wake up thinking "God, this is hard. I want to do this!" Problem finders are cheap; you want problem solvers.
- The lesson of the 2001 layoff (a third of the company cut, then twice the work with two-thirds the people, and everyone happier): the remaining people were the highest performers, and the best thing you can do for employees is hire only high performers to work alongside them. It beats foosball, free sushi, signing bonuses, even stock options. The powerful combination: excellent colleagues, a clear purpose, well-understood deliverables.
- Small, unencumbered teams beat large ones. A small team of brilliant engineers outperforms a large team of hardworking ones, and that logic holds for marketing, finance, and HR too. Large teams get crippled because managing them is hard, so companies build infrastructure to make sure people do the right things, which slows everyone down.
- Manage people the way you manage product: A/B test, experiment, debate openly, and if something doesn't work, get rid of it.
- Proven policy removals: no vacation policy (take what's appropriate, discuss with your manager; people took the same time as before), no expense/travel policy ("use good judgment about how you spend the company's money"; not abused). Trusting people with their own time was one of the first steps in giving them back their power.
- Kill annual planning if your projections are always wrong in three to six months anyway. Move to quarterly planning and rolling three-quarter budgets, as far out as you can ostensibly predict.
- Keep management flat. After the layoff removed middle-management layers, everyone moved faster without the layers of opinions and approvals.
- Freedom pairs with accountability: give proven people the latitude to realize their vision (no micromanaging, no approval committees) and make clear they own the quality of the outcome.

## 3. Communicate Context Constantly: Every Employee Should Understand the Business

What replaces rules, processes, approvals, and permissions? Clear, continuous communication about the context of the work: "Here's exactly where we are, and here's what we're trying to accomplish." The more time managers spend being transparent about the job to be done, the challenges, and the competitive landscape, the less policies, approvals, and incentives matter.

- People need to see the view from the C-suite to connect to the problem solving needed at all levels (the six-year-old soccer players who finally understood what a pass was after seeing the World Cup from the blimp).
- Establish a strong "heartbeat of communication," a constant drumbeat rather than an annual event. Working formats: leaders walking every batch of new hires through the culture and expectations; a quarterly "new employee college" where every department head gives an hour-long presentation of their metrics, deliverables, and current issues, and takes questions.
- Communication must flow both ways. Give people at every level license to ask questions and critique decisions up to the CEO ("You will take out of this day what you put into it. If you don't ask questions, you won't get answers"). A culture of curiosity produces strategic insight: an engineer's naive "Why does windowing of content happen like that? It seems stupid" planted the seed for releasing all episodes of a series at once.
- If someone seems clueless, treat it as your failure to inform, not their stupidity. Rule for managers: explain it as though you're explaining to your mother. If they couldn't follow, you made it too complicated.
- Teach frontline people the P&L from day one. Customer service reps should know the cost of customer acquisition and that every interaction drives free word-of-mouth marketing in one direction or the other. (Reported context: 78% of consumers have abandoned a transaction over poor service; cost to US businesses estimated at $62B annually; bad experiences spread to twice as many people as good ones.)
- Don't reserve "MBA stuff" for the top. If people are doing stupid things, assume they're uninformed or misinformed, not stupid.
- The single most valuable course you could teach anyone is how the business works and how it serves customers, not conflict management or interpersonal communication. Same answer for millennials: they're just people early in their careers who want to learn; teach them to read a P&L, not to tap a beer keg.
- The best perks are opportunities to understand the business and customers better (industry events, expert talks, off-sites with serious agendas: data, tough questions, vigorous debate about the future).
- If you don't inform your people, they'll be misinformed by colleagues or the internet. Public companies tell investors more than most tell their own staff; consider the equivalent of an earnings call for employees, or have them listen to the real one.
- The test of a strong heartbeat: stop any employee at any level and ask for the five most important things the company is working on in the next six months. They should rattle them off, one through five, ideally in the same words you used, and if they're really good, in the same order. If not, the heartbeat isn't strong enough.
- The job is never done: every new batch of hires has different questions, and the business keeps changing. Monitor and update the message forever.

## 4. Radical Honesty: Tell the Truth, Face to Face, in a Timely Way

It is not cruel to tell people the truth respectfully and honestly; it's the only way to ensure they trust and understand you. Part of being an adult is being able to hear the truth, and you owe the adults you hire the truth. That is what they want most from you. The desire to make people feel good is often really a desire to make ourselves feel comfortable, and it leaves problems uncorrected.

- Core mandate: people talk openly about issues with one another, up, down, and across, ideally face to face. No criticizing behind backs. The standing question to any complainer: "Have you told her yet?" (followed by "But you said it to me, didn't you?").
- Politics and backstabbing are not just nasty, they're inefficient. Honesty flushes out the differences of opinion and alternative ideas people keep to themselves.
- Withholding tough feedback puts undue pressure on the boss (covering for shortfalls) and cheats the employee of the chance to improve. "I was too kind... you end up sugarcoating things, and that's doing them a disservice."
- Feedback delivery rules: it must be about behavior, not an essentializing characterization ("you're unfocused"); it must be actionable, with specific examples and a proposed solution; strip the emotion out; practice the delivery out loud (mirror, spouse, recording) and mind your body language.
- Model from the top: the executive team ran "Start, Stop, Continue" out loud in its own meetings (each person tells a colleague one thing to start, one to stop, one to continue) and reported doing so back to their teams. Not an HR mandate; modeling made it spread.
- Scale mechanism: an annual whole-company feedback day where everyone sends "Start, Stop, Continue" comments to anyone they have feedback for. Signed, not anonymous (engineers rebelled against anonymity and were right: signed feedback became more thoughtful and productive). Monitor participation so people don't send softballs to three friends when they work with fifty people. Roughly 90% of people came to appreciate it.
- Anonymous surveys send a mixed message: that it's best to be most honest when nobody knows who you are. Truthful people are truthful in everything; without attribution you can't put comments in context. For a 70-person company, skip the purchased survey and ask seven groups of ten face to face.
- Be radically honest about business problems too. Preparing people for changes builds trust even when the changes are unpopular; not knowing is more anxiety-provoking than knowing. Half-truths breed cynicism, and "cynicism is a cancer."
- Honesty must flow upward: employees must never withhold questions or information. The fireable offense: "I knew that was a problem but nobody asked me." (Deloitte study: 70% of employees admit to remaining silent about issues that might compromise performance.)
- Leaders must publicly admit when they're wrong ("You were right, I was wrong") and call out silent dissenters in meetings ("You've been telling me for four months you're against this and you haven't said a word"). People who speak up must visibly live to see another day.
- When decisions are debated transparently and everyone had the chance to object, the team owns the outcome together instead of finger-pointing after a failure (the Qwikster split-and-reversal aftermath: the whole VP team pulled together because all were fully aware of the strategy).
- Companies that actively fostered honest feedback and open communication produced a ten-year return 270% higher than those that didn't (Corporate Executive Board study).

## 5. Debate Vigorously: Strong Opinions, Argued Only on Facts

Teach everyone to ask "How do you know that's true?" or the softer variant, "Can you help me understand what leads you to believe that's true?" Asked genuinely, these questions build bridges of understanding across functions (sales asking engineering "help me understand why buffering takes so long" instead of venting) and prevent rumor and back-channeling.

- "Have an opinion; take a stand; be right most of the time." Strong opinions are required, but they must be fact-based and defended with a fact-based case. Beware the eloquent persuader who wins arguments on charisma and is almost always wrong.
- Fact-driven, not data-driven. Data is vital but is the basis of good questions, not the answer to them. Decision making should be "data informed rather than data driven" (Ted Sarandos): data gave a big assist on House of Cards, but the judgment call rested on the creator's talent; Orange Is the New Black was green-lit against the well-developed-script rule because of the creator's vision, not data.
- Data traps: data as an accountability shield ("well, it tested really well") deflects responsibility for judgment calls; people marshal biased data and privilege their own department's numbers; data can't tell you what customers would want if they could have it (global box office data understated demand for American shows because access was limited); data that looks impressive but drives no decision is worthless ("HR will finally have data!" is not a use case).
- Metrics must be fluid, continuously revisited and questioned, never fixed. Fixating on metrics that don't matter (like HR's retention obsession) drives bad behavior.
- Set the terms: debates are only for the sake of the business and the customer. When argument becomes argument for its own sake, interject "And how does this help the customer exactly?" or "What problem are we trying to solve here?" or "What leads you to believe that's true?"
- Earn a reputation for selflessness: if people believe you're fighting for your ego or your department, they'll route around you; if they believe you're fighting for the customer, they'll hear you. Be genuinely prepared to lose, admit it openly when you do, and avidly recognize others' contributions (Reed remembering and reviving Ciancutti's forgotten inventory idea).
- "Fact-based" does not equal "true." Even compelling fact-based conclusions can be wrong; revisit debated-to-death conclusions when warranted.
- Orchestrate debates deliberately:
  - Stage formal debates in front of a group; executives keeping disagreements private deprives everyone below of the most important context. Watching well-argued cases teaches what excellence and good judgment look like and reveals your most talented people.
  - Have opponents argue each other's side, onstage, facing each other. To prep, each must get into the other's skin.
  - Break into small groups of 3-4 to debate and present solutions: cuts groupthink, makes silence conspicuous so everyone speaks, mixes people across functions, and counteracts the danger of expertise (spread domain specialists across groups; fresh eyes find ways around constraints experts take for granted).
- Institutionalize the forum: a monthly meeting (the Consumer Science Meeting) where the people who ran last month's customer tests present results and the rationale for next month's tests, and executives intensively question them. Debates go to the test: A/B results settled the queue-removal fight (no material difference in retention or viewing) and the sign-up friction hypothesis (removing credit card entry was expected to boost subscriptions; signups plummeted by half).
- Payoffs: hones the Culture Deck's core hiring qualities of good judgment (good decisions in ambiguous conditions, digging into causes, strategic thinking articulated) and courage. Younger workers take to transparency and questioning "like moths to a flame"; nobody would choose a negotiation seminar over the right to ask a tough question of a senior leader with impunity.

## 6. Build the Team You Need for the Future, Not the One You Have

Inverse of Rumsfeld's "you go to war with the army you have": hire now the team you wish to have in the future. Leaders plan product and market futures rigorously but rarely plan the future team, and when they do they think in sheer numbers.

- Don't let hiring become a numbers game. To a CEO planning to double from 150 to 300 people: what's that number based on? Same work or new kinds of work? More experienced managers or flatter teams? And the most thought-provoking question: "Are you sure you don't want seventy-five people who you pay twice as much because they have twice as much experience and can be higher performers?"
- Rushed hiring fails (twenty hires in a quarter, five wrong for the job); so does being picky with no pipeline (postponed projects). Budget requests for headcount routinely run 10-15% above actual need; demand a business case. Building the muscle to hire great people is a huge competitive advantage.
- Don't assume current employees can grow into future responsibilities. The start-up version: the people and improvisational working styles that got you product-market fit are often not the people for problems of scale and complexity, which require experience. Every company faces this continuously. Ask: can they do the job at scale? Do you need them to do tomorrow the same job they do now? What's your plan for them?
- The standing question for leaders: "Are we limited by the team we have not being the team we should have?"
- **The Fast-Forward Six Months exercise** (six months because that's as far ahead as anyone can imagine now): (1) Imagine you have the most amazing team you've ever assembled. Write down what it's accomplishing six months from now that it isn't now, with figures. Make a movie of it in your head. (2) More important, picture how things are being done differently: more meetings or fewer? Big loud debates? Faster decisions, made by whom? Heads-down or whiteboards? More cross-functional? (3) Ask what people would need to know how to do for that to happen: hard skills, soft skills, management experience, "capacity builders" (people who know how to build great teams; they'll tell you what teams you need and build them). (4) Only then look at the team you have and gap-analyze. Starting with the team you have gets you more, but not necessarily amazing.
- **Team, not family.** Great sports teams constantly scout new players and cull the lineup; team leaders continually reconfigure talent based purely on the performance the team needs to produce. You don't owe people a job they're not prepared for, a role invented to reward service, or holding the company back to avoid change.
- Promote-from-within vs. hire-outside rule of thumb: does the job require expertise no one inside has (cloud operations: hire out), or is it an area where you're at the forefront of innovation with top talent inside (data algorithms: promote)? Watch for hidden talents people don't see in themselves (the designer who could fix dysfunctional teams; the analyst who refused a big role three times and then thrived), and be honest that stretch roles are a risk.
- Not a career-management company: tell people in interviews that their careers are theirs to manage, that there may be lots of opportunity but you won't be designing opportunities for them. Never give someone half a job because they can't do the whole job. Don't promote strong individual performers into management they're unsuited for.
- Encourage employees to interview elsewhere regularly. It keeps them calibrated on the market, and it tells you how sought-after they are and what you should be paying them.
- Nostalgia is an early warning sign. Fondness for the scrappy early days is natural, but nostalgia that fuels resistance to change breeds discontent. Some people are happiest at a certain company size and stage; it's fine for them to find that environment elsewhere.

## 7. Someone Really Smart in Every Job: Talent Density and Hiring

Three tenets of talent management: (1) responsibility for hiring great people, and for determining whether someone should move on, rests primarily with managers; (2) for every job, hire a person who's a great fit, not just adequate; (3) be willing to say goodbye even to very good people if their skills no longer match the work needed. Hiring well and letting go well are two sides of the same coin; you'll never be good at one without the other.

- Be a great company to be from (like early Apple or Microsoft). The best companies stay limber by proactively bringing in new talent; the best employees eventually seek new challenges, and often nothing will stop them. Retention is a bad metric. The right measures: how many great people with the skills and experience you need do you have, how many are you keeping, how many are you hiring, and how rigorously and quickly are you replacing where needed.
- Great work is not about perks. Happiness at work comes from being deeply engaged in solving a problem with talented people and knowing customers love the product, not from kegerators, hammocks, or gourmet salads. If someone leaves for better craft beer, wave goodbye.
- Money doesn't buy love: pay highly competitive salaries, but never discuss numbers with candidates until they want to accept. Early money talk signals the candidate is underpaid, worried you can't match, or chasing money over the work; weed out the last group ("go chase the money at a competitor"). No rigid salary bands or 6% merit-increase budgets, so no reason to negotiate early. No bonus system: if your employees are adults who put the company first, an annual bonus won't make them work harder or smarter. Equity done differently: employees choose what portion of compensation to take as stock options in lieu of salary; no golden-handcuff vesting cliffs (monthly vesting, ten years to exercise).
- The strongest draw for high performers is other high performers and appealing challenges, not promises of a long-term career path. Being open about the absence of guaranteed paths was no deterrent.
- There is no "A player" island. Great hiring is great matching: one company's A player is another's B player, and people let go for not excelling in one context often excel elsewhere. "Culture fit" usually means "someone I'd like to have a beer with," which is wrongheaded; quiet, unlikely-on-paper hires can become VPs, and organizations can adapt to many styles.
- Get under the résumé: match the person's approach to problem solving to your root problems, not keywords to job descriptions. Interview probes that reveal reasoning (asking candidates to deduce which device drove a million subscribers in thirty days; asking a nervous candidate to explain complex technical work simply). Mine your own data on what great hires share (the data-science team's common trait turned out to be avid interest in music: left-brain/right-brain toggling).
- Build a culture of hiring:
  - Hiring managers are the lead recruiters and building a great team is their most important job. "Always be recruiting": candidates come from conferences, kids' soccer sidelines, airplanes. Model it from the top (the CEO personally messaging twenty LinkedIn prospects within a day).
  - Recruiters coach every hiring manager one on one on their interview process, interview team, and structure.
  - Ironclad rules: anyone who sees a stranger waiting alone for an interview stops, introduces themselves, and helps them find their next interviewer. Interviews trump any meeting, including executive staff.
  - Goal: every single candidate walks away wanting the job, even if you hated them ("we might love their next-door neighbor"). Candidates are evaluating you just as you're evaluating them.
  - The hiring manager makes the final decision (team and HR give input) and owns the team's performance. Move fast once decided: no running offers through two levels of management plus compensation plus HR. Speed closes candidates who are interviewing at other great companies.
- Consider an internal recruiting firm staffed with people from headhunting firms instead of paying outside headhunter fees; make the business case on fees saved, and gain the ability to tell managers "it's okay to lose people, we can get great new people fast."
- HR must be businesspeople: "We are a service organization, but it's not spelled S-E-R-V-A-N-T," in service to the customer, not to hiring managers. Recruiters must understand the business's root problems as deeply as product managers do. If managers say recruiters aren't smart enough to engage with, the answer is to expect and demand that they are, and hire smart ones. Consider putting a businessperson, not an HR specialist, in charge of HR.
- The annual performance review test that kills it: "What business metric is directly affected by the completion of the annual performance review?" (revenue, growth, profit?). No one can answer; "it's worth it" is not allowed as justification anywhere else in the company.

## 8. Pay People What They're Worth to You: Compensation Is a Judgment Call

Jobs are not widgets and neither are people. Salary surveys are elaborate and sophisticated but can't account for the specialized bundle of skills a real role needs, and they are always in arrears: behind current market conditions, while hiring should be about the future.

- Price the future, not the present. When a great candidate has a competing offer $35K higher than planned: weigh the extra revenue she might produce, the launch she might win you, the team members who might stay because she's a great leader, the cost of a distant-second choice plus three more months of searching, and the value of her not working for your competitor.
- Decouple compensation from the performance review process. The tight bond between reviews and salary/bonus calculation is a main reason companies can't kill the review process, which is itself a reason to decouple. Tie pay to performance, full stop, not to performance reviews; the way reviews are generally carried out, those are very different things.
- Account for the value of working for you. Some jobs create their own expertise and scarcity (the personalization engineer a competitor offered nearly 2x salary: his work had given him a whole new market value, and the P&L supported doubling the whole team's pay). Rigid internal salary ranges can financially harm your best contributors. Never use a system where people have to leave to get paid what they're worth; encourage regular outside interviewing as the most reliable market check.
- Pay top of market, not a percentile of it. "Marking to the 65th percentile" means 65% of people in the role are paid less than yours, but the math of comparing non-comparable jobs is questionable and it's disconnected from the results you want. Top of market should mean estimating the overall market value of the work this person will do for you in the time frame you need it done.
- If you can't afford top of market everywhere, identify the positions with the greatest potential to boost performance and pay top of market for those. One supremely talented person can do the work of two or more (the 80/20 pattern is not sales-only). Bain study support: on average only 15% of employees are performance "stars," but the best companies practice "intentional non-egalitarianism," clustering stars so that upward of 95% of business-critical roles are filled with A-quality talent, versus stars spread evenly elsewhere.
- On internal imbalance ("the new hire earns twice what the team makes; are they all underpaid?"): ask whether the new person moves you twice as fast and whether anyone on the team could replace him at his former company. Usually yes and no, respectively.
- Never peg offers to pay history. Two comparable candidates at $130K (her) and $150K (him): offer both $160K. Pegging to history perpetuates the wage discrimination that produced the gap. Under-offering because "she'll be over the moon at $140K" is budget-thinking, not value-thinking, and the real inequity is pay unrelated to results contributed.
- Signing bonuses are magical thinking: build $20K into year one and the person experiences the next year's 6% raise as a pay cut ($140K effective down to $127.2K). Pay the salary you need to pay.
- Transparency helps mark to market. Share your compensation rationale and even survey data; secrecy mostly protects percentile rules and irrational pay set by favoritism or seniority. If pay tracks contribution, you can defend any discrepancy openly ("here are the five times she was crucial; here's the net value of her decisions"). If you don't have a rationale you can share openly, take a hard look at why. Transparency also surfaces and hastens the cure for gender pay gaps ("my lawyers say fixing it admits we were wrong"; you are wrong, and that's the true liability; give women the information and they'll negotiate fine).

## 9. The Art of Good Good-byes: Part Ways Fast, Honestly, and Humanely

Clear communication about where the company is heading equips people to judge for themselves whether their skills and passions fit that future, and to move proactively if not. Sometimes you need more than a brilliant person; you need a brilliant person who wants to do the specific job you need done ("we need the guy who's running search to be into search").

- **The performance-feedback model: ten games at a time** (from record-holding NHL coach Scotty Bowman): an eighty-game season, and every ten games sit down with each player individually, bring their stats, gather feedback from other coaches and teammates, have the player bring a self-evaluation, and hold a conversation about what to do for the next ten games. That is the replacement for the annual review.
- The annual review's failures: rigid, tied too tightly to compensation, time-consuming and costly, and bad at its actual job. People get slammed for shortfalls from months ago that nobody mentioned when correction was possible, then handed a bad raise. Feedback from teammates and colleagues, not just the boss, defeats the "boss is biased" rationalization.
- If you can't kill the annual review, start with frequent one-on-ones anyway; quarterly appraisals are a good first step. Ask for proof: if there's no data showing the review process moves a business metric, lobby to end it. Accenture, Deloitte, and GE all dropped the traditional system (GE transitioned via pilots involving 30,000 employees, ending in a mobile app for continuous real-time feedback). Try killing it in one small corner of the company and see what happens.
- **RIP the PIP.** PIPs as usually practiced are cruel because they're really about building the case that someone is incompetent. Often nothing is wrong with the person's effort or conduct; they're just not right for the job as it's evolving. Run a PIP only when genuine, realistic improvement in a defined skill within a reasonable time is the true goal; if the goal is documentation for firing, the responsible thing is to skip it.
- When a hire can't do the job, the failure is the hiring process, not the individual. You hired the wrong person; don't make them feel it's their fault. The honest frame: not personal, not failure, a mismatch between skills and what the team now needs. Keeping a struggling but beloved under-skilled hire "we'll get up to speed" typically leaves him further behind in six months and the team exhausted covering for him; helping him into the right job elsewhere serves everyone.
- People very rarely sue, and it takes years when they do. They sue because they feel treated unfairly, which almost always means nobody told them the truth in time: either "you're being a jerk and we won't want you around if it continues," or "in six months success here looks very different, and if I were recruiting this team from scratch I'm not sure I'd hire you." Painful, but truthful, and it lets both sides assess whether the person can and wants to adapt. The PIP-as-lawsuit-shield actually fans resentment because it substitutes process for honesty.
- Engagement is not performance. A heat-map survey showed underperforming teams just as "green" (happy, engaged) as top-performing ones; only superimposing business metrics over happiness metrics distinguished them. High performers are often somewhat frustrated, pushing for results through pain and discontent. Foster commitment to achievement, not the expectation that hard work guarantees the company has your back. And retire the "family" language: every HR person has laid people off; none has laid off a family member.
- No false promises of job security. Sometimes people are hired to do something, they do it, and it's done ("if I hire people to rebuild my garage, when they're done I don't need them to rebuild the back of the house"). Recruiters will keep quoting an old "everyone has a future here" speech for years; false promises breed betrayal.
- **The algorithm** for every personnel decision: *Is what this person loves to do, that they're extraordinarily good at doing, something we need someone to be great at?* It takes the emotion out, focuses on talents rather than deficits, and works equally for employees deciding whether to stay.
- Give honest references, scoped to what you can vouch for ("I'm happy to be a reference for your technical acumen, but not your management skills"). Actively recommend departing people to other companies; one company's mismatch is another's treasure. People shouldn't carry a stigma of being "fired"; good good-byes preserve relationships (the designer who hugged the HR head years after being let go).
- Well-executed exit choreography (a manager's own plan): months of honest conversations first so the person knows it's coming; a set last day; the direct conversation in the morning; the person's team told in person the same day; email to adjacent teams after; management informed that night; the public message centered on how great the person was and that she's moving on.
- The discipline of proactively letting people go is the single hardest part of the culture for managers to adopt. It spreads manager-to-manager, not through HR ("go talk to Kevin; you'll learn more from another leader"). Managers who cross the threshold report lighter teams, better meetings, faster movement, and never look back.

## 10. Implementation: Culture as an Experimental Practice

"Culture is the strategy of how you work. And if people believe it is a strategy and that it is important, they will help you think about it deeply and try things" (Jessica Neal). Approach people management exactly the way you approach product management: test, make mistakes, rethink, try again, forever.

- You can't do it all at once. Pick where to start and prioritize, just as with the rest of the business. Not every practice fits every company, or fits right away; help teams create their own version of a freedom-and-responsibility culture.
- Expect the process to be evolutionary: some changes won't be adaptive and you'll try again; some people will find the changes uncomfortable, push back, or leave.
- Variation is fine. Different team leaders adapt practices differently; teams and whole departments can have their own cultures (engineering vs. marketing vs. content) while staying unified on the fundamental precepts. The culture must "live across the company."
- Make HR true business-building partners, in the room for staff meetings, coaching hiring managers on interviewing and feedback. The test: do they know your three key drivers of revenue? Your top four competitors? The technology about to disrupt your market? Tell them. If they don't want to know, replace them.
- Be honest about the challenges of the change itself. Fear isn't disqualifying: climbing a big mountain requires oxygen and is scary, but if a storm comes you return to base camp and nobody calls you a failure. Frame the transformation as a great adventure that will include setbacks.
