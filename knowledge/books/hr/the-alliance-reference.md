# The Alliance — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing hiring processes, or advising on team-building.*
*Book: "The Alliance: Managing Talent in the Networked Age" — Reid Hoffman, Ben Casnocha, and Chris Yeh*

---

## 1. Why Both Old Employment Models Fail

Modern employment rests on a dishonest conversation. Day one: the manager welcomes you "to the family," then HR explains you're on 90-day probation and at-will, fireable at any moment for any reason or none. Employers use fuzzy language (retain "good" employees, "indefinitely") while asking for commitment they won't reciprocate. Employees profess loyalty while scanning the market. Both sides contradict their stated positions, so neither trusts the other and neither invests fully. Managers sit in the middle, focused on keeping teams intact through the next project instead of growing people.

Two failed models in sequence:

- **Lifetime employment (old deal):** Loyal service for job security; careers as permanent as marriage, advancement like an escalator. Worked in a stable era of scale economies (GE in 1962: "maximizing employee security is a prime company goal"). Shareholder capitalism, globalization, and Moore's Law destroyed the stability that made it viable; companies now topple out of the S&P 500 faster and faster.
- **Free agency (current default):** The relationship reduced to the legal contract; employees and jobs as short-term commodities. Need to cut costs? Lay off. Need new skills? Hire different people instead of training. Employees rationally job-hop. The collapse is measurable: in a 1980s Conference Board survey, 56% of executives said loyal employees deserved assurance of continued employment; a decade later, 6%. Jack Welch by the 1990s: loyalty to a company is "nonsense." Towers Watson's 2012 study found roughly half of employees wanted to stay with their employer but most believed they'd have to leave to advance.

Core diagnosis: a business without loyalty is a business without long-term thinking; a business without long-term thinking can't invest in the future; a business not investing in the future is already dying. Trust in management is near an all-time low. A third model is needed.

## 2. The Alliance: Mutual Investment, Mutual Benefit, Explicit Terms

The alliance reframes employment as **a mutually beneficial deal, with explicit terms, between independent players** — relational rather than transactional, honest rather than fuzzy.

- Employer's pitch: "Help make our company more valuable, and we'll make *you* more valuable" — more marketable in the labor market generally, not just internally (Bain's chief talent officer tells recruits exactly this).
- Employee's pitch: "Help me grow and flourish, and I'll help the company grow and flourish."
- Employee invests in the company's success; company invests in the employee's market value. Explicit expectations on both sides restore the honesty trust requires.

**Team, not family.** Parents can't fire children; companies that call themselves "family" then run layoffs create justified feelings of betrayal. The right metaphor is a professional sports team: a specific mission, members who join to accomplish it, a roster that changes, yet real trust and mutual investment while together. Winning as a team is the best route to individual success — members of winning teams are the most sought after. "Family" survives only in how people treat each other (compassion, appreciation, respect), not in the employment terms. Netflix's Reed Hastings: "We're a team, not a family," and if you wouldn't fight to keep someone, give generous severance and open the slot for a star.

Practical payoff — the training paradox dissolves: under free agency, training people who might leave looks like subsidizing a competitor, so budgets get slashed. Under the alliance, manager and employee openly state what the company will invest and expects back, and what growth the employee seeks and what effort they'll commit in return.

## 3. Entrepreneurial Talent and the Founder Mind-Set

The capability companies most need in a fast-changing economy is entrepreneurial thinking and doing throughout the workforce — what eBay CEO John Donahoe calls the **founder mind-set**: people who "drive change, motivate people, and just get stuff done." It doesn't require founding a company; such people happily work inside firms whose employment relationship encourages entrepreneurial behavior instead of punishing it. Employees not investing aggressively in their own careers probably can't deliver the quick, decisive action an adaptive company needs.

- Old economy: efficiency was the cardinal virtue; employees on fixed tracks toward ever-deeper specialization. When markets shift, specialization flips from asset to liability (the buggy-whip problem).
- Cautionary case — Disney/Lasseter: Disney fired young animator John Lasseter for being "too distracted" by his computer-animation idea, then spent over $7 billion buying Pixar two decades later to bring him and the business back. Entrepreneurial talent treated as commodity, not ally.
- Positive case — Amazon/AWS: the AWS idea came not from Bezos but from engineering manager Benjamin Black's 2003 paper proposing selling virtual servers. Bezos backed it against board skepticism; AWS contributed an estimated $3.8B revenue in 2013. Multibillion-dollar ideas from "ordinary" employees are exactly what the alliance is designed to harvest.
- Intuit CEO Brad Smith: a leader's job isn't to put greatness into people but to recognize it exists and create the environment where it can emerge.

Silicon Valley's real secret isn't perks or stock options; it's how companies build alliances with entrepreneurial employees. The talent techniques that work in the fastest-moving, hardest-to-retain market are battle-tested for anywhere.

## 4. Tours of Duty: The Organizing Unit of the Alliance

A **tour of duty** is an ethical (not legal) commitment by employer and employee to a specific, finite mission with defined benefits for both sides. It borrows one thing from the military metaphor: honorably accomplishing a defined mission. It blends the trust/investment of lifetime employment with the flexibility of free agency, and builds trust incrementally — commitment deepens as each side proves itself. Recruiting pitch value: a concrete tour with specific outcomes beats "you'll get valuable experience."

**Three tour types:**

- **Rotational** — standardized, interchangeable, not personalized. Two flavors: (a) fixed-term entry-level programs (bank/consulting analyst programs of 2–4 years; Google People Ops' 27-month, three-rotation program; Facebook's 18-month, three-rotation PM program) that serve as on-ramps and mutual fit assessment; (b) ongoing structured roles at any career stage where talent swaps in and out (assembly line, delivery driver). Provides **scalability**.
- **Transformational** — personalized, negotiated one-on-one, defined by mission completion rather than calendar. Core promise: the employee transforms both career and company; the résumé should look considerably more impressive afterward. Rule of thumb: an initial Transformational tour runs **2–5 years** — near universal across industries (product cycles, 4-year option vesting, P&G's 2–4 year brand-manager tours). Brad Smith's arc: year 1 gain context, year 2 make your definitive mark, years 3–5 implement and grow or pivot. Eric Schmidt's version: a couple of years to learn, a couple to do the job, one to arrange transition. Provides **adaptability**.
- **Foundational** — the company is the foundation of the employee's career and life, and vice versa; both sides expect it to be the last job. Effectively a marriage: presumed permanent, moral obligation to work through problems, still needs explicit ongoing conversations. Founders and CEOs are Foundational by definition; ideally most top executives too (average tenure of direct reports to the CEO at Apple, Amazon, Google exceeds a decade). Also valuable below the executive level as stewards of continuity and institutional memory ("no one ever washes a rental car"). Rarely an entry point — employees graduate into it from Transformational tours (Brad Smith joined Intuit Transformational in 2003, went Foundational as CEO in 2008). Provides **continuity**.

**Blending:** No type is superior; the mix is an alloy chosen for market conditions. Silicon Valley runs roughly 80% Transformational; a stable quasi-monopoly manufacturer leans Rotational + Foundational. The general trend is away from Foundational toward Transformational. Don't put most employees on Foundational tours — that's lifetime employment by another name, and many stars refuse Foundational commitments because their ambitions exceed the available seats (when GE picked Immelt, the passed-over candidates immediately left to be CEOs elsewhere).

**Corporate middle class:** Tours aren't just for stars. Middle-class tours often bring no title change; success is marked by network growth, project progress, new skills and opportunities. Employees here lead the process more actively themselves (75% of employees in one survey were willing to use their own time on career-advancing learning). Stars get title upgrades and manager-led tours with aggressive goals; the middle class maintains employability by helping the company adapt.

**Longer-term alliances:** A tour's end can be the start of the next tour at the same company (SAS expects employees to have three or four careers, all at SAS). Knowing when a tour ends lets you define the next one *before* the employee looks elsewhere. A trusted employee grants the **Right of First Conversation** — discussing outside plans with you before approaching other employers. Where training investments are huge and firm-specific (Boeing's decade to full engineer productivity), essentially Foundational tours make sense; the framework's point is enabling the high-trust conversation that makes wise investment possible at any length. Example of the full arc: one LinkedIn employee went from junior analyst to VP over four distinct tours in nine years, each with a scoped mission benefiting both sides; another built a business line from a $1.2M run rate to $200M over her tour, with the relationship surviving an 11-month maternity absence because the alliance was strong.

## 5. Alignment: Matching Employee Aspirations to the Mission

A company can't expect its purpose to become the employee's sole purpose (the discredited "organization man" model). The goal isn't perfect congruence but **sufficient overlap for a finite scope and time frame** — align the employee's values and aspirations with the specific mission objective, not their entire life. Required overlap scales with tour type: modest for Rotational, substantial for Transformational, near-100% for Foundational.

**Three steps to building alignment:**

1. **Establish and disseminate the company's core mission and values.** Must be specific enough that some competent people feel strong alignment and others self-select out — losing the unaligned is a feature. "Build great products, meet customer needs" is meaningless (those are results, not purposes). Contrast Walmart's crisp "Saving People Money So They Can Live Better" with boilerplate that translates to "win, but don't break the law." If the company lacks real values, a manager can define them for their own team.
2. **Learn each employee's core aspirations and values.** Expect three levels of clarity: some know exactly what they want; many have abstract "progress" aspirations (fine — help them define what progress means for this tour); some, especially juniors, haven't reflected at all. For the last group, use the **People We Admire exercise**: name three admired people, list three admired qualities of each (nine total), rank the nine — instant personal values list to compare against the company's. Expect company values to be clear and stable, employee values comparatively fuzzy and evolving.
3. **Work together to align employee, manager, and company.** Collaborative, not top-down; begin during hiring (eBay probes "Who do you look at and say, 'I want to be her someday?'" then links how time at the company serves those aspirations; Warby Parker's costume question tests values fit, not costume-wearing). Alignment often involves compromise — an employee who wants to found a company someday will leave, but interests can still align for a tour ("work here, learn skills, help the company, and I'll help you land your next thing").

**Conversation tactics:** Define company/team values in groups (CEO drafts with executives, refines with Foundational non-executives, then cascades; in companies over ~75 people, discuss drafts in small cross-functional groups — you may discover a supposedly missionary culture is actually mercenary). Define personal values one-on-one, converting implicit hints to explicit points. Build trust by opening up first: managers share their own aspirations before asking (research shows one hour of mutual deep-question sharing can generate weeks' worth of trust; Intuit interviewers tell their own life journey first to model vulnerability). Direct questions that work: "Who's the best coworker you ever worked with?" "What's your proudest career moment?"

## 6. Implementing a Transformational Tour: Conversation Structure

Replace template performance reviews with frank, structured, bidirectional conversations producing an explicit (non-binding) agreement.

**Step 1 — Start the conversation and define the mission.** Ideally during hiring; also valuable for current employees. Answer three questions in writing:
- *What is the overall objective?* A clear, concrete mission (project launch, initiative), long enough to complete; length also shaped by employee preference (many short varied tours vs. fewer longer ones trending Foundational).
- *What does success look like for the company?* Quantitative or qualitative: revenue targets, product launches, thought leadership. Ask: "How will the company be transformed by this employee?"
- *What does success look like for the employee?* New skills, experience, personal brand — usually not a title change. Ask: "How will the employee's career be transformed?" Distinguish **Big T** transformations (promotion, plum assignment — only ~20% of missions) from **small t** transformations (marketable project experience, new skills, industry endorsements) and weight small t at least as heavily.

**Step 2 — Regular checkpoints, both directions.** The mission, not the calendar, defines the tour, so annual reviews don't fit. Set checkpoints quarterly or milestone-tied. Bidirectional: the company assesses the employee's contribution *and* the employee assesses whether the company is delivering the promised career growth. Enables course corrections.

**Step 3 — Define the next tour before this one ends.** Set the conversation well in advance (the sample Statement of Alliance suggests ~12 months before tour end). Two outcomes:
- *New tour internally* — the ideal; both sides leverage past investment. Before starting, the employee helps recruit and train a successor. If no obvious next mission exists, extend the current tour but re-examine in months, not years.
- *Tour at another company* — a planned exit beats being blindsided. Help the employee honestly assess options; negotiate a transition period and a **transition checklist** (everything the company needs to finish the mission, especially who picks up the ball). Checking every box = tour completed in good order, good standing preserved.

**Step 4 — Managing the unexpected.**
- The tour is ethical, not contractual. Don't use moral suasion to trap someone in a bad role, especially if the misfit stems from management mistakes.
- Early exits go both ways: an employee taking an amazing outside offer owes a hard-working, smooth succession (even delaying departure if needed); a company reorganizing away a tour owes hard work keeping the employee's agreed growth goals on track.
- *Broken alliances have consequences:* an employee who walks mid-tour with no transition takes a reputation hit and forfeits alumni benefits and references. A company that lays off mid-tour or withholds promised growth can't expect future advocacy — and in the social media era, ex-employees talk.
- *New manager:* default is continuing the existing tour; the new manager may change the mission but inherits the moral obligation to land the employee successfully. This is why tours must be written down.
- *Poor performance:* maintain a relationship view, not knee-jerk transactions — a team doesn't cut a player for one bad game, but a month-long slump is different.
- *Internal lateral moves:* same collaborative completion process; don't block a move if the employee has built the structure for a safe handoff.

**Manager conversation guidance:** mold the conversation to tour type; be sensitive to power imbalances (whoever has the upper hand should proactively demonstrate fair dealing); manage leading indicators (mission alignment, network intelligence gathering, check-in satisfaction) to move lagging ones (retention, engagement); apply moral suasion only for genuine alliance violations, never guilt-tripping; check in regularly — consistency over time is what distinguishes this from a management fad; use vocabulary of trust/transparency/alliance and willingly discuss departure scenarios; explicitly reassure employees this isn't a prelude to firing. Tactically: schedule a formal multi-hour meeting, share the agenda in advance so the employee prepares proposals too, take notes, agree next steps, and produce a written **Statement of Alliance**.

## 7. Employee Network Intelligence

Premise: **there are more smart people outside your company than inside it** — always true in a healthy ecosystem. Each employee is a scout picking up data from the outside world, mostly through relationships. The alliance framing: *growing their professional networks helps employees transform their careers; employee networking helps the company transform itself.* In the authors' HBR reader survey, over three-quarters of respondents already used personal networks to do their jobs. Don't treat networking on the job as an infraction — fund and expect it. A visible network intelligence program also attracts entrepreneurial recruits, who are outward-facing by nature.

**Four functions of network intelligence:**

1. **Outside information source and filter** — connecting the company to knowledge circulating beyond its walls instead of just convening internal meetings.
2. **Hidden data** — nonpublic (but non-secret) knowledge living in people's heads: up-to-the-second, nuanced, not in any Google search. Case study: PayPal vs. Billpoint (eBay + Wells Fargo's rival payments venture). PayPal asked everyone down to individual engineers to gather intelligence through the eBay-ecosystem network and learned (a) Billpoint believed its bank relationship was the decisive advantage, and (b) sellers actually cared about ease of use, with fraud prevention a hygiene factor. All gathered ethically through open conversations; Billpoint never asked the reverse questions. PayPal won; eBay paid $1.5B+ for it.
3. **Serendipity** — innovation happens at intersections of disciplines; diverse networks generate the collisions. MIT research: when innovation and adaptation are critical, team success tracks how much the team reaches across boundaries to outsiders.
4. **Opportunity spotting** — seeing what you'd otherwise miss. PayPal's viral-growth formula was adapted from an eBay-ecosystem company's counter widget that spread seller-to-buyer-to-seller — an insight brought in by front-line employees, not executives, which became "Pay with PayPal" on every auction.

The trade-off is real: networked, discoverable employees are discoverable by recruiters too. There's no one-way mirror. The book's judgment: for most companies the defection risk is outweighed by the intelligence benefits — the company that lives this most fully (every employee with a public, current profile) has lost some people and gained far more.

**Implementation program (4 steps):**

1. **Recruit connected people.** Network strength ≠ follower count; it's being connected to the *right* people with the realistic ability to extract useful information or influence action. Interview for it: ask about strongest professional allies; ask how they solve problems (do they call experts?). For senior hires, ask "Who are the key people you'd consider hiring after you?" — then reference-check with those people. Hiring for network strength also signals internally that this matters.
2. **Teach employees to mine their networks.** Kill the defensive "don't talk to anyone" posture — it wrongly assumes employees can't distinguish *nonpublic* from *secret*. Employees can share hard-won experiential knowledge without revealing secrets. Equip them with standing questions: How is [key trend] shaping our industry? What are other companies doing that works or doesn't? What are customers feeling and how has it changed? Who should we engage with? Who are interesting new entrants? Etiquette: with a friend at a competitor, steer to a third company; anonymize sources when reporting back. Then build a **push process** so intelligence reaches management — e.g., circulating everyone's external meetings weekly for question suggestions, a standing "most interesting person you talked to this week" round, or a $100 prize at each partner meeting for the best rumor, confirmed or not.
3. **Roll out network-building programs and policies.**
   - *Discoverability:* encourage complete, professional public profiles (when Netflix's CEO found nine of his top 170 stars weren't on LinkedIn, he asked them to sign up so internal nodes were findable). Never mandate personal branding — forced tweeting reads as inauthentic.
   - *Networking fund:* the corporate version of an "interesting person fund" — employees expense meals with interesting people, with reporting-back expected. HubSpot's Learning Meals lets any employee take anyone out if they'll learn something; LinkedIn requires a summary of what was learned on the expense report. Social reach converts to results: HubSpot employees average 6.2x the connections of the average member and 8x the sharing rate, and the company draws twice the average number of applicants per posting.
   - *Speaking gigs:* cover travel/lodging for employees who land speaking slots; one company funds employees to host mini-conferences for users in other cities. Thought-leader employees build both the company brand and their own.
   - *Host events on site:* industry events, plus letting employees use company space for clubs and associations (open to any colleague who wants to attend).
4. **Have employees share what they learn.** Unclaimed learning is like flying without a frequent-flyer number. Formats from a simple email to full presentations: post-conference brown-bag lunches, an internal portal for publishing insights. One luxury-goods firm gave designers a half-day weekly for network research with mandatory monthly show-and-tell — it retained two independent-minded designers who would have left and produced novel product lines (e.g., the first luxury line using skateboard materials, sourced from a designer's friend in the skateboard business). Founders modeling the behavior matters: reporting your own external-meeting findings back to the team sets the norm.

**Conversation guidance:** bake network intelligence into the tour of duty explicitly — "we give you time and budget to build your network; you deploy it toward your mission." Frame it as a mutual asset, not an employee perk. Personally model the programs (policies honored only on paper stay unused). One team exercise: have every employee list the three smartest people they know outside the company — a bench for speakers, problem-solving, and relationship-strengthening.

## 8. Corporate Alumni Networks

Lifetime employment is over; **lifetime relationship** is the new ideal. The alliance should persist after the final tour. The canonical proof: LinkedIn, Tesla, YouTube, Yelp, Yammer, and SpaceX were all founded by alumni of one company (PayPal). Demand already exists without companies lifting a finger: LinkedIn hosts 118,000+ corporate alumni groups covering 98% of the Fortune 500, yet most have no relationship with the company. A University of Twente study: only 15% of surveyed companies had formal alumni networks, but another 67% had employees running informal ones on their own time and money — a massive unclaimed asset, because informal groups serve alumni-to-alumni value, not the company.

**Who already gets the ROI:** professional services firms (McKinsey's official program since the 1960s, 24,000+ members; Bain with nine full-timers on alumni work, six placing alumni into executive roles at clients) and universities — both because alumni drive *direct revenue* (client referrals, hiring the firm, donations). Elsewhere returns are uncertain and serendipitous, so companies ignore the opportunity — but unpredictable ≠ low value, and the costs are far lower than assumed.

**Four returns:**

1. **Hiring.** (a) *Boomerangs* — outsider perspective plus insider knowledge; staying in touch makes return likelier (one firm doubled its rehire rate within two years of launching its alumni network; another runs a program letting alumni sign up for contract assignments that can convert to full-time). (b) *Referrals* — several large firms pay cash bonuses to alumni who refer successful hires; alumni also help with reference checks and culture-fit reads. (c) *Closing candidates* — alumni act as credible surrogates for what working there does to a career (McKinsey absolutely reminds recruits its alumni run hundreds of billion-dollar companies). A network producing even a few hires a year is worth six figures on hiring value alone.
2. **Intelligence.** Alumni combine outside knowledge with understanding of how the organization works. Regular standing polls surface employer-brand perception, competitive intel, and customer leads. Alumni are also trusted truth-tellers — an alum beta-testing a product gives more honest feedback than a current employee, and rumors about emerging competitors get taken more seriously coming from former employees.
3. **Customer referrals.** Alumni become or refer customers, especially when incentivized (even high-end swag helps). B2B: incentivize direct customer introductions (one alum-turned-executive can be worth millions). B2C: focus on influencers.
4. **Brand ambassadors.** Third parties with credibility current employees can't duplicate — they're not paid to tweet. Especially powerful once alumni outnumber current staff. Brand strength then compounds the other three returns.

**Three investment levels:**

1. **Ignore** — defensible only for tiny startups; once alumni number in the dozens/hundreds, ignoring forfeits the returns (informal groups run for their moderators' benefit).
2. **Support** — connect with existing organizers and ask "What can we do for you?": mailing lists, pizza at meetups, endorsement. Minimal cost, real returns (one consultancy's 31,000-member group has yielded significant recruiting savings via boomerang hires). Almost every company should at least do this.
3. **Invest** — company-run official group, dedicated (full/part-time) staff, systematic benefits (e.g., store discounts), coordination with current employees for intelligence gathering. Example pattern: hosting "class of [year]" reunion dinners explicitly modeled on college reunions.

**Reference model (the book's worked example):** launch the formal network before the alumni pool balloons; include all ex-employees in good standing; run it as a group on an existing social platform; have one HR person post company news *repurposed from an existing internal comms email* (near-zero incremental effort); seed the group with useful questions from operating managers; quarterly email with updates, alumni news, and a short survey; gifts — a free premium subscription for every alum, nice gifts plus personal notes for referral hires or customers. Layer on an **invitation-only distinguished alumni network** selected by the executive team (based on contributions as employee, contributions as alum, or career accomplishments) with special benefits: event invitations, hackathon judging, fireside chats. Total incremental cost is small versus recruiter fees or hired analysts. Treat the alumni network as a potential profit center, not a line-item cost.

**Implementation (4 steps):**

1. **Decide who's included.** Simplest: all ex-employees; exclude fired-for-cause and pending-legal-action cases; contractors/interns case-by-case. Retain the ability to "fire" individuals from the network for toxic behavior (competitor poaching, public bad-mouthing). The distinguished tier is the cleaner long-term instrument: more service for more engagement, and losing distinguished status is a meaningful deterrent.
2. **Explicitly define expectations and benefits — reciprocity.** Common programs: referral bonuses on posted openings; product discounts and beta whitelist access (one large tech company gives alumni up to 90% off at its company store — a benefit *and* a source of informed, objective product feedback); hosted events and reunions (the engine of university alumni engagement); official recognition/badging for select alumni (modeled on Top Reviewer / Power Seller / Elite programs — weigh favoritism perception); keeping alumni informed (better-informed alumni give more practical help; alumni learning bad news from the press instead of the company is a breakdown of the alliance).
3. **Comprehensive exit process.** Skipping this is setting up a trade-show booth and not collecting business cards. At exit: decide whether the person merits distinguished-tier sponsorship; collect everything needed for a long-term relationship (permanent email, phone, profile URLs, expertise, what they'd like to help with). Build *organizational* connectivity, not just the manager's personal relationship.
4. **Build links between current employees and alumni.** Value doesn't flow automatically. Set up formal channels before you need them: alumni advisory councils, topic mailing lists mixing employees and distinguished alumni, regular events where execs mingle with alumni — standard parts of the problem-solving toolkit. One company sends an executive to every alumni meeting to run an open 30–40 minute AMA including confidential questions. Include alumni in big wins (at one IPO, the founder had 45 personalized bobbleheads made for early supporters, most of them already alumni).

**Conversation guidance:** three moments each need a different alumni conversation — hiring (use the network as a selling point: stats plus your own personal stories of it helping you), during employment (make tapping alumni easy and obvious; name-drop specific alumni in problem-solving meetings; circulate impressive alumni profiles), and exit (reframe the exit interview: the employment ends, the alliance lives on — with you and with the company).

## 9. The Statement of Alliance and Getting Started

The tour of duty conversation's final deliverable is a written **Statement of Alliance** — personalized per employee, but with the same general policies applied fairly across the team. In larger organizations, senior management and HR customize the template while leaving managers leeway to adapt it. Structure:

- **Preamble:** the relationship is a mutual alliance; no lifetime-employment commitment either way, but a commitment to maintain a long-term alliance even if employment ends. The company helps transform the employee's market value; the employee helps transform the company.
- **Article 1 — Tour of Duty:** mission objective (blank to fill), expected duration, what success looks like for the company, what success looks like for the employee; if progressing toward mutual goals, the company won't fire and the employee won't leave; with ~12 months remaining, discuss the next tour or transition; deepening investment may someday lead to a Foundational commitment.
- **Article 2 — Alignment:** all three parties (employee, manager, company) articulate core aspirations and values; work to maximize overlap knowing it won't be 100%; recognize both business results and values exemplified; address gaps explicitly rather than letting them fester.
- **Article 3 — Network Intelligence:** the employee's network is an asset to both parties; company provides time, equipment, and an expense budget (up to $X, larger amounts by ask) for network-building; employee commits to deploying the network for the mission and sharing what's learned; manager clarifies what counts as nonpublic-but-shareable vs. secret; company facilities available for hosting external groups.
- **Article 4 — Alumni Network:** departure in good standing brings an alumni invitation; same principles continue (mutual trust, investment, benefit); company keeps alumni informed and flags relevant opportunities; alumni give company requests a fair hearing but may decline; current employees may tap the alumni network for business challenges.

**Supporting tools from the book's appendices:** the People We Admire values exercise (three admired people × three qualities each, ranked 1–9) as the standard alignment jump-starter, and the authors' companion site/community for worksheets, industry adaptations, and variations.

**Adoption path:** ideally companywide and CEO-led (values definition and alumni networks especially benefit from CEO sponsorship), but the primary implementers are managers — every element (tours, alignment conversations, networking funds, alumni outreach) can be piloted at the single-team level. The closing logic: mutual investment restores long-term thinking, and honest conversations about goals and timelines — including scenarios where the employee leaves — are what make great people decide to stay ("opt-in culture": you can't oblige people to stay; you create an environment where they *decide* to).
