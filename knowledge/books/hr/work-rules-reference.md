# Work Rules! — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing hiring processes, or advising on team-building.*
*Book: "Work Rules!: Insights from Inside Google That Will Transform How You Live and Lead" — Laszlo Bock*

---

## 1. The Founding Premise: High-Freedom Management

The core choice every leader makes, usually without realizing it: do you believe people are fundamentally good, or not? Everything else follows from that answer.

**Two extreme models:**
- **Low-freedom:** command-and-control. Common because it's profitable, requires less effort, and managers fear the alternative. Employees managed tightly, worked intensely, discarded.
- **High-freedom:** employees treated with dignity and given a real voice. The most talented people on the planet are increasingly mobile, connected, and discoverable, and they flow to high-freedom companies.

**Evidence that freedom outperforms:**
- MIT's Richard Locke compared two Nike T-shirt plants in Mexico. Plant A (workers set production targets, self-organized teams, authority to stop production): 150 shirts/day per worker, higher wages, $0.11/shirt cost. Plant B (tight control, strict rules): 80 shirts/day, $0.18/shirt. Nearly 2x productivity, 40% lower cost per unit.
- Kamal Birdi (University of Sheffield) studied 308 companies over 22 years. Operations programs (TQM, just-in-time inventory) showed "no overall performance effect" in aggregate. Performance improved only when companies empowered employees, provided learning beyond job needs, or increased team autonomy: +9% value added per employee.

**Deliberately strip managers of unilateral power.** Decisions a manager should NOT make alone: whom to hire, whom to fire, performance ratings, salary/bonus/stock amounts, promotions, management awards, when work is good enough to ship. Each goes to a group of peers, a committee, or an independent team. What's left for managers: "Managers serve the team." Clear roadblocks, inspire, coach.

**Decision hierarchy still matters:** decisions should be made at the lowest possible level; escalate only questions where, given the same data, senior leaders would decide differently. Someone must break ties. Micromanagement is mismanagement.

**Work as calling:** Amy Wrzesniewski (Yale) found people see work as a job (necessity), a career (something to win), or a calling (fulfilling, socially useful). Across every occupation studied (doctors, nurses, teachers, librarians, engineers, secretaries), roughly one-third saw their work as a calling, and those people were happier and healthier. It's framing, not occupation.

**"Founder" is an attitude, not a cap table.** Anyone can be the founder of a team's culture. Choose whether to be a founder or an employee, then act like a founder and leave room for others to found alongside you.

## 2. Culture Eats Strategy: Mission, Transparency, Voice

Ed Schein's three levels of culture: artifacts (visible perks, offices), espoused values, and underlying assumptions. Outsiders copy artifacts (beanbags, slides); the values and assumptions underneath are what matter. Culture built on three cornerstones:

**1. Mission that matters**
- Works when it's a moral goal, not a business goal. No mention of profit, market, customers, or shareholders. It should be unachievable, so it always pulls forward (a compass, not a speedometer). "Be the market leader" inspires nothing once accomplished.
- The mechanism is personal connection to impact. Adam Grant's call-center study: fund-raisers who read stories from scholarship recipients (the beneficiaries of their work) grew weekly pledges 155% (9 to 23/week) and revenue 143% ($1,288 to $3,130). Fund-raisers who met a recipient for five minutes raised weekly funds over 400% more. Meeting the people you help is the single strongest motivator; even reading about them works. Watch for and share "magic moments" (real beneficiary stories) with the team.

**2. Transparency ("default to open")**
- Assume all information can be shared with the team; restricting information should be a conscious effort requiring a good reason. A new engineer gets access to nearly the entire codebase on day one; roadmaps, launch plans, weekly status snippets, and everyone's quarterly goals (OKRs) sit on the intranet; the board presentation is walked through with the whole company days later.
- Weekly all-hands with unrestricted Q&A is the highest-value ritual; questions crowdsourced and voted on so leadership answers what people actually care about.
- Transparency itself improves performance: after New York State required hospitals to publish coronary-bypass death rates, deaths fell 41% in four years.
- Anti-politics rule: if you write a nasty email about someone, expect them to be added to the thread. Forces direct conversation.
- Prevents redundant work and internal rivalry (contrast: GM's five brands competing against each other in the same sedan category while Toyota fielded one Camry).

**3. Voice**
- Give employees a real say in how the organization runs; many of the best people-practices come from employee suggestions. Research (Ethan Burris): employee voice improves decision quality, team performance, and organizational performance.
- Mechanism example: "Bureaucracy Busters." Employees submit and vote on their biggest frictions (first round: 570 ideas, 55,000+ votes), then leadership implements the winners. Most frustrations are small, cheap, and fixable.
- Culture is proven when tested: hard decisions should be resolved by values, not economics (culture shaping strategy, not vice versa). Expect constant low-grade paranoia that "the culture is degrading." That vigilance is healthy; worry when it stops.

**Work rule:** give people slightly more trust, freedom, and authority than you're comfortable with. If you're not nervous, you haven't given them enough.

## 3. The Hiring Bar: Why Hiring Beats Training

**The two paths to exceptional talent:** (A) hire 90th-percentile performers who do great work immediately, or (B) hire average performers and hope training makes them exceptional. Path B is what most companies actually do while claiming otherwise. It fails because:
- Most interviewers are bad at interviewing and never check their predictions against later performance ("Lake Wobegon effect": everyone believes their hires are above average).
- ~90% of training produces no sustained behavior change. When mediocre performers later became stars, it's almost always from changed context/work, not training.
- Companies spend more per employee on training ($606) than hiring ($456). Spending on training is evidence of failing to hire well, not of investing in people.

**Core prescriptions:**
- **Front-load the people investment:** spend the majority of people time/money on attracting and assessing. Spend twice the average on recruiting as a share of the people budget. The worst case with a 90th-percentile hire is an average year; an average hire can consume massive training resources and land below average.
- **Hire more slowly.** Only ~10% of applicants at best are top performers, and true top performers aren't looking (they're succeeding where they are). Keep roles open until the exceptional candidate appears. Optimize against false positives: better to miss two great hires than let in one lousy one. Bad performers are toxic and consume management time.
- **Only hire people better than you** in some meaningful way. If you can't name what a candidate is better at than you, don't hire them. Waiting years for the right person is acceptable.
- **Take hiring decisions away from the hiring manager.** Managers compromise standards as searches drag on ("by day ninety most managers will take anyone who will answer a phone"), are biased toward friends and favors, and gain too much power over their team when they own hiring.
- **Don't hire exclusively for smarts.** A single attribute (intelligence, pedigree) is not "best." Screen for humility and conscientiousness too; hire people who will succeed in *your* context and make everyone around them better. Accomplishment beats pedigree; top-of-class at a state school beats an average Ivy grad, and for some roles college doesn't matter.
- An exceptional performer in high-leverage roles can be worth 300x an average one ("I'd rather lose an entire incoming class of engineering graduates than one exceptional technologist" — Alan Eustace).

## 4. The Hiring Machine: Sourcing, Committees, Self-Correction

**The quality-decay problem:** founders hire to their own standard; each hiring generation drifts slightly lower because successors don't share the founders' exact bar, plus favor-hires creep in. Fix: an objective standard enforced by a central, final reviewer (or review team) who does nothing but uphold the bar, up to and including the CEO reviewing every hire. The visible message that hiring is taken seriously at the top matters as much as the review itself.

**Sourcing framework (in priority order):**
1. **Referrals, managed well.** Raising the referral bonus from $2,000 to $4,000 changed nothing; referrals are intrinsically motivated (people refer because they love the place). What does increase referral volume (+1/3): "aided recall" — ask specific questions ("Who's the best finance person you've ever worked with?" "Who's the best Ruby developer you know?") instead of "know anyone?"; run "sourcing jams" where groups methodically walk their LinkedIn/social contacts with recruiters on standby. Also: give referred candidates white-glove treatment (contact within 48 hours, weekly status updates to the referrer) or referrers stop referring.
2. **In-house sourcing of passive candidates.** The best people aren't applying anywhere. Build an internal recruiting capability that identifies and cultivates them over years ("Knowable Universe" exercise: systematically map essentially every person in a target company, school, or profession, review the list with insiders, then build relationships). Delivers more than half of hires at lower cost than agencies.
3. **Own careers site** (candid job info, ongoing relationship with near-miss candidates whose skills you may need later).
4. **External recruiters** only for unknown local markets and highly confidential searches. Variance in quality is greater *within* firms than across them; pick the individual consultant, not the brand.
5. **Job boards: essentially worthless.** Many applicants, vanishingly few hires; dropped entirely.
- Be willing to do crazy things for extraordinary people, including hiring an entire intact team on their terms (location included). Failed experiments teach too: a famous puzzle billboard generated press and zero hires, and solo-contest winners often aren't team players.

**Decision structure — layered, disinterested review:**
- **Hiring committee** of people familiar with the role but with no direct stake in it (hiring manager excluded; members recuse on candidates for their own teams). Reviews a full packet: interview notes, scores, references.
- **Senior leader review** as a second calibration layer; most common rejection reason at this stage is culture (e.g., brilliant but arrogant: "a great candidate, but not for us").
- **Final single-owner review** for the whole company.
- Include a **subordinate** (someone who would report to the candidate) and a **cross-functional interviewer** (no connection to the role, so no interest in filling it and less thin-slice bias) on every panel.
- Equal weight for every interviewer's feedback: a subordinate's assessment counts at least as much as the hiring manager's. Averaged scores beat any individual: across five thousand interviews analyzed, essentially no single interviewer out-predicted the crowd average. Averaging also kills blackballing and politicking.

**Self-correction loops:**
- **Rule of Four:** four interviews predict the hire decision with 86% confidence; each additional interviewer adds ~1%. Capping on-site interviews cut median time-to-hire from 90-180 days to 47 and saved hundreds of thousands of employee hours.
- **Revisit program:** algorithmically re-score recently rejected resumes against keywords weighted from successful hires; recruiters take a second look at high scorers. One run: 300,000 rejected resumes, 10,000 revisited, 150 hires — a 1.5% yield vs. the 0.25% baseline (6x better).
- **Interviewer feedback:** every interviewer sees their historical scores vs. actual hire outcomes; consistently wrong interviewers get trained or pulled from interviewing.
- **Kill signals that don't predict:** GPA and test scores don't predict performance beyond 2-3 years out of school, so stop asking except for new grads.
- **Candidate experience is a KPI:** survey every interviewee; require time for their questions; treat rejected candidates so well they still recommend you (benchmark achieved: 80% of *rejected* candidates would recommend a friend apply).

**Four-step summary for any team:** (1) set a high bar for quality before you start and never compromise; (2) find your own candidates; (3) assess objectively (subordinates and peers on panels, written evidence, unbiased group decides); (4) give candidates a reason to join (mission plus the people they'll work with — a stack of teammate resumes closes candidates better than any pitch).

## 5. Structured Interviewing and the Four Attributes

**Why gut instinct fails:** judgments formed in the first 10 seconds of an interview predict its outcome (Prickett/Gada-Jain/Bernieri thin-slice study), and the rest of the interview is spent confirming that snap impression (confirmation bias). Typical interviews mostly measure the interviewer's biases.

**What predicts job performance (Schmidt & Hunter 1998 meta-analysis, 85 years of research; % of performance explained):**
- Work sample tests: **29%** (best single predictor; have candidates do a realistic slice of the actual job, e.g. write real code in the interview)
- General cognitive ability tests: **26%** (predictive because they capture learning ability, but standardized tests carry demographic bias)
- Structured interviews: **26%** (predictive even for unstructured jobs; also perceived as fairest by candidates)
- Combination of GCA + conscientiousness assessment: **36%** (combinations beat any single technique; conscientious people "work to completion" and act like owners)
- Unstructured interviews: **14%**
- Reference checks: **7%**
- Years of work experience: **3%**
- Brainteasers and case puzzles ("golf balls in a 747"): near-worthless; at best a practiceable skill, at worst interviewer self-indulgence with no link to performance.

**Structured interviewing mechanics:**
- Two question types: **behavioral** ("Tell me about a time…" — match past achievement to job requirements) and **situational** ("What would you do if…" — job-relevant hypothetical). Probe deeply into the veracity and thought process behind answers.
- Questions are deliberately bland; the *answers* differentiate. Consistency is the point: same questions, same standard, so variance comes from the candidate, not the interviewer.
- Score against a defined rubric with explicit performance levels per component. Interviewer must record the attribute assessed, the question asked, the candidate's answer, and their assessment, so later reviewers can independently re-judge the evidence.
- Provide interviewers a question bank/tool so good, pre-validated questions are the path of least resistance. Refresh questions so they don't leak. (Public starting point the book recommends: the US Department of Veterans Affairs' library of ~100 sample structured questions.)
- At least two independent interviewers must assess each attribute.

**The four attributes to screen (in this order of emphasis):**
1. **General cognitive ability** — learning ability and real problem-solving, not GPA.
2. **Emergent leadership** — steps into leadership when the team needs it, and just as critically recedes back into the team when the need passes; ignores formal titles. Strong bias against self-championing "I"-heavy leaders who focus on what they accomplished rather than how.
3. **Culture fit** (define your own version) — intellectual humility (can't learn if you can't admit you're wrong), conscientiousness (owners, not employees), comfort with ambiguity, enjoyment of fun, and evidence of courageous or interesting paths taken in life.
4. **Role-related knowledge — deliberately the least important.** Veterans replicate the solution that worked before (Maslow's hammer); curious generalists more often invent something new. Screen expertise deeply only for genuinely technical roles, and even there prefer broad expert understanding over narrow specialization. At scale, balance a portfolio of generalists and experts.

Also: the interview is a two-way sell. The candidate is making a bigger decision than you are (a company has many employees; a person has one job). Always leave 10 minutes for their questions; make even rejected candidates feel it was worth their day.

## 6. Give Away Power: Managing Without Sticks and Carrots

**The problem with power:** managers tend to amass and exert it (Lord Acton: holders of power must be held to *higher* standards, not given the benefit of the doubt); employees tend to defer and follow orders (Milgram: 65% of subjects administered maximum "shocks" on instruction). Both dynamics kill ownership thinking. People also self-create hierarchy without prompting (the head seat at the table is always left for the boss). Breaking this requires deliberate, visible countermeasures.

**Mechanisms of mass empowerment:**
- **Eliminate status symbols.** Few meaningful levels (e.g., IC, manager, director, VP) plus a parallel individual-contributor track with equal rewards for technical people. No executive dining rooms, parking, pensions, or exclusive perks; benefits offered to all levels or none. Ban self-aggrandizing titles ("global," "strategy"). Symbols matter: leaders sitting mid-table, biking to work, cleaning up their own messes send louder signals than policy.
- **Decide with data, not the highest-paid opinion.** "If you have facts, present them and we'll use them. If you have opinions, we're gonna use mine" (Jim Barksdale) is how most companies actually run; invert it. Data transforms managers from providers of intuition into facilitators of a search for truth, and frees leadership to focus on what can't be quantified. Run experiments instead of debating (thousands of A/B and 1%-rollout tests a year on product; apply the same method to people programs — pilot every major change on a subgroup first).
- **Bust myths with published data.** Employees infer unfairness from biased samples (Wald's WWII bomber-armor insight: the bullet holes you see are on the planes that survived). Counter by publishing the actual data, e.g. promotion analyses showing that working with senior people, product area, office location, and receiving constructive feedback have little or no effect on promotion odds. A promotion packet with *no* critical feedback is the real warning sign.
- **Let people shape their work:** 20%-style discretionary time (in practice ~10% utilization; the *idea* matters more than the accounting — it's an outlet for the most determined people, operating somewhat outside formal oversight; successful projects start at 5-10% of someone's time and grow by demonstrated impact). Let employees redesign systems that affect them: engineers given anonymized pay data redesigned bonuses to be a percentage of role-median salary instead of actual salary, removing negotiation-skill bias (which structurally disadvantages women: 57% of male MBAs negotiated starting pay vs. 7% of women in the Babcock study).
- **Annual all-employee survey done right:** ~100 questions, ~90% participation, confidential or fully anonymous, written in-house not by consultants, all results (good and bad) shared with the whole company within a month, senior leaders' reports auto-published. Measure concrete outcomes (innovation, execution, retention), not vague "engagement" — engagement doesn't tell you where to invest. Five questions predict attrition: below 70% favorable on them means people will leave in the next year unless you intervene. Survey results drive the next year of employee-led improvement work; never feed manager survey scores into ratings or pay.
- **Expect a lot.** Difficult, specific goals outperform "do your best" exhortations (Locke & Latham). The manager who asks "Do I need to review this?" until the answer is an honest "no" builds more capability than the one who edits every draft. People live up or down to expectations, and every bit of control a manager gives up creates an opportunity for the team to step up.

## 7. Performance Management: Separate Ratings from Development

**The core insight (keep this if nothing else): evaluation and development must be split.** Evaluation distributes finite resources (raises, bonuses); development grows people. Combine them in one conversation and learning shuts down — the employee argues about the rating instead of absorbing the feedback. Extrinsic rewards crowd out intrinsic motivation (Deci & Ryan's puzzle experiments: paid subjects worked 26% more while paid, then 37% less than their paid level once payment stopped). Employees have rational incentives to argue ratings endlessly, and managers cave — or pre-cave (managers given a "limited funds" framing gave men 71% of raise money vs. 29% to women with identical rating distributions, purely to avoid anticipated tough conversations — Belliveau study). Practical rule: hold the annual review, the pay conversation, and equity decisions in different months. Development feedback should be continuous, never a year-end surprise.

**Goal setting (OKRs):**
- Objectives and Key Results (Intel via John Doerr): results must be specific, measurable, verifiable; include both a quality and an efficiency measure so people can't optimize one at the other's expense (relevant AND fast).
- Set ambitious goals you won't fully hit; ~70% attainment is healthy. "If you set a crazy, ambitious goal and miss it, you'll still achieve something remarkable."
- Leadership sets top-level OKRs quarterly; everyone's OKRs are public next to their name. Don't cascade goals bureaucratically — research shows goals improve performance but cascading them wastes enormous time. Visibility creates market-style alignment; grossly misaligned teams stand out.

**Ratings:**
- Scale design barely matters (3, 5, or 6 points — no external evidence favors any). A 41-point scale was false precision: managers overrode its pay implications two-thirds of the time, and nobody could reliably distinguish a 3.2 from a 3.3 (unreliable *and* invalid). Landed on 5 labels: needs improvement / consistently meets / exceeds / strongly exceeds / superb.
- More categories = *less* differentiation: teams that subdivided 5 buckets into 15 put only 1% of people in the top bucket vs. 5% for plain 5-bucket teams, unknowingly dropping 4 of 5 genuine top performers out of the top rating.
- Reduce frequency: moving from quarterly to semiannual assessment halved the time cost with no harm. Don't force one distribution on all teams; different jobs and teams legitimately have different performance shapes, and freeing the distribution doubled managers' use of the extremes (which better matched reality).

**Calibration is the soul of the system:**
- Manager assigns a *draft* rating; then groups of 5-10 managers of similar teams meet, review all their people together, and agree final ratings. Removes inflation pressure, aligns idiosyncratic standards (easy vs. hard graders), forces managers to justify decisions to peers, diminishes bias, and makes ratings portable and trusted across the org. This is why employees can be twice as favorable toward ratings as industry average.
- Open every calibration meeting with a one-page handout of common assessor errors (recency bias etc.). Simply naming them at the start measurably reduces distortions and creates a shared vocabulary for policing them mid-meeting ("Wait, that's recency bias — look at the whole period").

**Peer feedback:**
- Annual feedback from peers and juniors, not just the manager (peers see the real person; managers get gamed by upward-managers). Template design matters enormously: switching from "list 3-5 strengths / 3-5 weaknesses" to *one* thing to do more of and *one* thing to change cut review-writing time 27% and raised "this was helpful" 26 points. Cap project descriptions (512 characters) — if a reviewer needs more explanation, they don't actually know the work. Give employees a one-page discussion guide for the review conversation (+14 points on conversation quality).

**Promotions:**
- Decided by committees calibrated against prior promotions and defined standards, using peer feedback — not by the boss. Allow self-nomination. Observed gender dynamic: women self-nominate less until leadership explicitly encourages it (a single recurring email), then nominate at equal rates and are promoted at slightly *higher* rates; publish the data and repeat the nudge. Rejected candidates get specific feedback on what to fix for next time — obvious, and vanishingly rare in practice.

## 8. The Two Tails: Compassion for the Bottom, Study the Top

**Premise:** performance in most jobs follows a power-law distribution, not a bell curve — a small elite dominates through massive output (Aguinis & O'Boyle). The biggest improvement opportunities live in the two tails: moving someone from the 5th to the 50th percentile is worth far more than nudging a 40th-percentile performer to 50th.

**Bottom tail ("compassionate pragmatism"):**
- Regularly identify the bottom ~5%, *outside* the formal rating process (includes chronic "skimmers" along the bottom edge of meeting expectations). The goal is not firing, it's help. Tell them directly: "You're in the bottom 5% of performers. I'm telling you because I want to help you grow."
- Diagnose skill vs. will. Skill gaps: targeted training and coaching (interventions reserved for the few who struggle, not blanket training for everyone). If that fails: help them find a different internal role. Typical outcome: performance rises to about average — the person who outperformed 4 colleagues now outperforms 49. Will problems are personal issues or a signal something bigger is wrong with the team.
- The remainder leave or are exited, and tend to end up happier at companies where the demands fit them. Be direct early: letting underperformers "plod along for years" and axing them at 45 in a downturn is the cruel option (Welch's own later framing).
- This is explicitly NOT stack ranking. Forced-distribution rank-and-yank makes employees compete against each other instead of against competitors and can poison a culture (Microsoft employees universally named it the company's most destructive process before it was abolished).

**Top tail — put your best people under a microscope:**
- Benchmarking tells you what worked elsewhere; performance is context-dependent (Groysberg: star analysts who switch firms suffer "an immediate and lasting decline" — their success rode on colleagues, resources, culture fit, and brand). So study *your own* stars in *your own* environment, comparing extremes rather than averages. Slice specifically: not "best salesperson" but "best at closing new accounts of a certain size."
- Then turn findings into checklists and make the stars the teachers.

**Project Oxygen (the manager-quality template — the method transfers even if the list doesn't):**
1. Test the null hypothesis ("managers don't matter") with two data sources: team performance ratings and bottom-up survey scores about the manager.
2. Isolate the extremes: top 25% and bottom 25% on both measures. Aggregate differences looked trivial (86% vs. 78% favorable) until decomposed: teams under the best managers scored 5-18% better on fairness, career development, decision speed, respect, empowerment, and work-life freedom. Manager quality was the single best predictor of whether employees stayed or left ("people don't quit companies, they quit bad managers").
3. Causality via natural experiment: employees who switched from best to worst managers dropped significantly on 34 of 42 survey dimensions the next year; switchers in the other direction improved.
4. Double-blind interviews of best and worst managers to extract behavioral differences; validate against award nominations, survey comments, and peer feedback.
5. Result — 8 behaviors of the best managers, ranked: (1) be a good coach; (2) empower, don't micromanage; (3) show interest in team members' success and personal well-being; (4) be productive and results-oriented; (5) communicate — listen and share information; (6) help with career development; (7) have a clear vision/strategy for the team; (8) have key technical skills to advise the team. Technical expertise came *last* among differentiators (still table stakes, but the smallest difference-maker). Make each behavior specific ("prepare for 1:1s by thinking about the person's strengths and circumstances, then ask questions rather than dictate answers").
6. Operationalize with a semiannual **upward feedback survey** whose items ARE the checklist (one question per behavior plus "I would recommend my manager"). The survey is the checklist (Gawande: checklists beat training when complexity outstrips human capacity). Results go to each manager for development only, never wired to ratings or pay — otherwise managers game the survey and purge unhappy reports. Retaliation fear proved empirically negligible (rating changes moved manager UFS scores by 0.03 on a 100-point scale). Most managers voluntarily share results with their teams and ask how to improve; that inversion drives the change.
7. Have winners teach: best-manager award recipients train others as a condition of the award. Targeted courses per attribute lift scores measurably ("Manager as Coach" +13% coaching scores).
- Outcome over two years: average manager favorability 83% to 88%; bottom-quartile managers 70% to 77% — the bottom quartile became nearly as good as the old average. "It became harder to be a bad manager."

Anyone can replicate: group managers by performance and team-survey results, interview the extremes, survey teams twice a year, have the best at each attribute teach everyone else.

## 9. Building a Learning Institution

**The waste problem:** US companies spent $156.2B on training in 2011 (~31 hours per employee per year), and most of it changes nothing — by some estimates 90% of training produces no sustained behavior or performance change. Cause: training is insufficiently targeted, delivered by the wrong people, and measured by hours and spend instead of changed behavior.

**1. Deliberate practice, not course hours (Ericsson):**
- Mastery isn't raw hours; it's *how* they're spent: shard skills into tiny component actions, repeat them relentlessly, observe, get immediate feedback, make small adjustments, repeat (Tiger Woods spending four hours hitting the same shot in the rain — the one shot he couldn't otherwise practice). Practice without feedback and experimentation produces little improvement; "twenty-five years of experience" can be one year repeated twenty-five times.
- Format that works: principles, then role-play, then review the recording, then repeat — labor-intensive but it sticks. Deliver less content that is retained rather than more hours that are forgotten.
- Cheap everyday version: before any important meeting ask "What are your goals? How do you think each person will respond? How will you open the difficult topic?"; afterward, "How did it go? What did you learn? What will you try differently next time?" A few minutes, zero budget, compounds forever, and teaches people to run experiments on themselves.

**2. Build the faculty from within (the "local maximum" principle):**
- The best teacher isn't the world's best expert; it's your best practitioner — the local maximum. They know your specific context, are available for follow-up questions and feedback, and are essentially free. Academics have theoretical knowledge; consultants have shallow secondhand knowledge; practitioners have lived it. Partner with outside experts selectively to co-develop, not wholesale outsource.
- Break the craft into component skills and have the best at each one teach it (best cold-caller teaches cold-calling, best negotiator teaches negotiation).
- Teaching scales geometrically while individual output scales linearly: pulling your best salesperson out of the field 10% of the time costs some of her revenue, but a 10% lift across ten peers more than repays it, permanently. Andy Grove's version: 12 hours preparing four lectures for ten people yields the equivalent of 200 hours of extra output from a 1% performance lift.
- Peer-teaching pattern at scale: employee-taught courses on anything from technical topics to presentation skills (one year: 2,200 classes taught by ~3,000 volunteer employee-teachers to 21,000+ colleagues), plus volunteer advisor/guru networks for confidential 1:1 career, leadership, and sales counsel. Side effects: the teachers gain purpose and the advisors build listening and empathy skills. "The best way to learn is to teach."

**3. Only invest in courses that provably change behavior (Kirkpatrick's four levels, 1959):**
1. **Reaction** — did students like it? Worthless as a quality measure; entertaining ≠ effective, and students can't judge course design while learning.
2. **Learning** — test knowledge/attitude change at the end. Better, but decays if the environment the student returns to is unchanged.
3. **Behavior** — wait, then ask the student's *team* whether behavior actually changed. External validation plus time lag is where measurement gets real.
4. **Results** — did the targeted outcome move (sales, quality, patient outcomes)?
- Shortcut for any organization: split comparable groups, train one, wait, compare outcomes. If the trained group doesn't outperform the control, kill the course. Requires patience (a year of waiting is normal) but is the only way to know.
- The 70/20/10 learning doctrine (70% on-the-job, 20% coaching, 10% classroom) has no empirical support and is unmeasurable in practice — "convenient hand waving that allows HR departments to say people are learning without proving it."

## 10. Pay Unfairly: Power-Law Compensation

**"Fair" pay is not equal pay. Fairness is pay commensurate with contribution — which means wide, sometimes extreme variance within the same job.**

**Why conventional systems push your best people out:** market-data-plus-narrow-band systems (±20% of market, 2-3% average raises, "red-circling" at range top) guarantee that the fastest-growing contributors' pay falls further behind their value every year (Lazear: people are underpaid relative to contribution early in careers, overpaid late). Their rational move is to quit and reprice themselves on the open market. Internal pay systems that can't flex are quit-machines for exactly the people you most want to keep.

**The math (why the variance is justified):**
- Human performance follows a power law, not a Gaussian (O'Boyle & Aguinis, five studies, 633,263 researchers, entertainers, politicians, and athletes). Most performers are *below* the mean because elite outliers pull the average above the median (66% of researchers below average in publications; 71% of NBA players below average in points). Normal distributions massively underpredict extreme outcomes.
- Top 1% of workers generate ~10x average output; top 5% generate more than 4x average.
- Re-running Schmidt's 1979 government-programmer study with power-law assumptions: an 85th-percentile programmer is worth ~$23K/year more than average and a 99.7th-percentile one ~$140K more (1979 dollars; roughly half a million adjusted).
- Exceptions: manual/limited-technology jobs with strict min/max output standards, where distributions really are normal.
- Litmus test: "How many average people would you trade for your single best performer?" More than five: you're probably underpaying them. More than ten: almost certainly.
- In practice this can mean two people in the same role receiving rewards 100x apart in extreme cases ($10K vs. $1M stock awards for the same job family), with 300-500% variance routine at any level and junior stars out-earning senior averages. If the best performer generates 10x the impact, they should get at least 5x the reward; stay in budget by giving smaller rewards to average and poor performers, and be honest with B players about why and what changes it.

**Two capabilities required before paying this way:**
1. A clear read on true individual impact, net of market luck, team effects, company brand, and short- vs. long-term value.
2. Managers who understand the system well enough to explain exactly why an extreme award was given and what anyone else can do to earn one. Without both, wide variance breeds jealousy instead of aspiration.

**Distributive AND procedural justice (Thibaut & Walker):** outcomes must be deserved *and* the process must be visibly clean. The jerk salesman with the huge bonus fails procedurally even when the number is "earned," and rewarding him endorses the behavior. Fairness perceptions drive how valued people feel, job satisfaction, trust in supervisors, and commitment (Dekas). Reward systems that fail either test corrode everything.

**Celebrate accomplishment, not compensation (the failed mega-bonus lesson):** a public program of start-up-sized team cash awards ($5K to $1M+) made almost everyone *less* happy: half the company felt structurally ineligible, near-winners at arbitrary team cutoffs were embittered, winners of "small" awards were crushed by $1M anchoring, and the few $1M winners tried to transfer away from the very work that won it (no second award for the same work). Celebrate publicly with experiences; differentiate money privately through bonus and stock following a power-law-ish distribution.

**Experiential beats cash:** employees *said* they preferred cash (by 15%, expecting it to be 31% more meaningful). In the actual experiment, recipients of equal-value experiences (trips, team dinners, gifts) rated their awards 28% more fun and memorable and 15% more thoughtful; five months later cash recipients' happiness had dropped ~25% while experiential recipients were happier than at award time. Cash is evaluated cognitively and spent on staples; experiences generate emotional, durable memories.

**Make it easy to spread the love:** peer-to-peer recognition at two levels — public thank-you "kudos" (making them one-click and public more than quadrupled usage, +460%) and small peer bonuses (~$175) any employee can grant to anyone with *no* management approval. Abuse is rare and community-policed. Simple public recognition is one of the most underutilized management tools ("men will risk their lives for ribbons").

**Reward thoughtful failure:** if calculated risk-taking is punished on failure, no one takes risks. When a big, smart bet fails, pay the team fairly anyway (less than success would have paid, more than they feared), run a blameless "what did we learn?" postmortem, and move on ("Did we get more than a million dollars in learning out of this? Then get back to work"). Argyris: high-achievers who have rarely failed never learned to learn from failure — they turn defensive exactly when learning matters most. Build the muscle deliberately.

**Supporting case that generosity pays even in thin-margin industries:** Costco vs. Sam's Club — $17/hour average wage vs. $10-12, yet $21,805 operating profit per hourly employee vs. $11,615 (55% higher wages, 88% higher profit) via loyalty, productivity, and the industry's lowest employee theft.

## 11. Frugal Generosity, Perks, and Nudges

**Perks framework — every program should serve at least one of three goals: efficiency, community, innovation.** Most of what makes a workplace feel special is free or near-free; the constraint is imagination and the willingness to say yes, not budget.
- **Free (vendor-comes-to-you) tier:** on-site services employees pay for themselves — dry cleaning drop-off, haircuts, car care, produce delivery, ATMs, mobile libraries. The company contributes permission and maybe a negotiated group discount (viable once you have 50-100 employees as a market). Employees often self-organize these.
- **Negligible-cost tier:** all-hands with open Q&A, talent shows, random-lunch matchups, clubs and employee resource groups, take-your-parents-to-work days, internal tech talks, and outside-speaker series (you don't need celebrities; call a local professor or musician).
- **Deliberate serendipity for innovation:** innovation happens in the structural holes between social groups (Ronald Burt: "people who stand near the holes in a social structure are at higher risk of having good ideas"; creativity is an import-export game — moving a mundane idea to a place where it's valuable). Engineer casual collisions: shared micro-kitchens placed at team borders, café layouts and queue lengths tuned for conversation, open bullpens over cube farms (Bloomberg's newsroom model vs. walled cubicles).
- **Save the big checks for when people most need you:** death benefits (surviving partner receives unvested equity plus 50% of salary for 10 years plus monthly per-child support) cost ~0.1% of payroll — less than shaving one annual raise from 3.0% to 2.9%. Extending paid maternity leave from 3 to 5 months at full pay erased a 2x post-childbirth attrition spike and *net cost nothing* after avoided replacement and training costs. Generosity at life's extremes defines how the institution is perceived every ordinary day. Find ways to say yes.

**Nudges (Thaler & Sunstein): alter choice architecture without forbidding options or changing economic incentives.** There is no neutral default — someone already chose the current environment, and "is" does not equal "ought" (Hume). Be transparent about experiments after the fact; nudge, don't shove. A good nudge is **timely, relevant, and simple to act on.** Proven patterns:
- **Onboarding checklists, both sides.** Manager gets a just-in-time email the night before a new hire starts with five actions: (1) discuss role and responsibilities; (2) match the new hire with a peer buddy; (3) help them build a social network; (4) set monthly onboarding check-ins for six months; (5) encourage open dialogue. Include the supporting data (credibility) and click-ready templates (low cognitive load). New hires whose managers acted became fully effective **25% faster** — a month saved. Mirror-nudge the new hire too (be proactive: ask lots of questions, schedule 1:1s, get to know the team, solicit feedback, take risks): the least naturally proactive gained most (+15 points on proactive behavior), worth ~2% whole-workforce productivity from one email and a 15-minute orientation segment. Double-sided reminders are mistake-proofing (poka-yoke): if the manager misses a step, the new hire picks it up. Context: half of senior hires fail within 18 months, half of hourly workers quit within 120 days, and new grads who didn't understand job expectations left 5x more often — onboarding is high-stakes.
- **Savings nudges.** Wealth at retirement varies ~30x among households with identical lifetime earnings, driven almost entirely by early savings habits — and habits shift only when cued. A simple email showing year-to-date retirement contributions plus an illustrative increase moved 27% of recipients to raise contributions (average savings rate 8.7% to 11.5%, worth ~$262K more each at retirement); the lowest savers increased most (+60% vs. control). Thaler & Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow (pre-commit a slice of *future raises* to savings, opt-out anytime) at ordinary industrial companies: 78% joined, 80% stuck, savings rates 3.5% to 13.6% in 40 months.
- **Environment beats information; choice removal backfires.** Calorie labels, color-coding, and shock signage changed consumption not at all; removing choice (meatless days, small-plates-only) triggered revolt. What worked: keep every option but re-architect defaults — candy into opaque containers, healthy snacks at eye level in glass (calories from candy −30%, fat −40%; one office ate 3.1M fewer calories in seven weeks); offer small plates alongside large with a table card citing the research (32% adoption, −5% total consumption, −18% waste; smaller dishes also *increase* satiety perception, per Wansink's Delboeuf-illusion studies).
- **Social-comparison nudges fix team dysfunction:** a two-question quarterly peer survey ("this person helped me when I reached out" / "this person involved me when I could have been helpful"), anonymously ranked and shared, moved a warring leadership team from 70% to 90% favorable in eight quarters with no other intervention — where performance management and coaching had both failed.

## 12. Guardrails, the Ten Work Rules, and the People Operations Model

**Failure modes of high-freedom cultures (and the responses that preserve the culture):**
- **Leaks:** expect roughly one serious leak a year as the price of default-open. Investigate, fire the leaker every time (deliberate or careless), and tell the whole company what leaked and what the consequence was. Openness survives only if the consequence is certain.
- **Entitlement creep:** people habituate; yesterday's delight becomes today's baseline. Confront abuse publicly (name the behavior, not the person) — once the silent majority sees it, social norms self-correct without firings. Retire benefits when their original rationale disappears (e.g., a hybrid-car subsidy once hybrids went mainstream) and explain why; pre-label new programs as tests so ending them breaks no promise.
- **Change management in a voice culture:** decisions pre-agreed with dozens of leaders can still detonate when thousands see them. When it happens, drop everything ("a crisis is an opportunity to have impact"), personally call the most credible objectors, and be willing to reverse publicly — the answer improves and so does your network. Institutionalize early warning: a standing "canaries" group of respected employees across constituencies who pressure-test people decisions before launch.
- **Value collisions:** emotionally charged debates (the "goji pie" class of controversy) rarely change anyone's mind and resolve nothing — and that's acceptable; brawling, inconclusive debate is *evidence* that voice is real. Never punish someone for sparking a debate; reverse managerial overreactions publicly.
- **Idea sprawl:** untrammeled freedom floods you with projects and nothing gets enough investment to be great. Periodically prune ("more wood behind fewer arrows"), and explain the rationale each time — the more values-driven the culture, the more every decision needs explaining, both individually and in broader context.
- The test of the system is not perfection; it's staying true to values under stress. Work rules for screwing up: admit the mistake, be transparent about it, take counsel from all directions, fix what broke, find the moral and teach it.

**The Ten Work Rules (the whole book in one list):**
1. Give your work meaning.
2. Trust your people.
3. Hire only people who are better than you.
4. Don't confuse development with managing performance.
5. Focus on the two tails.
6. Be frugal and generous.
7. Pay unfairly.
8. Nudge.
9. Manage the rising expectations (announce experiments as experiments; expect setbacks).
10. Enjoy — then go back to No. 1 and start again.

**The People Operations model (how to build the people function that runs all this):**
- **Earn the right to innovate (the hierarchy):** first deliver flawless basics — offers, payroll, hiring on time, fair promotions ("HR that just works"; as ubiquitous and reliable as circulation). Then **mass customization**: one consistent philosophy, different front-end manifestations per population (a multi-committee appeals process for engineers who demand procedural transparency; a fast executive call for sales — same talent standard enforced behind the scenes). Top of the pyramid: **anticipation** ("french fry moments") — deliver what people need before they ask (e.g., $500 of meal-delivery money at childbirth; proactively reaching out to people who just missed a promotion — data showed a salesperson not promoted by 16 quarters is all but guaranteed to quit, and an hour of outreach converts a demotivating event into drive).
- **Data maturity ladder:** description (first agree on shared definitions of basic people data — even "current employee" differs across departments) → analysis/insight (compare closely similar groups to isolate real causes) → prediction (act before the attrition or failure happens). Start small: hire one or two stats-literate PhDs, or a finance/ops analyst, and challenge them to prove your programs work. Pilot every idea on a subgroup — even five or ten people beats nothing.
- **Improve relentlessly:** measure everything done more than once; formal debrief after every cycle ("what did we learn, what were we told to do that we'll consciously not do"); bring services in-house for cost and data advantages. Benchmark achieved: +6% people-function productivity per year, five years running (same services at 73% of the prior per-head cost).
- **The three-thirds staffing model:** no more than 1/3 traditional HR people (pattern recognition, relationships, high EQ); 1/3 top-tier *strategy* consultants (business understanding and problem-solving — screen hard for EQ); 1/3 serious analysts with at least a master's in a quantitative field (they keep everyone honest and raise the evidence bar). Rotate everyone across every role. Hiring only HR people into HR is an error; the blend is what makes evidence-based people management possible.
