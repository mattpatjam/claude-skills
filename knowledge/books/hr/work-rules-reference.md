# Work Rules! — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing people-ops practices, or advising on hiring and culture.*
*Book: "Work Rules!: Insights from Inside Google That Will Transform How You Live and Lead" — Laszlo Bock*

---

## 1. The Founding Premise: High-Freedom Management

Bock's core claim: there are two ways to run an organization. Low-freedom (command and control) is common because it is profitable, takes less effort, and managers fear the alternative. High-freedom management treats employees as owners, not machines, and consistently produces better performance. Research he cites: performance improved only when companies empowered employees (moved decision authority from managers to individuals or teams), provided learning beyond the immediate job, and increased team autonomy and self-organization. Those factors together accounted for a 9% increase in value added per employee. Programs alone (incentive pay, TQM-style initiatives) did not move the needle.

The structural move that makes high freedom real: deliberately strip managers of unilateral power. At Google, a manager cannot single-handedly decide:

- Whom to hire or fire
- How someone's performance is rated
- Salary increases, bonuses, or stock grants
- Promotions
- When code is good enough to ship
- Final product design and launch

Each of these goes to a group of peers, a committee, or an independent team. Rationale: individual managers define "best" differently, so fairness and trust require calibration across the whole organization. What is left for the manager: "Managers serve the team." The default leadership job becomes clearing roadblocks and inspiring people, not punishing and rewarding.

Founders set the ceiling. Culture flows from what founders actually believe about people (Bock contrasts Henry Ford and Milton Hershey's mix of generosity and control with Google's founders insisting from day one on group hiring decisions, broad stock ownership, and transparency). Anyone who leads a team can act as a founder: decide deliberately whether you treat people as owners or as gears, and leave room for others to found things alongside you.

## 2. Culture Eats Strategy: Mission, Transparency, Voice

Three defining components of a high-freedom culture. Perks and fun are outcomes of culture, not the culture itself.

**Mission that matters**
- A mission works when it is a moral goal, not a business goal. "Organize the world's information" mentions no profit, market, customers, or shareholders.
- It should be unattainable. A never-finished mission forces constant innovation; "be the market leader," once achieved, inspires nothing.
- Meaning is the strongest motivator. Adam Grant's call-center study: fund-raisers who met a single scholarship recipient for five minutes raised over 400% more per week. Connect people directly to whoever benefits from their work.
- Wrzesniewski's job/career/calling research: about one third of people in any occupation see their work as a calling, and framing (not the job itself) determines it. Callings correlate with being happier and healthier.

**Default to open (transparency)**
- Share by default; restrict only by exception. A new Google engineer gets access to almost the entire code base on day one. Roadmaps, launch plans, weekly status snippets, and everyone's quarterly OKRs are visible internally.
- Weekly all-hands with unfiltered Q&A to leadership, continued at any scale.
- Anti-backstab norm: complain about someone in an email and expect them to be copied onto the thread. Forces direct conversation.
- Logic: if you say "our people are our greatest asset" and hide information from them, you are lying to them and to yourself. Openness signals trust and gives people the context to contribute in ways top-down management cannot anticipate.

**Voice**
- Give employees a real say in how the company runs. Example mechanism: Bureaucracy Busters, an annual program where employees submit and vote on their biggest frustrations, and the company fixes them (first round: 570 ideas, 55,000+ votes, mostly small fixable irritations).
- Hard decisions get debated broadly (in meetings, all-hands, mass email threads), not just decided at the top. The test of culture is whether principles win when they collide with economics.
- Culture needs guardians: named owners (a chief culture officer, volunteer culture clubs per office with small budgets) whose job is keeping the culture true to itself as the company grows, and channeling "the culture is dying" frustration into repair work.

## 3. The Hiring Bar: Why Hiring Beats Training

Hiring is the single most important people activity in any organization. The math and the biases:

- Two team-building strategies: hire proven top performers (expensive, rare, and last year's stars are not guaranteed to repeat) or hire average and try to train stars. Almost everyone claims the first and actually does the second.
- Most companies spend more per employee on training than on hiring. Bock: a huge training budget is not evidence you invest in people; it is evidence you failed to hire the right people. Redirect resources from training to hiring; it returns more than any training program.
- People are worse at interviewing than they believe (the "Lake Wobegon" effect: everyone thinks their hires are above average). Most assessment happens in the first three to five minutes; the rest of the interview confirms the bias. Nobody checks their interview notes against actual performance later, so nobody improves. Result: typical hiring yields average results.

The two changes that raise the bar:

1. **Hire more slowly.** Only ~10% of applicants at best are top performers, and real top performers are not applying because they are succeeding where they are. Take longer, see more people, and refuse to compromise as the search drags on. The bar: only hire people who are better than you in some meaningful way. ("Interviewing people better than you and saying no" is the phenomenon a healthy bar produces.)
2. **Take hiring decisions away from managers.** Managers compromise standards when searches drag, favor friends and political hires, and gain too much power over their team if they alone decide who joins. Committees decide instead. New managers hate it for about six months, then notice the quality of people around them.

Supporting principles:

- Pedigree matters less than accomplishment. Prefer the top-of-class state-school graduate over the average Ivy grad; for some roles college does not matter at all. Look for resilience and evidence of overcoming hardship.
- "Best" is not one attribute. Do not hire exclusively for smarts; humility and conscientiousness are among the most important attributes. The goal is people who succeed in your context and make everyone around them better.

## 4. The Hiring Machine: Committees, Quality Bar, Self-Correction

The system that keeps the bar from eroding as the organization grows ("the self-replicating talent machine"):

- **Hire by committee, never by one person.** No individual interviewer gets it right every time. Multiple independent assessments beat any single judgment.
- **One objective standard, centrally enforced.** A single final reviewer (or small senior team) is charged with upholding the founders' quality bar across all hires. Without this, each generation of hiring is a slightly poorer copy of the last, and pressure to hire friends, customers' kids, and favors erodes quality. Extreme selectivity is the output (Google hires ~0.25% of applicants, more selective than Harvard admissions).
- **Audit the machine, not just the candidates:**
  - Revisit Program: algorithmically re-score rejected applicants' resumes against the keyword patterns of successful current employees, and have recruiters take a second look at high-scoring rejects. Assume you make errors; build the loop that catches them.
  - Interviewer feedback: every interviewer sees their own historical scores versus actual hire/no-hire outcomes, so they learn whether their judgment is reliable, and packet reviewers know whose scores to weight or ignore.
  - Continuously rebalance speed, error rate, and candidate experience (e.g., testing how many interviews are actually needed per candidate, and cutting the excess).

## 5. Structured Interviewing and the Four Attributes

**What does not work.** Unstructured interviews ("tell me about yourself," "greatest weakness") are mostly worthless: 99%+ of the time is spent confirming a first-ten-seconds impression (confirmation bias). Case interviews and brainteasers ("how many golf balls fit in a 747") are equally worthless; performance on them is a practicable trick that mainly makes the interviewer feel clever.

**What the science says** (Schmidt & Hunter's 85-year meta-analysis of 19 assessment techniques, variance in performance explained):

- Work sample tests: 29% (best single predictor; have candidates do a piece of the actual job)
- General cognitive ability tests: 26% (real tests with right answers, not brainteasers; predictive because they capture capacity to learn)
- Structured interviews: 26% (consistent questions, clear scoring criteria)
- Unstructured interviews: ~14%
- Combinations beat any single method (e.g., cognitive ability + conscientiousness assessment: 36%)

**The working method:**

- Combine behavioral structured questions ("tell me about a time...") and situational structured questions ("what would you do if...") with assessments of cognitive ability, conscientiousness, and leadership. Give technical candidates real work samples (write code, solve engineering problems live).
- Use pre-validated question banks so interviewers default to reliable questions instead of inventing their own (Google's internal tool, qDroid, emails interviewers a tailored guide; public equivalents exist, e.g., the US VA's structured-question library). Bland questions are fine: the answers differentiate, and consistency is what makes candidates comparable.
- Score every interview against a written rubric with clearly defined performance levels per component, and require interviewers to record the evidence (question asked, answer given) so later reviewers can judge independently.
- Require at least two independent interviewers to assess each attribute.
- Design for candidate experience: they are making a bigger decision than you are. Leave time for their questions, give feedback loops (Google surveys every interviewee and adjusts the process). A healthy target: even rejected candidates would recommend a friend apply (Google reached 80%).

**The four hiring attributes** (what to actually assess, in this priority):

1. **General cognitive ability.** How the person has solved hard problems in real life and how they learn. Not GPA or test scores.
2. **Emergent leadership.** Leadership that ignores formal titles: stepping in when a problem needs you, and critically, stepping back and letting go when someone else is better placed. Not club-president resume entries.
3. **"Googleyness" (culture/values fit).** Enjoying fun, intellectual humility (able to admit being wrong and update on data), conscientiousness, comfort with ambiguity, evidence of having taken courageous or interesting paths.
4. **Role-related knowledge.** Deliberately last. Prefer expert-level general understanding over narrow specialism; experts default to the answer they already know, generalists more often produce novel solutions.

## 6. Give Away Power: Managing Without Sticks and Carrots

The freedom chapters extend the founding premise into daily operations:

- Eliminate status symbols and visible hierarchy: minimal management layers, no executive perks that separate leaders from everyone else, decisions justified by data rather than rank ("don't politick, use data").
- Let employees shape their own work: 20%-style discretionary time on projects the employee chooses signals real trust and has produced disproportionate innovation; even where formal programs fade, the license to self-direct matters more than the accounting of hours.
- Ask employees how to run the company, then act on it: annual surveys with published results and manager-level accountability, idea programs with voting, all-hands Q&A. Voice mechanisms only build trust if visible changes follow.
- Trust is the cheap, scalable substitute for control: if you believe people are fundamentally good, act like it (share information, delegate authority, let teams self-organize). If you cannot bring yourself to do this, the honest conclusion is that you either hired wrong or do not believe your own rhetoric.
- Safety to speak up is a precondition for all of it: people cannot use voice or freedom if disagreement with a manager is career-dangerous, which is why power over pay, promotion, and rating is removed from the individual manager in the first place.

## 7. Performance Management: Separate Ratings from Development

The core insight: performance management fails everywhere because it mixes two conversations that must never happen at once. When a rating and pay are on the table, people stop listening to feedback and start defending themselves.

- **Split the conversations.** Development conversations happen continuously and are safe: about growth, gaps, and how to improve. Evaluation happens on its own cycle: about outcomes against goals. Never deliver both in the same meeting.
- **Set goals the OKR way.** Objectives and Key Results: ambitious objectives with measurable key results, set quarterly, visible to everyone in the company (including the CEO's). Target ~70% attainment; hitting 100% means the goals were too easy. OKRs are deliberately not mechanically tied to pay, which keeps them ambitious.
- **Calibrate ratings in groups.** Managers propose ratings; groups of managers meet and calibrate across teams so a rating means the same thing everywhere. This removes individual manager bias and pressure, and is what makes ratings trusted enough to base rewards on.
- **Use peer feedback.** Peers see what managers do not. Gather it for both development and evaluation, and keep the forms short and focused on what the person should do more of and do differently.
- **Simplify.** Fewer rating points and less process than the annual-review theater most companies run. The measure of a performance system is whether employees consider it fair and whether it changes behavior, not its sophistication.

## 8. The Two Tails: Compassion for the Bottom, Study the Top

Performance in any organization follows a distribution with two informative tails. Most companies obsess over the middle; the leverage is at the ends.

**Bottom tail**
- Identify the bottom ~5% and tell them honestly: not as a firing step, but as "you are in this group, and we want to help you improve."
- Diagnosis first: most bottom-tail performance is a role mismatch or a skill gap, not a character flaw. Offer training and, often more effective, a different role.
- Result pattern: a meaningful share improve, others choose to leave on their own terms, and either outcome beats fear-driven stack-ranking cultures. Compassionate handling of the bottom tail is also watched closely by everyone else; it sets the tone of the whole system.

**Top tail**
- Put your best people under a microscope. Top performers are your best internal benchmark: study exactly what they do differently, then convert it into checklists and training for everyone else.
- Project Oxygen is the template: Google tested the hypothesis that managers do not matter and proved the opposite with its own data (teams with the best managers had higher retention, satisfaction, and performance). Then it distilled what the best managers do into eight behaviors, ordered with technical skill last:
  1. Be a good coach
  2. Empower the team; do not micromanage
  3. Express interest in team members' success and well-being
  4. Be productive and results-oriented
  5. Be a good communicator; listen and share information
  6. Support career development
  7. Have a clear vision and strategy for the team
  8. Have key technical skills
- Close the loop with upward feedback: employees rate their managers on these behaviors regularly; managers see their scores; the worst improve the most. Measurement plus visibility, not punishment, drives the change.

## 9. Pay Unfairly: Power-Law Compensation

The most counterintuitive rule: fairness in pay does not mean sameness. Human performance is not normally distributed; it follows a power law. Your best person is not 10% better than average, they can be 10x more valuable. Compensation systems built on normal-distribution assumptions (tight salary bands, 3% vs 5% raises) systematically underpay your best people, and they leave to capture their value elsewhere.

- **Pay top performers dramatically more.** Two people in the same role can legitimately earn several-fold different amounts. "Unfair" pay is fair when it tracks actual impact. The real injustice is paying your best and your average people nearly the same.
- **Internal consequence to manage:** exceptional pay must map to exceptional, well-evidenced performance (this is why calibrated ratings must come first). If people cannot see the connection, differentiated pay corrodes trust instead of building retention.
- **Celebrate accomplishment, not compensation.** Public recognition celebrates the work; money stays private. Getting this backwards (public money, private praise) breeds envy and gaming.
- **Make rewards experiential where possible.** Experiences (trips, dinners, team events) generate more happiness and memory per dollar than cash, which is quickly absorbed into baseline expectations.
- **Let peers reward each other.** Small peer-to-peer bonuses and thank-you awards that any employee can give without manager approval spread recognition to contributions management never sees.
- **Reward thoughtful failure.** If a well-conceived, well-executed bet fails for reasons outside the team's control, pay and celebrate the team anyway. Punishing smart risk teaches your best people to stop taking risks.

## 10. Nudges, Frugal Generosity, and the Ten Work Rules

**Benefits philosophy: be frugal and generous.** Most of the famous perks cost little or nothing; they work because they optimize for one of three things: efficiency (services that give employees time back: on-site conveniences, transport, food), community (cafes, events, and layouts that engineer chance encounters, which drive both belonging and innovation), or innovation (talks, tools, access). Save money on things that do not matter to people; spend where tragedy or need strikes (e.g., generous death benefits for survivors of deceased employees). Generosity at the moments that matter buys more loyalty than expensive perks year-round.

**Nudge: design the environment, don't mandate behavior.** Small, cheap changes to defaults and choice architecture shift behavior at scale while preserving freedom of choice:

- Onboarding checklists: a short, timed nudge to managers (discuss role and responsibilities, match the new hire with a peer buddy, help build a social network, set up monthly check-ins for six months, encourage open dialogue) measurably accelerated new hires to full productivity. A parallel just-in-time checklist to the new hires themselves (ask questions, ask for feedback, update your manager) compounds the effect.
- Health by default: smaller plates in cafeterias, healthy items at eye level, candy in opaque jars and water in clear coolers. Nobody is forbidden anything; consumption still drops.
- Savings by default: prompts and defaults that raise retirement-contribution rates capture free money employees were leaving on the table.
- The general method: identify the behavior you want, change the default or the friction, measure, keep what works. Nudges must be transparent and in the employee's interest, or they become manipulation.

**When it goes wrong.** High-freedom systems fail visibly: confidential things leak, some people exploit generosity, entitlement creeps ("the perks are the point"), and well-intended programs backfire. The response framework: acknowledge the mistake openly, fix the system rather than revoking freedom for everyone, and accept these costs as the price of trust. Occasional abuse by a few is cheaper than distrust of everyone.

**The ten work rules (the whole book in checklist form):**

1. Give your work meaning
2. Trust your people
3. Hire only people who are better than you
4. Don't confuse development with managing performance
5. Focus on the two tails
6. Be frugal and generous
7. Pay unfairly
8. Nudge
9. Manage the rising expectations
10. Enjoy! And then go back to No. 1 and start again

(Rule 9 matters for adopters: announce experiments as experiments. Freedoms once given are treated as entitlements, so set expectations before you iterate or roll anything back.)
