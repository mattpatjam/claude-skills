# The Sales Acceleration Formula — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing hiring processes, or advising on team-building.*
*Book: "The Sales Acceleration Formula: Using Data, Technology, and Inbound Selling to go from $0 to $100 Million" — Mark Roberge*

*Status: IN PROGRESS. Built from Matthieu's reading notes, section by section, verified against the book as we go. Part One: Sales Hiring Formula captured, plus Finding Top-Performing Salespeople. Parts Two onward pending.*

## Context on the Author's Claim

Roberge was HubSpot's fourth employee and first sales hire, and built their sales org from zero to roughly $100M in revenue and 450 salespeople. His background is engineering (MIT), not sales, and the book's central argument follows from that: sales success is not an art form dependent on charisma, it is a process that can be engineered, measured, and made predictable.

The book is organised in four parts. Part One is the Sales Hiring Formula.

## PART ONE: THE SALES HIRING FORMULA

### 1. Core Premise

**Sales success comes from alignment, not from generic sales talent.** Specifically: when the unique strengths of a salesperson align with the company's sales context.

Two consequences follow:

- **There is no universal "great salesperson."** A rep who dominates in one company's context can fail in another's. Enterprise versus SMB, long cycle versus short, inbound versus outbound, technical product versus simple product: each context rewards a different profile.
- **Therefore the ideal hiring formula is different for every company.** You cannot copy another company's hiring criteria and expect it to predict success in yours.

What IS universal is the process used to engineer the formula. That process is the framework below.

### 2. The Four-Step Process to Engineer the Formula

#### Step 1: Establish a theory of the ideal sales characteristics

Write down which characteristics you believe predict success in YOUR sales context. This is a hypothesis, stated explicitly, before you start interviewing. The point of writing it down is that a hypothesis can be tested and corrected, while an instinct cannot.

Candidate characteristics to draw from:

| Characteristic | |
|---|---|
| Coachability | Preparation |
| Curiosity | Adaptability to change |
| Work ethic | Competitiveness |
| Intelligence | Brevity |
| Prior success | Rapport building |
| Passion | Technical aptitude |
| Objection handling | Convincing |
| Closing ability | |

This is a menu, not a checklist. The work is selecting and ranking the few that matter for your context, not scoring all fifteen.

#### Step 2: Define an evaluation strategy for each characteristic

For every characteristic you selected, decide in advance how you will actually measure it in a candidate. A trait you cannot assess reliably is not a usable criterion.

Assessment methods available:

- **Behavioral questions** (past behaviour as evidence of the trait)
- **Role play** (live demonstration under pressure)
- **Pre-interview exercise** (work product submitted before you meet them)
- **Reference checks** (third-party corroboration)

The mapping matters. Some traits are best caught by one method and invisible to another. Coachability, for example, shows up in role play (give feedback mid-exercise, see whether they apply it immediately) far better than in any interview question.

#### Step 3: Score candidates against the ideal characteristics

Rate each selected characteristic from **0 to 10**, weighted by how strongly that characteristic aligns with your company's sales context.

The scoring exists to force comparison across candidates on consistent criteria instead of relying on overall impression, and to create a record you can later check against actual performance.

#### Step 4: Learn and iterate on the model

The formula is not fixed. As the company evolves and hiring experience accumulates, revisit the theory. Diagnostic questions:

- Which characteristics do the top performers actually have in common?
- Which characteristics turned out not to matter?
- What are you missing entirely?

This step is what separates the method from a static scorecard: the criteria are treated as a model to be corrected by outcome data, not as received wisdom.

### 3. Finding on Buyer Behaviour

Statistics indicate that salespeople who are **intelligent and helpful** outperform those who are **aggressive and high-pressure** with the modern empowered buyer.

The buyer now has access to product information, pricing comparisons, and peer reviews independently of the salesperson. The traditional information asymmetry that made high-pressure tactics viable has collapsed. The salesperson's value has shifted from gatekeeping information to helping the buyer interpret it.

This has direct hiring implications: the profile that reads as "a natural salesman" in the old sense is, on the evidence, the wrong profile to select for.

### 4. HubSpot's Five Traits (One Company's Answer)

The five characteristics that correlated most significantly with sales success **at HubSpot specifically**. Roberge's own framing is that these are the output of running the four-step process in one particular context, not a template to copy.

| Trait | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Coachability** | The ability to absorb and apply coaching. |
| **Curiosity** | The ability to understand a prospect's context through effective questioning and listening. Naturally curious. Asks great questions, listens intently, probes points of interest. Seeks to understand customer goals, aspirations, fears, and struggles, all through tactical questioning. |
| **Prior success** | A history of top performance or remarkable achievement. |
| **Intelligence** | The ability to learn complex concepts quickly and communicate them in an easy-to-understand manner. |
| **Work ethic** | Proactively pursuing the company mission with a high degree of energy and deep daily activity. |

Note the absence of what conventional sales hiring screens for: no closing ability, no objection handling, no competitiveness, no industry experience.

## FINDING TOP-PERFORMING SALESPEOPLE

### 5. Core Premise: Recruiting Must Be Passive

**Great salespeople never need to apply for a job.** They are employed, performing, and being paid well for it. They are not reading job boards.

Everything follows from this. If your pipeline consists of people who applied, you are by definition selecting from the population that needed to apply. A passive recruiting strategy, going out and finding people who are not looking, is therefore not an optional enhancement to inbound applications. It is the only way to reach the target population.

### 6. Who Does the Recruiting

Roberge's position, stated as three rules:

1. **Do not hire a recruiting agency.**
2. **Do not build a corporate recruiting team.**
3. **Build a recruiting agency within your corporation.**

The distinction between the second and third is the point. A corporate recruiting function is an HR service centre that processes requisitions. A recruiting agency inside the company does what an external agency does (proactive sourcing, outreach, pipeline building, candidate relationship management) but with the company's own context, incentives, and knowledge of what good looks like, and without the agency's incentive to close any placement rather than the right one.

### 7. The Four-Step Sourcing Process

#### Step 1: Source a candidate list via LinkedIn search

Use LinkedIn's search capability to build a list of qualified candidates. Screening criteria to search on:

- **Location**
- **Job title**
- **Schools** (used as a proxy for intelligence)
- **Company**

#### Step 2: Screen the results using profile detail

Indicators of sales excellence visible on a LinkedIn profile:

| Indicator | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Rankings within their team** | Direct evidence of prior success. A rep who states "ranked 2 of 47" is giving you the trait you most want to measure. |
| **Longevity at current or former employers** | Reps who leave constantly are usually leaving for a reason. Tenure suggests both performance and stability. |
| **Alignment between their current buyer context and yours** | The core premise of the hiring formula applied to screening. A rep succeeding in a context like yours is far more likely to succeed in yours than an equally talented rep from an unlike context. |
| **Where they went to school** | Intelligence proxy. |
| **Quality of their LinkedIn profile** | Treated as a work sample. A salesperson's own profile is a piece of positioning and writing, done for themselves. |

#### Step 3: Engage the pre-screened candidates

Mechanics that matter:

- **Prefer a second-degree connection.** Warm beats cold, the same as in sales.
- **Use their current company or their school as the email subject line.** It is the thing most likely to be recognised and opened.
- **Keep the email appropriately brief.**
- **Never ask them directly.** Do not open by asking whether they want a job.

Instead, ask for a referral. The pattern:

> "Our current team can't keep up with the inbound lead flow, so we are expanding the team. Your background is similar to those of our current top performers. Are there any folks in your network who are in the job market and have a background similar to yours?"

Three things this does at once: it pays the recipient a specific compliment (your background matches our top performers), it removes any threat or pressure by not asking anything of them personally, and it opens the network behind the candidate rather than just the candidate.

- **Always ask your own team for referrals too.** Who do they think would fit the team?
- **Understand the sales talent pool in your area.** Treated as a standing requirement, not a one-off exercise. You cannot source passively from a pool you have not mapped.

#### Step 4: The ideal first sales hire

When facing the first sales hire decision, many founders weight **senior leadership experience** and **industry domain knowledge** most heavily. This is a trap.

**The most critical value from the first sales hire is not the first customers or the revenue she generates. It is her ability to accelerate the company toward product-market fit.**

The first sales hire is in the only seat that hears unfiltered objections, confusions, and desires from the market at volume. That feedback is worth more at that stage than the bookings, and it calls for a different profile than seniority and a rolodex.

## Cross-Book Connections

### Tension with *Who* (Smart & Street), on the HR shelf

Both books demand a written, scored standard before interviewing, and both reject gut-feel hiring. They disagree on what the standard is made of.

- ***Who* is outcome-first.** Its scorecard starts with the mission and the specific, measurable outcomes the role must deliver, then adds competencies as the means to those outcomes.
- **Roberge is trait-first.** He starts from characteristics that statistically predict performance, and treats outcomes as the data you later regress the traits against.

Not actually contradictory. *Who* answers "what must this person accomplish," Roberge answers "which attributes predict they will." Using both means writing a *Who*-style scorecard for the role's outcomes and a Roberge-style weighted trait sheet for evaluating candidates against it.

### Convergence with *Predictable Revenue* (Ross & Tyler), on the Negotiation & Closing shelf

Both treat the sales org as an engineered system rather than a collection of talented individuals, and both insist the profile you hire for depends on the specific job. *Predictable Revenue*'s role specialisation argument (prospectors and closers are different jobs) implies Roberge's conclusion directly: different sales context, different ideal characteristics, therefore different hiring formula per role.

## Open Verification Items

Items to check against the book text. Flagged where Matthieu's notes may compress a distinction the book draws.

1. **"Prior success" versus prior sales experience.** Roberge's HubSpot analysis found that relevant industry experience and years of sales experience were *not* predictive, while a track record of top performance in any domain was. If correct, the trait is "has been the best at something" and not "has sold before," which is a much larger claim and changes who you shortlist. Confirm the book draws this distinction.
2. **Scoring versus weighting.** The notes describe rating 0 to 10 "depending on which characteristics align with your sales context," which blends two separate operations. Check whether the book's method is a raw 0 to 10 score per trait multiplied by a fixed per-trait weight, giving a single comparable total. The distinction matters in practice: without separate weights, a candidate can win on traits that do not matter.
3. **Whether aggressiveness was negatively correlated.** The buyer-behaviour finding implies it. Confirm whether Roberge reports aggressiveness as merely non-predictive or as an active negative signal.
4. **The rest of Part One.** Sourcing and recruiting mechanics now captured above. Still missing and expected: running the candidate funnel with conversion metrics (treating recruiting as a funnel with measured stage conversion, the way the book treats the sales funnel). Confirm whether that is in the book and capture it if so.
5. **"Build a recruiting agency within your corporation" in practice.** Roberge hired a dedicated recruiter into the sales org early. Confirm the mechanics: who the recruiter reported to, and in particular how they were compensated. The claim worth verifying is that the recruiter was paid on hire quality and retention rather than on placement volume, since that incentive is the whole argument for in-housing it.
6. **Why "never ask directly" works.** Confirm whether Roberge states the second-order effect: that a meaningful share of recipients respond by putting themselves forward, because the referral framing removes the threat of being seen as a job seeker. The tactic reads as a referral play but may be designed primarily as a response-rate play.
7. **Chapter boundary around the first sales hire.** Step 4 in the notes (ideal first sales hire) is a different subject from sourcing top performers. Confirm whether it is its own chapter rather than the fourth step of the sourcing process, so this reference does not misrepresent the book's structure.
8. **The schools criterion.** It appears twice, as a search filter and as a screening indicator, both as a proxy for intelligence. Check how strongly Roberge actually endorses it versus reporting it as something HubSpot used. It is the weakest and most context-dependent of the five indicators, and the one least likely to transfer outside a US market with recognisable tier rankings.
