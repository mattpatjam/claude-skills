# Who — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing hiring processes, or advising on team-building.*
*Book: "Who: The A Method for Hiring" — Geoff Smart & Randy Street*

---

## 1. Core Premise: Who, Not What

The most important decisions in business are not what decisions (strategy, product, process) but who decisions (the people you put in place to make the what decisions). Most managers obsess over what problems while their real bottleneck is who.

- An A Player is defined mathematically: a candidate with at least a 90% chance of achieving a set of outcomes that only the top 10% of possible candidates could achieve. Both halves matter: high probability of success AND a high bar for the outcomes themselves.
- Average hiring success rate for managers is roughly 50% (Drucker's estimate, confirmed by the authors' research).
- The average hiring mistake costs about 15x the employee's base salary in hard costs and lost productivity. A bad $100K hire can cost $1.5M or more.
- Hiring failures cluster at four points: (1) being unclear about what the job requires, (2) a weak flow of candidates, (3) inability to reliably pick the right person from similar-looking candidates, (4) losing wanted candidates at the offer stage.
- The A Method addresses each failure point with one step: Scorecard, Source, Select, Sell.
- "You are who you hire." Hire C Players and you lose. Hire B Players and you plateau. The difference between an A and a B leader produces an order-of-magnitude difference in returns (Blackstone's finding across 20 years of deals).

## 2. The Ten Voodoo Hiring Methods (Failure Patterns)

Common interview approaches that feel productive but are statistically near-random predictors of performance. If mis-hires keep appearing, one of these is usually in use:

1. **The Art Critic** — gut-feel assessment in minutes. Candidates who want the job can fake a short interview; gut instinct is highly inaccurate for hiring.
2. **The Sponge** — everyone interviews the candidate with no coordination, all asking the same superficial questions. Verdict never goes deeper than "seems like a good guy."
3. **The Prosecutor** — trick questions and logic puzzles (why are manhole covers round). Tests knowledge or cleverness, not ability to do the job.
4. **The Suitor** — spends the interview selling the role instead of assessing the person. Lands candidates, but blind on fit.
5. **The Trickster** — gimmick tests (drop a paper wad to see if they pick it up). Irrelevant behaviors, no predictive value.
6. **The Animal Lover** — pet questions ("what animal would you be?"). No relevance, no scientific basis.
7. **The Chatterbox** — pleasant small talk, the "la-di-da" interview. Enjoyable, decision-useless.
8. **The Psychological Tester** — personality bubble tests for selection decisions. Recommended against by industrial psychology; savvy candidates fake answers.
9. **The Aptitude Tester** — aptitude tests used as the sole determinant. Fine as a screening supplement, never in isolation.
10. **The Fortune-Teller** — hypothetical questions ("what would you do if..."). Candidates give idealized answers. Judge the walk, not the talk.

Shared root error: assuming people are easy to assess. The truth: it is hard to see people for who they really are, so you need structured fact-gathering over decades of track record, not impressions from an hour.

## 3. Scorecard: The Blueprint for the Role

The first failure point of hiring is vagueness about what the hire must accomplish. The scorecard is a one-page document that defines A performance for a specific role before sourcing begins. It is not a job description (job descriptions list activities; scorecards define outcomes). Three parts:

**Mission** — one to five plain-language sentences on why the role exists, its core essence. No corporate gobbledygook. Test: candidates, recruiters, and teammates understand what you want without clarifying questions.
- Missions force specialist hiring. Avoid the "all-around athlete" trap: impressive generalists rarely work because real jobs need narrow, deep competence matched to the specific business problem at that specific time (Intel's sequence of Noyce the promoter, Moore the technologist, Grove the driver).
- Scorecards are situational and evolving documents, not templates dusted off per vacancy.

**Outcomes** — three to eight specific, objective, ranked outcomes the person must accomplish. "Grow revenue from $25M to $50M by end of year three," not "responsible for sales."
- Where outcomes resist quantification, make them observable ("launch new campaign within 180 days").
- High, realistic outcomes self-select: they scare off B/C Players and attract A Players who want big challenges.
- Specificity liberates the hire: they know exactly what they will be judged on.

**Competencies** — how the person is expected to operate to achieve the outcomes. Two layers:
- Role competencies, tailored per job. Research-backed critical competencies for A Players: efficiency, honesty/integrity, organization and planning, aggressiveness, follow-through on commitments, intelligence, analytical skills, attention to detail, persistence, proactivity. For managers add: ability to hire A Players, ability to develop people.
- Cultural competencies, the same five to eight on every scorecard in the company. One in three CEOs interviewed named failure to evaluate cultural fit as a top cause of mis-hires. Derive them by asking the leadership team "what adjectives describe our culture?" and writing down even the obvious ones. Have the discipline to pass on talented people who don't fit (and to remove productive people who poison the culture).
- Don't over-narrow the competency list: different people achieve the same outcome by different routes.

**Creation process:** (1) write the mission, (2) define 3-8 outcomes, (3) list role competencies plus the standard cultural competencies, (4) pressure-test against the business plan and the scorecards of adjacent roles for alignment, then share with everyone involved in the hire.

**Scorecard as management system:** scorecards cascade the strategy. CEO outcomes translate into direct reports' scorecards and on down, so every role's outcomes support the plan. Beyond hiring, scorecards set expectations for new hires, monitor progress, objectify annual reviews, and drive talent reviews. Only ~10% of CEOs have written objectives for their direct reports; scorecards fix that.

## 4. Source: Generate a Flow of A Players Before You Need Them

Sourcing is continuous, not an event triggered by a vacancy. The passive approach (vacancy, panic, HR posting, three stale months) draws from overfished talent pools. Ads generate resume volume, not candidate quality. The best executives treat themselves as chief recruiting officers and are always building the pipeline. Methods, in order of effectiveness:

- **Referrals from personal and professional networks** — the #1 method, cited by 77% of interviewed leaders, yet least practiced by average managers. The one question: "Who are the most talented people you know that I should hire?" Ask it of your top ten contacts, of customers (best salespeople who call on them), suppliers, partners, and everyone you meet socially. Capture every name, call a few each week, nurture relationships for years before you need them.
- **Referrals from employees** — often the best-targeted source; employees know the needs and culture. Make sourcing an outcome on every employee's scorecard ("source 5 A Players per year who pass our phone screen") and reward it with bonuses or perks. Companies doing this report 60-85% of hires coming via employee referral, at higher success rates. Side benefit: everyone starts seeing the business through a who lens.
- **Deputizing friends of the firm** — give trusted outsiders (advisors, industry contacts, advisory boards) an incentive (bounty, gift, fee-free fund access, small equity) to feed you names. Keep the incentive strong enough and the reporting regular.
- **External recruiters** — useful for executive talent, but only as much as you educate them. Treat them like partners, share the scorecard, let them deeply understand your culture. Hire recruiters using the A Method itself.
- **Recruiting researchers** — contract researchers who map a market and feed names (no interviewing). Cheap pipeline, variable cost, but the vetting burden stays with you. Orient them to your culture or they optimize quantity over quality.

**Sourcing system:** two mandatory components. (1) A capture mechanism for every name and contact (index cards, spreadsheet, or applicant tracking system, the tool matters less than the discipline). (2) Thirty minutes on the calendar every single week to call and nurture prioritized talent until you get at least one live conversation. Opening script: "Sue recommended we connect. I'm always on the lookout for talented people and would love to hear about your career interests, even if you're perfectly content where you are." End every call with the referral question again. Done consistently, this compounds into an exponentially growing network (~40+ new conversations per year).

## 5. Select Step 1: The Screening Interview

Selection replaces impressions with facts via four structured interviews in sequence: screening interview, Who Interview, focused interview, reference interview. Together they build a fact base spanning the candidate's whole career, which you rate against the scorecard.

The screening interview culls B/C Players fast. Phone only, 30 minutes max, same questions every time:

1. **"What are your career goals?"** — Ask before telling them anything about the role, so they can't parrot you back. A Players have clear goals with passion that aligns with the role. No goals, or goals misaligned with the job, screen out.
2. **"What are you really good at professionally?"** — Push for 8-12 strengths with concrete examples. Screen out if strengths don't match the scorecard.
3. **"What are you not good at or not interested in doing professionally?"** — Reject cookie-cutter answers ("I work too hard"): "That sounds like a strength. What are you really not good at?" If still stuck, deploy the threat of reference check: "If you advance, we'll ask you to set up reference calls with bosses, peers, and subordinates, okay? So, what do you think they'll say you're not good at?" Aim for 5-8 genuine weaknesses or areas of disinterest. Screen out on scorecard deal-killers.
4. **"Who were your last five bosses, and how will they each rate your performance on a 1-10 scale when we talk to them?"** — "When we talk to them," never "if." Press for the reasoning behind each rating. Calibration: 8-10 good, 7 neutral, 6 and below is bad (a self-rated 6 usually means a real 2).

Mechanics:
- Open by framing: "First twenty minutes I get to know you, then I'll answer your questions. Sound good?"
- Follow up every answer with the **"What? How? Tell me more"** framework (getting curious): short open follow-ups ("What do you mean? What happened? How did that go? What were the results? Tell me more.") until the real story is clear. Never questions longer than about six words.
- **Hit the gong fast.** Collapse the call to 15-20 minutes if it isn't working. Never pass a borderline candidate forward "so colleagues can take a look"; better to miss a possible A Player than burn hours on a probable B/C. Rigorous screens typically pass only 10-20% of callers.
- Advance only 2-5 candidates you're genuinely excited about. Gut feel is legitimately useful in one direction only: deciding who NOT to hire (integrity red flags you observe directly), never as the reason to hire.

## 6. Select Step 2: The Who Interview (the Core Instrument)

A chronological walk-through of the candidate's career, the single most valid and reliable predictor of performance per decades of industrial psychology research (rooted in Brad Smart's Topgrading interview). Power comes from patterns: data across 15+ years of jobs reveals strengths, weaknesses, and trajectories that no single-day impression can. Feels like a conversation (people telling their own story), but every answer is a decision data point.

Structure: start with highs and lows of their educational years, then break the career into chapters (each job, or clusters spanning 3-5 years) from earliest to most recent. For each chapter, five questions:

1. **"What were you hired to do?"** — Reconstruct what their scorecard would have been: mission, outcomes, how success was measured.
2. **"What accomplishments are you most proud of?"** — A Players describe outcomes linked to the job's expectations. B/C Players talk vaguely about events, people, and what they liked, never results. Watch for accomplishments uncorrelated to what the job required.
3. **"What were some low points during that job?"** — Everyone has lows. Reframe relentlessly until they surface ("What went wrong? Biggest mistake? What would you do differently? Where were peers stronger than you?").
4. **"Who were the people you worked with?"** — This is **TORC (Threat of Reference Check)**. Follow the exact sequence: get the boss's name, make them spell it while visibly writing it down (signals you will call), ask what it was like working for that boss, then ask "What WILL he/she say were your biggest strengths and areas for improvement when we talk?" Will, not would. Reciprocity kicks in: having just described the boss, the candidate owes an honest answer about themselves. Keep reframing past "I don't know." For managers, add: "How would you rate the team you inherited? What changes did you make?" and "What will your team say about you as a manager?"
5. **"Why did you leave that job?"** — The push-versus-pull diagnostic. A Players are pulled to bigger things and their bosses are disappointed to lose them. B/C Players are pushed out (managed out, fired, marginalized). Never accept vague answers ("my boss and I didn't connect"); get curious until you know what actually happened.

Throughout: suspend judgment during the interview, stay curious, keep them talking. The truth (including spectacular disqualifiers) reliably surfaces under this structure.

## 7. Select Steps 3-4: Focused Interviews and Reference Interviews

**Focused interviews** — after the Who Interview, assign teammates to interview the finalist on specific slices of the scorecard, so multiple interviewers add data instead of duplicating each other (the fix for Sponge hiring). Each focused interview targets one or a few outcomes or competencies (often one on role outcomes, one on cultural fit). Pattern: "The purpose of this interview is to talk about [outcome/competency]. What are your biggest accomplishments in this area? What are your biggest mistakes and lessons learned in this area?" Same discipline as the Who Interview: chronological facts, getting curious, no hypotheticals.

**Reference interviews** — do not skip these and do not treat them as a formality; they verify the picture built in the Who Interview.
- Pick the references yourself from the names surfaced via TORC (the bosses, peers, and subordinates the candidate discussed), don't rely only on the candidate's hand-picked list.
- Have the candidate personally set up the calls. A Players' references call back enthusiastically; silence is data.
- Do about seven reference calls (e.g. three past bosses, two peers or customers, two subordinates).
- Core questions: "In what context did you work with the person? What were their biggest strengths? What were their biggest areas for improvement back then? How would you rate their overall performance in that job on a 1-10 scale? What about their performance makes you give that rating? The person mentioned they struggled with [X] in that job, can you tell me more about that?"
- Decode the code. References rarely volunteer negatives; lukewarm praise ("she was fine," "he tried hard") is a negative. Only genuine, energetic enthusiasm counts as a positive. "Areas for improvement back then" softens the question enough to get real answers.

## 8. Deciding: Skill-Will Match and Red Flags

**The decision rule:** rate the candidate against the scorecard, not against other candidates or a general impression.
- **Skill** = can they do it (evidence of achieving the scorecard's outcomes, from track record).
- **Will** = do they want to do it (motivation, goals, and momentum aligned with the mission and culture).
- A Players sit in the skill-will bullseye: both match the scorecard. Rate each outcome and competency A/B/C from the interview data. Hire only when the data says at least 90% likelihood of A performance. If nobody clears the bar, keep sourcing; do not settle, and do not hire a talented person for the wrong role.

**Red flags** (from any stage of the process) that predict failure:
- Candidate does not mention past failures, or exaggerates answers.
- Takes credit for others' work.
- Speaks poorly about past bosses (you will earn the next colorful nickname).
- Cannot explain job moves cleanly; a pattern of being pushed out rather than pulled up.
- People they have worked with are unwilling to serve as references.
- More interested in compensation, title, or the next job than the job at hand.
- Ego unchecked, poor listening, blames others, misses agreed process steps (late, unprepared, sloppy follow-up: how they behave as a candidate is their best behavior).
- Direct observation of dishonesty in small things (the gut is valid for who NOT to hire).

## 9. Sell: Landing the Candidate You Chose

Selection without selling loses the exact people you most want. Selling runs on empathy: identify what the candidate actually cares about and address it, don't pitch generically. The five things candidates care about, the **Five F's**:

1. **Fit** — connect the company's vision, needs, and culture to the candidate's goals, strengths, and values. "Here is why you, specifically, belong here."
2. **Family** — the decision is never the candidate's alone. Involve and win over the spouse/family: relocation support, meet them, address their concerns directly.
3. **Freedom** — autonomy to make decisions. "I will give you the freedom to do the job; I don't micromanage A Players."
4. **Fortune** — compensation and financial upside tied to the value they'll create.
5. **Fun** — the work environment, the team, and the relationships they'll enjoy.

Sell through all **five waves**, not just at offer time (deals die in the gaps):
1. When you source.
2. Throughout the interviews (interviewing rigorously is itself a sell: A Players respect a serious process).
3. Between offer and acceptance (highest-risk window: stay in close contact, address every hesitation).
4. Between acceptance and the first day (guard against counteroffers and cold feet; keep the relationship warm).
5. During the first 100 days on the job (the sell isn't done until they're established; early neglect reopens the decision).

Persistence closes A Players: treat "no" as an opening bid, make them feel like a top priority (board-level time commitment landed Jamie Dimon at Bank One), and be flexible on the issues that genuinely matter to them.

## 10. Installing the Method in an Organization

The method only pays off as an organizational habit, not a one-off technique:

- Train every hiring manager on the full method; expect every manager to build and maintain a team of A Players (Allied Waste: 27 A Player hires/promotions at a 90% success rate in 18 months, company value up 67%).
- Make talent everyone's job: sourcing outcomes on every scorecard, managers as chief recruiting officers, weekly calendar time protected for sourcing.
- Use scorecards beyond hiring: cascade strategy into role outcomes, run annual reviews and talent reviews against them, rate the existing team A/B/C and act on it (coach B's toward A, move or remove C's, keep A Players challenged).
- Expected resistance: "it takes too much time." Answer: the method front-loads hours (7-8 hours of structured interviews) to avoid the 15x-salary cost and months of pain per mis-hire. Slower per hire, dramatically faster per successful hire.
- The loop closes: A Players hire and refer more A Players, and the who problem stops being the #1 problem.
