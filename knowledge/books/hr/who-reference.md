# Who — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing hiring processes, or advising on team-building.*
*Book: "Who: The A Method for Hiring" — Geoff Smart & Randy Street*

---

## 1. Core Premise: Who, Not What

- The most important business decisions are not *what* decisions (strategy, products, processes) but *who* decisions: the people you put in place to make the *what* decisions.
- The four failure points where *who* mistakes happen, at every level from call-center worker to CEO:
  1. Unclear about what is needed in a job
  2. Weak flow of candidates
  3. No trust in the ability to pick the right candidate from similar-looking candidates
  4. Losing candidates you really want
- The cost of a mishire: **15x base salary** in hard costs and productivity loss (ghSMART client studies). One bad $100K hire = $1.5M+. Ten per year = $15M down the drain.
- Prevalence: typical manager hiring success rate is ~50% (Drucker and others). The A Method targets 90%+.
- **A Player definition (the two mathematical elements):** a candidate who has *at least a 90% chance* of achieving a set of outcomes that *only the top 10%* of possible candidates could achieve. Not just a superstar — the *right* superstar: can do the job you defined AND fits your culture.
- An A Player is always relative to YOUR scorecard and YOUR culture. If teamwork is a core value, a spotlight-seeking star is not an A Player no matter how productive. A Players work well together because each is a specialist selected for a defined role.
- What drives business success (survey of 400+ CEOs/billionaires interviewed for the book): management talent >50% of the equation, execution ~20%, strategy 17%, external factors 11%. Blackstone/TPG's own deal analysis: keys to private equity success are (1) buying right, (2) an A management team, (3) selling right — and the difference between an A and a B CEO produces an order-of-magnitude difference in return.
- The A Method has four steps (the four strokes of the letter "A"): **Scorecard → Source → Select → Sell.**
- Proof case: John Zillmer at Allied Waste hired/promoted 27 A Players in 18 months at a 90% success rate, trained every manager on the method; company value rose 67% in those 18 months after five flat years.

## 2. The Ten Voodoo Hiring Methods (Failure Patterns)

All share the assumption that assessing a person is easy. The core painful truth: it is hard to see people for who they really are. The ten broken approaches:

1. **The Art Critic** — gut-instinct read of the candidate in minutes. Candidates who want the job can fake a short interview; gut feel is terribly inaccurate for hiring in (though useful for deciding who NOT to hire when something feels off).
2. **The Sponge** — everyone interviews the candidate, nobody coordinates, all ask the same superficial questions. Verdict rarely deeper than "he's a good guy."
3. **The Prosecutor** — trick questions and logic puzzles ("why are manhole covers round?"). Lands the most knowledgeable candidate; knowledge and ability to do the job are not the same thing.
4. **The Suitor** — spends the interview selling the opportunity, talking instead of listening. Lands candidates without assessing fit.
5. **The Trickster** — behavioral gimmicks (drop a wad of paper to see if they pick it up). Tests nothing relevant.
6. **The Animal Lover** — pet questions ("what animal would you be?"). Zero predictive validity.
7. **The Chatterbox** — the "la-di-da" interview: nice lunch, nice chat, sports and weather. Enjoyable, useless.
8. **The Psychological/Personality Tester** — bubble tests for selection decisions. The Handbook of Industrial/Organizational Psychology recommends against them for executive selection; savvy candidates fake the answers.
9. **The Aptitude Tester** — aptitude tests can be useful as screening tools for a specific ability, but never as the sole determinant.
10. **The Fortune-Teller** — hypothetical questions ("what would you do if…?"). Fifty years of academic literature argues against them; answers are rehearsed ideals. It's the walk that counts, not the talk.

Per the research the authors cite (4,000+ studies and meta-analyses), traditional interviewing is "almost a random predictor" of job performance. The fix in all four A Method interviews: stop judging how someone acts *during* the interview; collect facts and data about the performance track record spanning decades.

## 3. Scorecard: The Blueprint for the Role

Not a job description. A document describing exactly what a person must accomplish and how — the definition of A performance for one specific role. First failure point of hiring: not being crystal clear about what you want the hire to accomplish (different stakeholders carry conflicting vague notions).

**Three components:**

1. **Mission** — 1-5 plain-language sentences on why the role exists. Test: candidates, recruiters, and teammates understand what you want without clarifying questions. No corporate gobbledygook. Example: "Grow revenue through direct contacts with industrial customers" — not channel sales, not new verticals, not administration.
2. **Outcomes** — 3 to 8 specific, objective outcomes ranked by importance, defining what must get done. "Grow revenue from $25 million to $50 million by end of year three." Job descriptions fail because they list activities (what a person *will be doing*); scorecards work because they specify outcomes (what a person *must get done*). Where quantification is hard, make outcomes observable ("create and implement a new marketing campaign within 180 days of start date"). High-but-reasonable outcomes scare off B/C Players and attract A Players who want big challenges. Specificity liberates hires: they know exactly what they'll be judged on.
3. **Competencies** — how you expect the person to operate. Two layers: role competencies (derived from outcomes) plus 5-8 cultural competencies placed on *every* scorecard in the company.

**Critical competencies for A Players** (University of Chicago analysis of the ghSMART CEO database merged with CEO interviews, prioritized): efficiency, honesty/integrity, organization and planning, aggressiveness, follow-through on commitments, intelligence, analytical skills, attention to detail, persistence, proactivity. Extended list includes: ability to hire A Players and develop people (for managers), flexibility/adaptability, calm under pressure, strategic thinking, creativity, enthusiasm, work ethic, high standards, listening, openness to criticism, communication, teamwork, persuasion. Don't over-narrow the competency list — different people achieve the same outcome through different approaches.

**Hire the specialist, not the generalist.** The all-around athlete with the knock-your-socks-off resume rarely works: job requirements are narrow, so look for narrow, deep competence matched to the actual business problem ("right person, right job, right time, right skill set for the business problem that exists" — Chabraja, General Dynamics). Scorecards must evolve with the situation — Intel needed Noyce the inventor, then Moore the technologist, then Grove the driver.

**Cultural fit is non-negotiable.** One in three billionaires/CEOs interviewed named failure to evaluate cultural fit as a top cause of hiring mistakes. People who don't fit fail even when perfectly talented. Quick culture-mapping exercise: gather the leadership team and ask "what adjectives describe our culture?" Translate the answers into competencies on every scorecard. Have the discipline to pass on talented people who are not a fit (Centerbridge passed on a high performer known for disrespecting management teams — and still hit 90%+ A Player rate raising a $3.2B first-time fund).

**Scorecard as management system, not just hiring tool.** Cascade: strategy → CEO/senior-team outcomes → outcomes for each level below, so everyone holds outcomes that support the strategy. Scorecards also: set expectations with new hires, monitor progress, objectify annual reviews, and feed the annual talent review. (Only 10% of 200 CEOs at a Fortune conference had written objectives for their direct reports.)

**How to create a scorecard (4 steps):** (1) Mission: 1-5 sentences on why the role exists. (2) Outcomes: 3-8 specific, objective outcomes defining A performance. (3) Competencies: role-based ones plus 5-8 cultural ones on every scorecard. (4) Ensure alignment and communicate: pressure-test against the business plan and interfacing roles' scorecards, then share with peers and recruiters.

## 4. Source: Generate a Flow of A Players Before You Need Them

Great hirers treat themselves as chief recruiting officers, always sourcing before a slot opens, never waiting for a vacancy and a panicked call to HR. Passive "talent pool" hiring produces stagnant candidates. Ads generate a tidal wave of resumes but a lousy flow of the right candidates. Sourcing is about process and discipline, not innovation.

**Methods, ranked by effectiveness:**

1. **Referrals from your professional and personal networks** — the #1 method. 77% of the industry leaders interviewed named referrals as their top sourcing technique (yet it's the least practiced among average managers). The one question: **"Who are the most talented people you know that I should hire?"** List your ten most talented contacts, call one per week, ask the question at the end of each call — ten calls can generate 50-100 names. Extend it to customers (best salespeople who call on them), business partners, suppliers, and social settings. Patrick Ryan (Aon, $13B) set a personal goal of recruiting 30 people per year and asked all his managers to do the same; he sourced his own CEO successor this way after years of relationship-nurturing.
2. **Referrals from employees** — better-targeted because employees know the culture. Middleby: employees referred 85% of new hires. Tudor Investment: 60% higher success rate for employee-referred hires ("it takes A Players to know A Players"). Make it systematic: put a sourcing outcome on every employee's scorecard (e.g., "Source 5 A Players per year who pass our phone screen") and attach a referral bonus or incentive. Side benefit: everyone starts viewing the business through a *who* lens.
3. **Deputizing friends of the firm** — pay outside "deputies" a bounty (from gift certificates up to ~$5,000 or, at BSMB, fee-free fund investment) for successful referrals. Advisory boards serve the same function at early-stage companies. Require regular check-ins and verify the incentive is strong enough for busy people.
4. **External recruiters** — useful, but only as effective as the exposure you give them. Treat them like partners: share the scorecard, let them deeply understand your firm and culture; the best will educate you about the talent market.
5. **Recruiting researchers** — contract researchers who map a market and feed names (no interviewing). Cheap pipeline, variable cost. Downside: thin qualification — vetting falls on you; watch for quantity-over-quality clogging.

**Sourcing system (the part that matters most):** (1) capture every name and contact into one system (index cards, spreadsheet with follow-up dates, or an applicant-tracking system — the tool doesn't matter, the discipline does); (2) schedule **30 minutes weekly** on the calendar to call top prospects until you get at least one live conversation. Opening script: "Sue recommended that you and I connect. I understand you are great at what you do. I am always on the lookout for talented people and would love the chance to get to know you — even if you are perfectly content in your current job." Done well: 40+ new connections per year. Always close with the referral question to compound the network.

Case study: Bank One's recruitment of Jamie Dimon — board members refined a generic scorecard with the recruiter, personally traveled to meet candidates ("we took no as an opening bid"), used every conversation to source additional names and references, and committed chairman-level time (John Hall: 100% of his time, 8-12 candidates seen). Candidate-side lesson: board commitment itself sold Dimon on the job.

## 5. Select Step 1: The Screening Interview

Purpose: cull obvious B and C Players fast, by phone, in max 30 minutes. Structured — same questions every time.

**The four screening questions:**

1. **"What are your career goals?"** — Ask before telling them anything about the role, so they can't parrot you. Screen out: no goals, or goals that echo your website. Listen for passion aligned with the role. Wrong role type (wants to manage, you need an individual contributor) = pass the name along, stop considering.
2. **"What are you really good at professionally?"** — Push for **8-12 strengths with examples** to build a complete picture. Screen out on a major gap between strengths and scorecard.
3. **"What are you not good at or not interested in doing professionally?"** — Reject cookie-cutter answers ("I work too hard"): "That sounds like a strength. What are you *really* not good at?" If still stuck, deploy the truth serum: "If you advance, we'll ask you to set up references with bosses, peers, and subordinates — so what do you think *they* will say you're not good at?" Need **5-8 real weaknesses**; too few, all disguised strengths, or scorecard deal-killers = screen out.
4. **"Who were your last five bosses, and how will they each rate your performance on a 1-10 scale when we talk to them?"** — "*When* we talk to them," never "if." Press for why each rating. Calibration: look for 8s, 9s, 10s; 7 is neutral; **6 or below is bad — a self-reported 6 really means 2.** But listen to context (Dimon flatly said "I was fired" at Citigroup and was still the right hire).

**Mechanics:**
- Set expectations up front: "First twenty minutes I'd like to get to know you; after that I'm happy to answer your questions. Sound good?"
- If it's going badly, collapse the call — accelerate the questions, finish in 15-20 minutes. If it's going well, extend or schedule more. You own the process.
- Post-call bar: strengths match the scorecard, weaknesses manageable, and you're *thrilled* to bring them in. Any hesitation ("let's test them a bit more in person") = screen out. Better to miss a potential A Player than burn hours on a borderline B/C ("hit the gong fast").
- Benchmark: after tightening screens, Halma's Health Optics division passes only **10-20%** of phone screens to in-person interviews. Target: narrow to 2-5 candidates before the heavy artillery.

**"Getting curious" follow-up framework (used in every A Method interview):** after any answer, probe with questions starting with **"What?" "How?" or "Tell me more."** (What do you mean? What's an example? What was your role? What were the results? How did you do that? How did that feel? How did that go?) None needs to be longer than six words. Keep going until you can see the real story.

## 6. Select Step 2: The Who Interview (the Core Instrument)

The key interview of the Select step: a **chronological walk-through of the candidate's career**, the style of interview that a half-century of industrial-psychology research shows is the most valid and reliable predictor of performance (lineage: Brad Smart's Topgrading). The power is in patterns: understand someone's story and their patterns and you can predict how they'll perform.

**Structure:**
- Open with highs and lows of their educational experience, then cover each job of the past ~15 years, **earliest to most recent** — the chronological order is essential; working backward scrambles candidates' thinking and kills the narrative.
- Divide long careers into "chapters" (a single job, or a group of jobs spanning 3-5 years) and ask the five questions per chapter.
- Duration: **3 hours on average** (up to 5 for big-company CEOs, ~90 minutes for entry-level). Every hour spent saves hundreds of hours not managing C Players.
- The **hiring manager conducts it personally** (you own the hire), **in tandem with a colleague** — one asks, one takes notes.
- Kick off with an expectation-setting script: explain the chronological format, the five questions, that career goals and their questions come at the end, that "80% of the process is in this room" with reference calls after if you mutually continue, and that you'll guide the pace toward the most recent, most relevant jobs.

**The five questions, per job/chapter:**

1. **"What were you hired to do?"** — Reconstruct what their scorecard would have been: mission, outcomes, how success was measured.
2. **"What accomplishments are you most proud of?"** — A Players describe outcomes linked to the job's expectations; B/C Players talk generally about events, people, and what they liked, never results. Watch for accomplishments uncorrelated with what the job needed.
3. **"What were some low points during that job?"** — Nobody has zero lows. Reframe relentlessly until they share: What went wrong? Biggest mistake? What would you do differently? Where were peers stronger?
4. **"Who were the people you worked with?"** — The **TORC** technique (Threat Of Reference Check). Exact sequence matters: (a) get the boss's name and make them spell it while you visibly write it down — signals you *will* call; (b) "What was it like working with him/her?"; (c) "What *will* he/she say were your biggest strengths and areas for improvement?" — *will*, not *would*. Reciprocity kicks in: they just described the boss, now they owe you the boss's view of them. Never accept "I don't know" — keep reframing. For managers add: "How would you rate the team you inherited?" (do they upgrade teams or accept the hand dealt?) and "When we speak with your team, what will they say were your biggest strengths and weaknesses as a manager?"
5. **"Why did you leave that job?"** — Promoted, recruited, or fired? Don't accept vague answers ("my boss and I didn't connect"). A Players are pulled to bigger things and bosses mourn their departure; B/C Players get nudged or pushed out.

**Five master tactics:**

1. **Interrupting** — mandatory, roughly every 3-4 minutes, or candidates ramble for hours. Never the stop-sign way (shames them, they clam up). Instead: match their enthusiasm, reflect ("Wow — that pig farm must have smelled horrible!"), then redirect to a relevant topic you'd "love to hear about." High rapport is what produces the most valuable data.
2. **The Three P's** — calibrate any claimed accomplishment: performance vs. **previous** year, vs. **plan**, vs. **peers**.
3. **Push versus pull** — pushed = "it was mutual," "my role shrank," "I missed my number." Pulled = "my biggest client hired me," "my old boss recruited me to a bigger job." **Do not hire anybody pushed out of 20% or more of their jobs** — roughly 3x higher chance of being a chronic B/C Player.
4. **Painting a picture** — you understand an answer only when you can literally see it ("empathic imagination"). "Excellent communicator" might mean brilliant writer and terrible presenter; get curious until the generic dissolves into specifics.
5. **Stopping at the stop signs** — inconsistency between words and body language is the biggest lying tell (shifting, looking down, covering the mouth while claiming "we did great"). Hit the brakes and probe. Posture: biographer, not investigative reporter — you want facts and texture, not dirt. Suspend judgment during the interview; candidates volunteer astonishing things when you stay curious (the "$3 million slap" story surfaced this way).

## 7. Select Steps 3-4: Focused Interviews and Reference Interviews

**Focused interviews** — the odds enhancer after the Who Interview, and the way to involve the team.
- Like a behavioral interview with one crucial difference: **focused on the scorecard's outcomes and competencies**, not a vague job description or intuition.
- Assign each interviewer a *slice* of the scorecard (e.g., three interviewers splitting four outcomes and six competencies by theme). 45-60 minutes each. Emphasize: this is NOT a second Who Interview (one full career walk-through is enough), and everyone follows the script — no reverting to voodoo methods.
- Get curious after every answer with What/How/Tell me more until you know *what* the person did and *how*.
- **Cultural-fit interview**: a focused interview built on the company's cultural values rather than role outcomes (First Solar runs at least one per candidate, after the Who Interview — capability plus culture together).

**Typical interview day:** 8:30 team huddle (scorecard, resume, screening notes, roles) → greet/orient candidate → 9:00-12:00 tandem Who Interview → informal lunch with non-interviewing teammates → 1:30-4:30 focused interviews → thank candidate, explain next steps → 4:45-5:30 candidate discussion: rate the scorecard from the day's data, hiring manager makes go/no-go on reference calls. (Variant: run focused interviews as a separate second round only for Who Interview passers.)

**Reference interviews** — never skip them; skipping loses ~25% of what you should know (Hurst). 64% of the moguls interviewed run reference calls for *every* hire.
- **Pick the references yourself** from Who Interview notes — don't rely on the candidate's list.
- **Have the candidate set up the calls** — doubles the chance references actually talk, and gets past no-reference company policies.
- **Do seven**: you personally do ~4, colleagues do 3. Mix: **3 past bosses, 2 peers or customers, 2 subordinates.** For CEOs especially, go two or three levels down — subordinates tell the truth ("don't believe the coach; talk to the players and the trainer").
- Five questions: (1) In what context did you work with the person? (warm-up/memory jogger) (2) Biggest strengths? (3) Biggest areas for improvement **"back then"**? — those two words liberate references to speak, and people don't change much: "people aren't mutual funds," past performance predicts future performance. (4) Rate overall performance 1-10 — and why? Look for consistent 8-10; **a 6 is really a 2**; probe any discrepancy with the candidate's self-ratings. (5) Test what the candidate told you via TORC data: "The person mentioned *you might say* he was disorganized — can you tell me more?" — "you might say" grants permission because the candidate raised it.
- Also gather off-list references from your own network (check local labor law — some countries, e.g. Canada, require candidate permission).
- **Break the code.** People hate giving negative references, so they speak in code: qualified "if…then" praise ("if you're willing to have a guy disagree with you, then hire him"), hesitations and *um*s on the tough questions, confirming only employment dates, faint or lukewarm praise. **"Faint praise in reference interviews is damning praise."** A genuinely positive reference brims with unhesitating enthusiasm — that spark is the clearest confirmation of an A Player.

## 8. Deciding: Skill-Will Match and Red Flags

**The Skill-Will Bull's-Eye:**
- **Skill** = what they *can* do — rate each scorecard **outcome** A/B/C: A only if the data says ≥90% chance they achieve it.
- **Will** = what they *want* to do (motivations and competencies) — rate each **competency** the same way: A only if ≥90% confident they'll display it.
- Bull's-eye = ≥90% confident they can get the job done (skills match outcomes) AND ≥90% confident they'll fit (will matches mission and competencies). An A Player is skill AND will matching the scorecard — anything less is a B or C regardless of apparent talent.

**Decision procedure:** (1) pull out the completed scorecards per candidate; (2) finalize an overall A/B/C grade per candidate, updated for reference-call data and team input; (3) **no A's → restart at Source** (never settle); (4) one A → hire; (5) multiple A's → rank and hire the best. Nobody is perfect — weight the most important outcomes and competencies.

**Red flags during the process** (none alone is a deal-killer, but they correlate strongly with apparent-A-Players who sink to B/C once hired):
- Doesn't mention past failures
- Exaggerates answers
- Takes credit for others' work
- Speaks poorly of past bosses
- Can't explain job moves
- The people most important to them are unsupportive of the change
- (Managerial hires) has never had to hire or fire anybody
- More interested in compensation/benefits than the job
- Tries too hard to look like an expert
- Self-absorbed

**Marshall Goldsmith's behavioral derailers to watch in interviews:** winning too much (boasting about battles that don't matter), adding too much value (piling their ideas onto yours — ego), starting sentences with "no/but/however," telling the world how smart they are (excessive credit-taking), destructive comments about previous colleagues (they'll do it about you next), passing the buck/blaming, making excuses (challenges were always someone else's fault), and the excessive need to "be me" ("that's just me, I'm not organized") — announces they won't adapt to your culture; don't hire.

**Full Select checklist:** screening interview (4 questions, What/How/Tell me more) → Who Interview (tandem, chronological, 5 questions per chapter) → focused interviews (scorecard slices assigned to team) → candidate discussion (grade via skill-will) → 7 reference interviews (candidate sets up) → final decision (re-verify the bull's-eye).

## 9. Sell: Landing the Candidate You Chose

Deciding who to hire and getting them are different things (five frogs on a log: one decides to jump — five are still there). Selling means putting yourself in the candidate's shoes and addressing what candidates actually care about: **the Five F's**. Identify which ones matter to this candidate; be sincere — these are areas for honest attention, not manipulation tools.

1. **Fit** — the most important. Show how their goals, talents, and values connect to your vision, strategy, and culture: "Here's where we're going; here's how you fit in." A Players are also looking for roles where *they* can be A Players. Sell all three alignments: position, company, culture. Differentiator: show you care as much about the fit *for them* as for you — "99% of your competitors are not doing that."
2. **Family** — the job change is a family trauma; "what can we do to make this as easy as possible for your family?" The family often makes the tough call, especially at small companies — the candidate is already sold or they wouldn't be there; sell the spouse, kids, parents, and friends. Woo them concretely: tours, dinners, real-estate help, personal attention in every conversation (Malone asked Maffei about his wife and kids in nearly every call to move him from Seattle to Denver). Failure mode: candidates dropping out at the eleventh hour because the family was never brought on board — including one who accepted, drank the celebration champagne, then withdrew after the honeymoon because nobody had sold his new bride.
3. **Freedom** — A Players won't tolerate micromanagement. The paradox: leaders gain more control by ceding control, because the scorecard already defines the outcomes they'll be measured on. Tactic: invite the candidate to reference-check *you* as a manager. In nonprofits, purpose plus room to prove what they can do is the strongest lever.
4. **Fortune** — money is rarely the key motivator (Herzberg: a raise given today is forgotten by tomorrow) and can't stand alone, but demonstrate the financial upside: company stability, growth record, the wealth prior hires achieved. "There is no such thing as a bargain in the labor market" — don't underpay or overpay; **pay on a performance basis, linking variable comp to scorecard attainment** so top pay flows only from A performance.
5. **Fun** — the work environment and relationships; what fun means is defined by your culture (for some it's doing what you love, for others the chance to use their talents to maximum effect).

**The Five Waves of selling** — selling isn't a final step; it runs through the whole process, with extra energy needed at each wave:
1. **When you source** — listen for which F's matter ("where are their hooks?"); people drop their guard early, giving you a faster, richer view of what they want.
2. **When you interview** — sell in the question time at the end of each session, framed by what you heard (their questions reveal their hooks).
3. **Between offer and acceptance** — the danger zone. Don't "give them space": silence is your worst enemy, and they likely hold a counteroffer. Stay in constant touch, work the F's, make the human connection (3M nearly lost George Buckley when lawyer-led negotiations made him feel "like they were buying a refrigerator, not a person"). Managers undersell far more often than they oversell.
4. **Between acceptance and first day** — celebrate the yes with a meaningful splash (flowers, balloons, gift), keep listening for F-related concerns; cold feet, counteroffers, and family doubt are all still live.
5. **First 100 days on the job** — new hires have an alarming failure/remorse rate; invest in real onboarding beyond an HR orientation. Everything learned via scorecard and Who Interview is a head start: "insights that typically take a year of working with them to figure out."

**Persistence** — the one-word answer to what matters most in selling. Great leaders don't take the first no; they keep positive pressure on until the A Player joins (calls every couple of weeks for months, repeated visits, escalating creativity — the book's extreme case ran from a $175K salary to $850K plus apartment, car, and chinchilla coat, and the hire turned the company around within a year). "Do whatever it takes when you are sure you have identified the right person."

## 10. Installing the Method in an Organization

**Ten steps to install the A Method company-wide:**
1. Make people a top priority — top leaders spend as much as 60% of their time on people; make it a visible top-three priority so it isn't dismissed as flavor of the month.
2. Follow the A Method yourself — lead by example; that earns the right to expect it of others.
3. Build support among your executive team or peers (books, off-sites, workshops).
4. Cast a clear vision ("we will win with A Players in every role") and reinforce it in every communication, backed by action.
5. Train every manager hands-on in the method.
6. Remove barriers — kill any HR policy or practice that blocks implementation.
7. Implement backstop policies: **(a)** every manager's scorecard carries "achieve 90%+ hiring success; build and retain a team of 90%+ A Players by [date]"; **(b)** no scorecard, no job requisition; **(c)** no Who Interview and rated scorecard, no offer — HR is the gatekeeper.
8. Recognize and reward users — link a substantial bonus portion to the 90% hiring-success outcome.
9. Remove managers who refuse to build a better team with the method (after fair chances).
10. Celebrate wins, then loop back to step one for the next round of improvement.

You don't have to be CEO — install it in your function or department; the results become a beacon for the rest of the company.

**Legal guardrails** (the method's consistency and fact-focus make it more legal and fair than ad hoc hiring):
1. Relevance — reject only for scorecard-relevant reasons; stick to facts.
2. Standardization — identical process for all candidates regardless of demographic group.
3. Nondiscriminatory language in interviews and written forms.
4. No illegal questions — in the US: marital status, children/pregnancy, age, birthplace, medical condition, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, disabilities (unless directly job-relevant); rules vary by country, check local law.

**Team-building corollaries:**
- A full team of A Players is both possible and desirable — they don't collide because each is a specialist selected against a scorecard *you* defined, including your cultural values. A star who violates the culture is not an A Player.
- The culture needs enough elasticity to absorb A Players who challenge you where you need challenging (Tesco hiring outsider John Hoerner to run clothing: "Tesco has changed them, but they have changed Tesco as well").

**Beyond hiring — development, promotion, succession:** most managers revert to voodoo methods here too, promoting on tenure or current-role performance instead of building a scorecard for the *future* role ("what got you promoted to one rank won't necessarily get you promoted to the next" — Gen. Wesley Clark). Apply scorecard + Who Interview + reference-style colleague interviews (12-20 per executive in the cited trillion-dollar-bank succession case) to assess internal talent against the roles of two to three years out.

**Cheetahs vs. Lambs (the CEO study):** 313 Who Interviews of private-equity-backed CEOs (2000-2005) matched to actual financial results with Steven Kaplan's University of Chicago team — the largest study of its kind pairing CEO trait assessments with financial performance.
- **Lambs**: mastered soft skills — open to feedback, great listeners, respectful; boards love them. Success rate: **57%**.
- **Cheetahs**: move quickly, act aggressively, work hard, persistent, set high standards and hold people accountable. Success rate: **100%** — every single one created significant investor value.
- Interpretation: emotional intelligence matters *only when matched with the propensity to get things done*. Cheetahs have soft skills too; the difference is knowing when to stop gathering feedback and attack the target. Given the choice, err toward fast-and-focused over slow-and-extremely-collaborative.
- The Bill Koch corollary (America's Cup 1992): scorecard the team, rate everyone 1-10 on the criteria, require 9-10 on attitude and teamwork, organize roles to minimize each person's weaknesses ("they just had to be exceptional at one thing"), and cut superstars with bad attitudes — "you can't take a superstar and train them to have a good attitude."
