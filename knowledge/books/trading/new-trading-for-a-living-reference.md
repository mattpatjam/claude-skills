# The New Trading for a Living — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, advising on trading/investing psychology, or designing risk management practice.*
*Book: "The New Trading for a Living" — Alexander Elder*

---

## 1. The Structural Deck Stacked Against Traders

Before any psychology or charting matters, Elder establishes that trading is a **minus-sum game**, not a zero-sum one. In a zero-sum bet, one side's win exactly equals the other's loss. In trading, the industry (brokers, exchanges) drains money from every transaction via commissions and slippage, so winners collect less than losers lose. Being "better than average" is not good enough — a trader must be head and shoulders above the crowd just to break even against the house.

**Commissions**: even small per-trade fees compound into a large percentage of an active account over a year. A trader doing one round-trip a day, four days a week, at $10/side, spends about $4,000/year — 20% of a $20,000 account. Shop aggressively for low commissions; design a system that trades less often.

**Slippage**: the gap between the price seen when placing an order and the price the fill actually executes at. Elder's rule of thumb: the average trader loses roughly **three times more to slippage than to commissions**. Two order types:
- **Limit order** — guarantees price, not fill ("give me that apple at 49 cents").
- **Market order** — guarantees fill, not price ("give me that apple"). Gets filled at the bad side of the bid-ask spread (buys at the ask, sells at the bid).

Rule: use limit orders almost exclusively; reserve market orders for stops (once a stop is hit, get out fast — that is not the moment to economize). Trade liquid, high-volume vehicles; enter during quiet periods, not while the market is running (jumping onto a moving train guarantees a worse fill).

**Bid-ask spreads** widen in thin markets and during sharp moves, and narrow in quiet, liquid markets — another reason to prefer liquidity and calm entries.

## 2. Individual Psychology: Fantasies, Self-Destruction, and the Trading-as-Addiction Model

### Universal Trading Fantasies
Elder catalogs recurring, distorting beliefs that keep losers losing:
- **The Brain Myth** — "I lost because I don't know the secret." Fuels a market for gurus, canned systems, and charlatans. Trading is intellectually simple (five data points: open, high, low, close, volume); what separates winners from losers is not intelligence or secret knowledge.
- **The Undercapitalization Myth** — "I'd win with a bigger account." In reality, a loser's problem is overtrading and poor money management, not account size; a bigger account just takes longer to destroy. Losers who raise money from friends on the strength of a paper track record usually lose that too.
- **The Autopilot Myth** — the belief that wealth-seeking can be fully automated. Every automatic ("black box") system eventually self-destructs because markets keep changing while rigid rules don't adapt. This fantasy echoes an infantile wish to passively receive nourishment (money flowing in like warm milk) without effort.
- **The Cult of Personality / Guru-seeking** — traders in distress look for a "little Stalin" to think for them. Elder identifies three guru types: **market cycle gurus** (ride a ~4-year theory-market alignment, then crash when the market changes character), **magic method gurus** (sell a "new" analytic edge that degrades once popularized), and **dead gurus** (posthumous legends — e.g., W.D. Gann's real estate was ~$100K, not the mythical $50 million — kept alive by people selling courses off an expired copyright).

### Self-Destructiveness and Gambling
Freud linked compulsive gambling to unconscious drives; Dr. Ralph Greenson divided gamblers into normal (can stop), professional (gambling as a business), and neurotic (driven, unable to stop, chasing the omnipotent feeling of winning rather than a plan). Trading in markets gives gamblers a more "respectable" outlet than betting with a bookie, but the psychology is identical. **Key sign of gambling**: inability to resist the urge to bet. If results are poor and you can't stop for even a month, that is a Gamblers-Anonymous-level problem, not a strategy problem.

**Self-sabotage**: much of trading failure is not incompetence or bad luck but an unconscious wish to fail, replaying old patterns (blaming bosses, brokers, spouses) rather than confronting one's own role. The market provides no "safety net" other professions have (colleagues who intervene when you behave badly) — it is closer to a demolition derby where every trader is trying to hit every other trader.

### The AA Framework (Elder's central psychological insight)
Elder's breakthrough came from attending an Alcoholics Anonymous meeting and realizing that substituting "loss" for "alcohol" made every AA share applicable to trading. Key transferable concepts:
- **Denial** — a losing trader, like a drinking alcoholic, insists he can "manage" losses that are already out of control.
- **Rock bottom** — real change usually only follows a painful-enough crisis that punctures denial.
- **The First Step** — an alcoholic admits powerlessness over alcohol; a trader must admit "I am a loser, I am powerless over losses" and define a **businessman's risk**: a predetermined, capped loss per trade that can never be exceeded (this becomes the 2% Rule in Part 9).
- **One day at a time** — don't plan for a lifetime of perfect trading; focus on not exceeding your risk limit today.
- **"Losers Anonymous"** — deliberately harsh branding (as AA doesn't call itself "Drinkers Anonymous") keeps attention on the self-destructive tendency rather than false confidence. A suggested morning ritual: "Good morning, I am a loser. I have it in me to do serious financial damage to my account" — keeps humility and risk-discipline front of mind regardless of yesterday's result.

### Winners, Losers, and Emotional Trading
The market is like an ocean: indifferent to you, uncontrollable, but not malicious. You cannot control it, only yourself — the sailor studies currents and weather but never controls the sea. Emotional highs from winning and lows from losing both impair judgment; a professional aims for a flat emotional affect ("I couldn't tell if I was $2,000 ahead or behind"). Casinos ply gamblers with free liquor to keep them emotional; markets don't need to — traders supply their own intoxication via greed and fear.

Seven rules Elder distilled for graduating from wild amateur to calm professional: commit to the long haul; keep learning with healthy skepticism; don't rush, take time to learn; develop an "if A then B" analytical method and test everything on real data; build a money-management plan where survival comes before growth, growth before big profits; recognize the trader as the weakest link in any system; accept that changing yourself is harder than changing your system, but necessary.

## 3. Mass Psychology and Crowd Behavior

### Price as a Psychological Event
Central axiom: **"Each price is a momentary consensus of value of all market participants, expressed in action."** Every tick reflects buyers, sellers, and an undecided crowd whose mere presence pressures both sides to act faster. Technical analysis is therefore **"for-profit social psychology"** — akin to political poll-taking, partly science (statistics, computers) and partly art (judgment, feel for rhythm).

### Crowd Psychology Principles (from LeBon, Mackay, Surowiecki)
- Joining a crowd makes individuals more credulous, impulsive, and prone to seek a leader — an evolved survival instinct (safety in numbers against predators) that is actively harmful in markets.
- **Price is the leader of the market crowd.** Traders relate to a rising/falling trend the way children relate to a rewarding or punishing parent, which is why objective signals get ignored under stress.
- A trend that rewards you (moves your way) increases confidence and participation; a trend that punishes you provokes fear, bargaining, and denial — exactly the emotional traps that prevent rational exits.
- **"The Wisdom of Crowds"** (Surowiecki) distinguishes a mob (members influence each other, creating emotional waves) from a smart group (members decide independently, then pool information). Elder's SpikeTrade community is designed on this principle: members submit picks blind, without seeing others' choices, and only compare notes after the "vote" is locked in.
- **A trick to detect your own bias**: flip your chart upside down. If it still looks like a buy, you have a bullish bias to correct; if it now looks like a sell, you were carrying a bearish bias.

### Managing vs. Forecasting
Amateurs want forecasts; professionals manage probabilities, the way an ER doctor manages a knife wound rather than "forecasting" survival. The trader's job: extract information about which side (bulls/bears) is dominant, gauge how likely the current trend is to persist, apply conservative money management, and observe one's own emotional state — not predict the future.

### Consensus and Commitment (Contrarian) Indicators
Financial journalists and newsletter writers are structurally biased toward following trends (fear of losing subscribers, "monkey see, monkey do" in the industry) and are most bullish at tops, most bearish at bottoms. **Contrary opinion theory** (Mackay, Neill, Cohen's *Investors Intelligence*): when a large majority of advisors turn strongly bullish or bearish, the trend is exhausted, because there aren't enough new buyers/sellers left to sustain it. Magazine covers, clustered ads pushing the same "opportunity," and spam pitch volume all serve as crude but real contrarian tells. These indicators are not precision timing tools — they flag exhaustion, then hand off to technical indicators for exact entries.

**Commitments of Traders (COT) / insider data**: hedgers (commercials) are the closest thing to informed "smart money" in futures and are exempt from speculative position limits; large speculators (today, mostly commodity funds) underperform as a group; small traders are the reliable "wrong-way" crowd. Corporate insider buying/selling only matters in clusters (3+ insiders acting within a month) — a single insider transaction is noise. **Short interest** ("Short Percent of Float" and "Days to Cover") flags overcrowded short positions vulnerable to a short-covering squeeze; fear-driven short covering tends to be sharper than greed-driven buying.

## 4. Classical Chart Analysis

Elder is skeptical of subjective classical patterns (trendlines, head-and-shoulders) because they invite wishful thinking — "the market doesn't know diagonals," but it does remember horizontal price levels. He keeps only a small set of objective classical tools.

### Anatomy of a Price Bar
- **Opening price** — set largely by amateurs (overnight news readers, morning orders); tends to occur near a bar's high or low, creating an emotional extreme the market often recoils from later in the session.
- **Closing price** — set largely by professionals who trade all day and often fade the amateurs; a higher close than open suggests professionals were more bullish than amateurs that day (and vice versa). Futures accounts are marked to market on the close, making it especially significant.
- **High of the bar** — maximum power of bulls during that period.
- **Low of the bar** — maximum power of bears during that period.
- **Bar height (high minus low)** — reflects intensity of the bull/bear conflict; short bars = quiet market (better fills, less slippage); tall bars = boiling market (better for taking profits than entering).

### Support and Resistance
**Support** = a price level where buying overcomes selling, reversing a decline. **Resistance** = a level where selling overcomes buying, reversing a rally. Both exist because of trader **memory, pain, and regret**: those who got hurt at a level want a second chance to get out even; those who missed an opportunity want a second chance to get in. Support and resistance can switch roles once decisively broken (old resistance becomes new support).

Strength of a support/resistance zone depends on three factors, visualized as length/width/depth:
- **Length of time** it held (a 2-week range is weak, a 2-year range is major).
- **Height** of the congestion zone relative to price (roughly: 1% of price = minor, 3% = intermediate, 7%+ = can grind down a major trend).
- **Volume** traded within the zone (high volume = strong emotional commitment).

Old support/resistance levels weaken over time as the traders who remember the pain exit the market and are replaced by newcomers with no emotional stake in that price.

**False breakouts**: one of Elder's favorite, most reliable patterns. A price dips below support (or above resistance), fails to hold, and closes back inside the range — signaling that the breakout side has been rejected. Place a protective stop near the extreme of the false breakout. Professionals systematically "fade" breakouts (trade against them) because most breakouts from ranges fail; true breakouts are confirmed by heavy volume and by indicators reaching new extremes in the breakout's direction.

### Trends vs. Trading Ranges
Uptrend = higher highs and higher lows; downtrend = lower highs and lower lows; trading range = irregular, roughly flat highs/lows. Markets spend more time ranging than trending (crowds are mostly aimless; sustained trends require sustained agitation). Tactical implications:
- In trends, give positions room (wider stops) and follow strength (buy strength in uptrends, sell weakness in downtrends).
- In ranges, use tight stops and fade extremes (buy weakness, sell strength).
- **The Hard Right Edge**: trends are obvious in hindsight (middle of the chart) but foggy at the current, tradeable edge — decisions must be made under real uncertainty, and cutting losses fast beats waiting to be "right."
- **Conflicting timeframes**: the same market can be an uptrend on one timeframe and a downtrend on another simultaneously. Always analyze the longer timeframe first for strategic bias (core Triple Screen principle, Section 10 below).

### Kangaroo Tails ("Fingers")
A three-bar pattern: one unusually tall bar bracketed by two normal bars, protruding from a tight congestion zone. An upward tail flags a failed bull raid (sell signal); a downward tail flags a failed bear raid (buy signal) — the market tested a price, found no orders there, and is likely to reverse away from it. Kangaroo tails are considered reliable enough to trust across all timeframes and instruments; place a stop about halfway up/down the tail (not at its extreme tip, which would make risk too large).

## 5. Trend-Following Indicators: Moving Averages, MACD, and the Directional System

### Moving Averages
A moving average (MA) is a "composite photograph" of market consensus over its time window — it smooths out single-price noise the way averaging several days' photos of a person reveals mood trends. **The single most important signal of an MA is the direction of its slope**, not its level: rising = crowd growing bullish, falling = crowd growing bearish.

Simple MAs have a flaw: they jump twice per data point (once absorbing the new price, once dropping the old one at the trailing edge), producing meaningless "jumps." **Exponential moving averages (EMA)** solve this by weighting recent data more heavily and letting old data fade gradually rather than drop off a cliff. In a 10-day EMA, the latest close accounts for ~18% of the value.

**Choosing EMA length**: no magic number; shorter EMAs catch trends sooner but whipsaw more, longer EMAs whipsaw less but lag more. Elder favors a **dual-EMA pair at roughly a 2:1 ratio** (e.g., 26-day and 13-day, or 26-week and 13-week) applied consistently across markets and timeframes rather than optimized per stock.

**The Value Zone**: the space between the fast and slow EMA. Since a short EMA reflects short-term consensus of value and the long EMA reflects longer-term consensus, "value lives" between them. Buy pullbacks into the value zone during uptrends; sell short rallies into it during downtrends — buying at or below value and selling at or above it, rather than chasing.

**Channels/envelopes**: two lines drawn parallel to (usually) the slower EMA, sized so they contain ~95% of the last 100 bars' prices. Volatile markets need wider channels; quiet markets need narrower ones. The upper line marks overvalued/"manic" territory, the lower line undervalued/"depressed" territory (Buffett's "Mr. Market" analogy) — professionals fade extremes rather than chase them. **Average True Range (ATR) channels** (Kerry Lovvorn's method) plot lines at 1, 2, and 3 ATRs from the EMA; moves outside 3 ATRs are rare and usually mark exhaustion.

### MACD Lines and MACD-Histogram (Gerald Appel)
**Construction**:
1. Fast MACD line = 12-day EMA − 26-day EMA.
2. Signal line = 9-day EMA of the fast MACD line.
3. **MACD-Histogram = MACD line − Signal line**, plotted as a histogram around a zero centerline.

**Trading MACD Lines**: buy when the fast line crosses above the slow Signal line; sell short on the reverse crossover.

**MACD-Histogram** is one of Elder's most-used tools because its *slope* (not just its zero-line position) reveals whether the dominant crowd is gaining or losing strength — an early warning trend-following indicators alone can't give. Best buy signals: MACD-H below zero but ticking up (bears exhausting). Best sell signals: MACD-H above zero but ticking down (bulls exhausting).

**Divergences** (a core recurring concept applied to nearly every oscillator in the book): a **bullish divergence** occurs when price makes a new low but the indicator makes a shallower (higher) low than its prior low — bears are weaker than price alone suggests. A **bearish divergence** is the mirror image at tops. Rule: the indicator must cross the zero/centerline between the two bottoms/tops for it to count as a true divergence — a "no crossover, no divergence" test. Kerry Lovvorn's research found the most tradable divergences occur when the two bottoms/tops are **20–40 bars apart** (closer to 20 is better) and the second extreme is no more than half the depth/height of the first. **Triple divergences** (three successive bottoms/tops) are rarer and stronger, but only occur after a regular divergence first fails — another argument for tight risk control, so a failed signal doesn't wipe you out before the stronger one arrives.

**The Hound of the Baskervilles signal**: named for the Sherlock Holmes story where the absence of expected barking was the tell. When a normally reliable pattern (like a divergence) fails to produce the expected reversal and price keeps moving the "wrong" way, that lack of response is itself a strong signal that something fundamental has shifted — Elder treats this as one of the few situations where he'll consider a stop-and-reverse trade.

### The Directional System (J. Welles Wilder) and ADX
Measures which side (bulls/bears) is extending price outside the prior day's range.
- **Directional Movement (DM)**: the largest part of today's range that falls outside yesterday's range (+DM above, −DM below; zero if inside).
- **True Range (TR)**: the largest of (today's high − today's low), (today's high − yesterday's close), (today's low − yesterday's close).
- **Directional Indicators**: +DI = +DM ÷ TR; −DI = −DM ÷ TR, then smoothed (e.g., 13-day) into +DI13/−DI13.
- **DX** = |(+DI13 − −DI13)| ÷ (+DI13 + −DI13) × 100; **ADX** = a moving average (e.g., 13-day) of DX.

Trading rules: trade long only when +DI13 is above −DI13, short only when the reverse; only use trend-following methods when ADX is rising. When ADX falls below both directional lines, it flags a flat, sleepy market — not to be trend-traded, but worth watching, since major trends are born from such lulls. The single best signal: when ADX rises **four steps** from a bottom below both directional lines, it "rings a bell" on a new trend being born. When ADX turns down from above both lines, the major trend has stumbled — time to take partial profits.

**Average True Range (ATR)** as a stand-alone volatility tool: helps size entries (normal pullbacks bottom near −1 ATR below the EMA), stops (place at least 1 ATR from entry — anything tighter sits inside normal noise), and targets (scale out at +1, +2, +3 ATR).

## 6. Oscillators: Stochastic and RSI

Oscillators catch turning points in flat/ranging markets but give premature, dangerous signals once a real trend begins — the mirror-image weakness of trend-following tools. Rule of thumb for combining the two groups: only take an oscillator's counter-trend signal when it aligns with the higher-timeframe trend (this becomes Triple Screen's Second Screen).

### Stochastic (George Lane)
Measures where the close sits relative to the recent high-low range.
- **Raw %K** = (Close − Ln) ÷ (Hn − Ln) × 100, where Ln/Hn are the lowest/highest points over the chosen window (default 5 days).
- **%D** = a smoothed (typically 3-day) average of %K.
- **Fast Stochastic** plots %K and %D directly (sensitive, whipsaw-prone); **Slow Stochastic** adds another smoothing layer (fewer whipsaws).

Reference lines typically at 20/80. Trading signals in order of importance: (1) divergences between Stochastic and price — strongest signal; (2) overbought/oversold level crossings; (3) direction of both lines together (confirms short-term trend). Best used inside a range; combine with a higher-timeframe trend filter to avoid fighting a real trend.

### Relative Strength Index (RSI, J. Welles Wilder)
RSI = 100 − 100 ÷ (1 + RS), where RS = average net up-closes ÷ average net down-closes over the chosen window. Fluctuates 0–100; shorter windows (7–9 days) make turning points more visible. Reference lines commonly at 30/70 (or adjusted per bull/bear market bias, e.g., 40/80 in bull markets, 20/60 in bear markets), redrawn periodically so the lines cut across only the extreme 5% of the last 4–6 months of readings.

Signal hierarchy, in order of importance: (1) bullish/bearish divergences between RSI and price; (2) chart pattern breaks in RSI itself (RSI often breaks its own trendline a day or two ahead of price); (3) overbought/oversold level crossings. As with Stochastic, only trust daily buy signals when the weekly trend is up, and sell signals when the weekly trend is down.

## 7. Volume, Open Interest, and Volume-Based Indicators

**Volume reflects the degree of financial and emotional commitment** — pain among losers and conviction among winners — not just activity level. High volume in a trend confirms it (a fresh supply of new losers is fueling the move); shrinking volume warns the trend is running out of new participants and is ripe for reversal. A volume spike often marks capitulation and a coming reversal — more reliably at the end of downtrends (fear is sharp but short-lived) than at the end of uptrends (greed is slower, can persist after a spike).

Rule of thumb: "high volume" ≈ 25%+ above the trailing 2-week average; "low volume" ≈ 25%+ below.

### On-Balance Volume (OBV, Joseph Granville)
Running cumulative total: add the day's volume if price closes higher, subtract it if lower, unchanged if flat. OBV's pattern of highs/lows (not its absolute level) matters. New OBV highs confirm strength; divergences between OBV and price (price makes a new extreme, OBV doesn't) are the strongest signals.

### Accumulation/Distribution (A/D, Larry Williams)
A/D = [(Close − Open) ÷ (High − Low)] × Volume, cumulative. Unlike OBV, it credits only the fraction of the day's volume proportional to how decisively bulls or bears won that session (based on open-to-close, similar in spirit to Japanese candlesticks). Tracks whether professionals (who dominate closes) are more bullish or bearish than amateurs (who dominate opens); divergences between A/D and price are the strongest signals, sometimes revealing informed accumulation ahead of news.

### Force Index (Elder's own indicator)
Force Index = Volume(today) × [Close(today) − Close(yesterday)] — combines direction, magnitude, and volume into a single number. Raw Force Index is jagged; smoothing with an EMA makes it usable:
- **2-day EMA of Force Index** — highly sensitive; used to time entries within an established trend (buy when it dips negative during an uptrend, short when it ticks positive during a downtrend). Central to Triple Screen's Second Screen (Section 10).
- **13-day EMA of Force Index** — tracks the longer-term balance of power; crossing above/below zero signals a shift in control; divergences against price mark intermediate/major turning points.
- A down-spike to 5x normal depth followed by a recoil tends to precede a rally (fear spikes don't persist); the mirror pattern (up-spikes persisting) does not hold as reliably, since greed is a slower emotion.

### Open Interest
In futures/options, long and short positions are always exactly equal (every contract needs a buyer and seller), so **open interest = total long or total short contracts outstanding.** Rising OI = "fresh blood" (new conviction) entering on both sides — confirms the existing trend. Falling OI = both sides bailing out (winners cashing in, losers giving up) — trend nearing exhaustion. Flat OI during a rally/decline = the supply of new losers has stopped growing — a caution flag that gains are largely made.

## 8. Time, Multiple Timeframes, and Trading Horizons

Elder treats time as a dimension most traders ignore, to their detriment, since crowds (unlike disciplined individuals) act as if they have unlimited time.

**Indicator Seasons** (applying an agricultural metaphor to any oscillator via its slope + position relative to centerline):
| Indicator Slope | Position vs. Centerline | Season | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rising | Below | Spring | Best time to go long |
| Rising | Above | Summer | Start taking profits on longs |
| Falling | Above | Autumn | Best time to go short |
| Falling | Below | Winter | Start covering shorts |

Spring/Autumn are emotionally the hardest times to act (against the recent trend memory) but offer the best risk/reward; Summer/Winter are emotionally easy (everyone agrees) but offer worse risk/reward since stops must sit farther away.

**The Factor of Five**: neighboring timeframes relate by roughly a factor of 5 (≈4.5 weeks/month, 5 days/week, ~5-6 hours/day, and so on down to intraday bars). This underlies **Triple Screen** (Section 10): pick your favored ("intermediate") timeframe, then go one order of magnitude longer for strategic bias, and optionally one order shorter for tactical entries.

**Market time runs slower than personal time** — beginners are typically late to trends (recognizing them only once well underway); experienced analysts often err the opposite way, entering reversals too early because they can see them coming from a distance. Antidotes: trade smaller size while waiting for confirmation, and always cross-check the longer timeframe before acting on the shorter one.

**Trading Timeframes** — three horizons with distinct risk/reward profiles:
- **Long-term investing/position trading** (months–years): low day-to-day attention, big potential gains, but can carry severe (50%+) drawdowns that are hard to psychologically tolerate even when the underlying thesis is intact.
- **Swing trading** (days–weeks): Elder's recommended starting point for beginners/intermediates — plenty of opportunities to learn fast, tighter risk control than investing, more thinking time than day-trading.
- **Day-trading** (minutes–hours): demands instant reflexes, magnifies the cost of commissions/slippage relative to the smaller price target captured, and empirically has a low success rate (cited: ~16% of day traders profitable after 6 months per a 2000 Massachusetts regulatory study). Elder recommends mastering swing trading first; the technique transfers, only the speed changes.

## 9. General Market Indicators

Broad-market tools used to gauge overall bullish/bearish leadership, useful for index trading and as a backdrop for individual stock decisions (general market trend drives roughly half of any single stock's movement).

### New High–New Low Index (NH-NL) — Elder's favorite leading indicator
NH-NL = New Highs − New Lows (number of stocks hitting a 52-week high minus number hitting a 52-week low, that day). Analogy: new-high stocks are the "officers" leading an attack; new-low stocks are officers deserting. Where officers lead, the rest of the market (soldiers) tends to follow.
- **Weekly NH-NL** built as a 5-day moving total. Two extreme levels matter most: a drop below **−4,000** followed by a rally back above it is a rare, powerful buy signal Elder calls a **"Spike"** (source of the SpikeTrade name) — worked in every instance studied except one, during the 2008 crash. A rise above **+2,500** confirms genuine bull markets (never seen in bear-market rallies).
- **20-day and 65-day NH-NL** (shorter look-back windows) give more sensitive short-term signals; a drop below −500 on the 20-day window followed by a rally above it is called a **"Spike Bounce"** — a shorter-term tradable buy signal.
- Divergences between NH-NL and the broad index (index makes a new high/low, NH-NL doesn't) mark likely reversals, following the same divergence logic as MACD-Histogram.

### Stocks Above Their 50-Day Moving Average
Percentage of all exchange-listed stocks trading above their own 50-day MA, plotted 0–100%. Since a moving average is the "average consensus of value," trading above it = above-average bullishness market-wide. Practically bottoms near 25% and tops near 75% (rarely near true 0/100 extremes); signals come from **reversals near those reference levels**, not from the level itself. Tops in this indicator tend to be broad (greed is slow-building); bottoms tend to be sharp (fear is fast).

### Advance/Decline Line
Cumulative total of (advancing stocks − declining stocks) each day. Confirms whether a rally/decline has broad participation ("soldiers following the officers") — new highs in the A/D line alongside new highs in price mean a durable, broadly-supported move; divergence warns of narrowing participation and an aging trend.

### Legacy/decayed indicators (Elder's caution)
TRIN, TICK, the Member/Specialist Short Sale Ratios, and odd-lot statistics were once useful but have been rendered unreliable by structural market changes (volume growth, options proliferation, changed investor behavior) — a reminder that **no indicator is permanent**; test relevance against current market structure rather than trusting an indicator's historical reputation.

## 10. The Triple Screen Trading System

Elder's signature framework (first published 1986), designed to combine trend-following indicators (reliable in trends, whipsaw-prone in ranges) with oscillators (reliable in ranges, dangerously premature in trends) so their respective weaknesses cancel out.

**Setup**: pick your favorite ("intermediate") timeframe. The **long-term** timeframe is one order of magnitude longer (the Factor of Five, Section 8); the **short-term** timeframe is one order shorter.

**Screen One — Market Tide (the strategic decision)**: analyze the long-term chart *first*, before even looking at the intermediate chart (to avoid being biased by it). Use a trend-following tool (originally weekly MACD-Histogram slope; later the slope of a weekly EMA; more recently the Impulse System, Section 11) to decide: is the long-term trend up, down, or flat? This screen is a **censor** — it doesn't tell you what to do, only what you're prohibited from doing. An up long-term trend permits only buying or standing aside; a down trend permits only shorting or standing aside.

**Screen Two — Market Wave (the tactical wave against the tide)**: apply an oscillator (Elder favors 2-day EMA of Force Index; RSI, Elder-ray, or Stochastic also work) to the intermediate/shorter chart to find a countertrend dip (in an uptrend) or rally (in a downtrend) as an entry opportunity — buying dips within an uptrend, shorting rallies within a downtrend. Never take the oscillator's signal against the Screen One trend.

**Screen Three — Entry technique**: original method was to buy a breakout above the prior day's high (or short a breakdown below the prior low), but this often produced overly wide stops. Elder's preferred alternative for traders without live intraday data: **"average EMA penetration"** — measure how far, on average, recent pullbacks have dipped below the fast EMA during the current trend, then place a buy order that same average distance below tomorrow's projected EMA level (mirror logic for shorts). This buys "at a bargain" during a normal dip rather than chasing a breakout.

**Summary table**:
| Weekly Trend | Daily Trend | Action | Order |
|---|---|---|---|
| Up | Up | Stand aside | None |
| Up | Down | Go long | EMA penetration or upside breakout |
| Down | Down | Stand aside | None |
| Down | Up | Go short | EMA penetration or downside breakout |

**Stops and targets**: set profit targets on the long-term chart (e.g., the weekly value zone), but place stops on the intermediate chart — aim for the bigger picture while controlling risk tightly, day to day.

**Day-trading application**: the same three-screen logic scales down (e.g., a 30-minute chart for strategic bias, a 5-minute chart for tactical entries; or a 39-minute/8-minute pair that neatly divides the 390-minute U.S. trading day).

## 11. The Impulse System and Channel Trading Systems

### The Impulse System (Elder's own invention)
Combines two ingredients into a single color-coded censorship rule:
- **Inertia** = slope of the fast EMA (rising = bullish inertia, falling = bearish).
- **Power** = slope of MACD-Histogram (rising = bullish power push, falling = bearish), regardless of whether it's above or below zero.

Coloring logic:
- EMA rising **and** MACD-H rising → **Green** (bullish): shorting prohibited; buying or standing aside allowed.
- EMA falling **and** MACD-H falling → **Red** (bearish): buying prohibited; shorting or standing aside allowed.
- EMA and MACD-H disagree → **Blue** (neutral): nothing prohibited.

Like Triple Screen, this is explicitly **a censor, not an autopilot** — early attempts to trade it mechanically (buy every green, short every red) got whipsawed badly in ranges. Its real value: it tells you what you may *not* do. Best entries occur immediately after a color changes away from the color that opposes your intended direction (e.g., buy right after red disappears, not while still green and "everyone already knows"). Applied across two timeframes (e.g., weekly and daily), Elder waits until *both* stop opposing his direction before entering, and exits a short-term momentum trade as soon as either timeframe's color turns against the trade — enter cautiously, exit fast, the opposite of what beginners instinctively do.

### Channel Trading Systems
Built on Benoit Mandelbrot's finding (originally applied to cotton prices) that **"prices oscillate above and below value."** Two channel-construction methods:
- **Symmetrical channels**: two lines parallel to the slower of a dual-EMA pair, at a percentage offset (e.g., Upper = EMA + coefficient×EMA) tuned so the channel contains ~95% of the last ~100 bars. Weekly channel coefficients run roughly double daily ones; cheaper stocks need wider coefficients than expensive ones.
- **Standard deviation channels (Bollinger Bands)**: width expands/contracts with volatility (calculated via the standard deviation of price around a 21-day EMA); most useful for options traders, since option pricing is driven by volatility swings — narrow bands flag options as cheap (buy), wide bands flag them as rich (sell/write). For stock/futures trading, Elder prefers fixed-percentage channels since they provide a stable, non-moving profit target.

**Trading rule set** (Gerald Appel, extended by Elder): in a flat channel, buy near the bottom, sell near the top. When a rising channel is punctured on the upside, expect a pullback to the EMA as a fresh buying opportunity (mirror for falling channels/downside punctures). Best signals combine channel-extreme touches with a **divergence** in an oscillator (e.g., MACD-Histogram) at that same extreme — the two independent confirmations reinforcing each other is a recurring theme throughout the book (never rely on one indicator alone).

## 12. Trading Vehicles: Comparative Risk Profile

Elder frames vehicle choice as a deliberate risk decision, not a preference. Two universal screening criteria: **liquidity** (average volume — protects against slippage on exit) and **volatility/beta** (a stock's move relative to a benchmark index; beta 1 = matches the S&P, beta 2 = double the swing) — beginners should favor high liquidity, low-to-moderate beta.

- **Stocks**: ownership stakes; driven by earnings in the long run but sentiment-driven ("Mr. Market") in the short-to-medium run, per Buffett's manic-depressive partner analogy. Elder recommends narrowing a "watch list" to a small, well-known pool rather than scanning thousands.
- **ETFs**: convenient but structurally disadvantaged for retail buyers — a two-tier market (institutional "authorized participants" trade wholesale; retail trades secondary/retail), tracking-error "haircuts" (Elder cites a Morgan Stanley study: ETFs missed targets by ~1.25% in 2009, double 2008's miss), and some ETFs (commodity, volatility, country, leveraged) can diverge wildly from their underlying index over time even while correctly tracking it intraday.
- **Options**: a call is a bet on a rise, a put on a fall; price = intrinsic value (in-the-money amount) + time value (hope). **Buying options requires being right on three things at once** — direction, magnitude, and speed — making option buyers a structurally losing group over time (only ~1-in-3 odds even before costs); **writing (selling) options** has the mathematical edge, since time decay favors the seller, but is capital-intensive and requires strict discipline (buy back after ~50-80% of premium is captured; use mental stops on both the underlying and the option; maintain a self-insurance reserve against rare catastrophic moves). Buying deep out-of-the-money, near-expiration puts is the one buyer-side strategy Elder endorses — cheap "lottery ticket" bets sized for an occasional 10x gain to offset a string of small losses, only when expecting a genuine waterfall decline.
- **CFDs**: contracts for difference, illegal in the U.S., legal in many other countries; issuer typically takes the counter-side of the client's trade and controls pricing — a structural conflict of interest regulators (e.g., Australia's ASIC) have flagged as riskier than casino gambling.
- **Futures**: binding contracts (unlike options) for both buyer and seller; long and short open interest are always exactly equal. Advantages: low correlation across different futures markets (unlike stocks, which move as a herd), interest earned (not paid) on margin. Danger: extreme leverage (3-5% margin) magnifies small price wiggles into account-destroying moves for the undisciplined — ~9 of 10 new futures traders are said to fail within months, driven by poor risk sizing, not the instrument itself. Key structural concepts: **hedging** (producers/consumers offset price risk, gaining a competitive edge over unhedged rivals), **contango vs. inversion** (inverted markets — nearby months pricier than distant ones — signal a real, acute supply shortage and are one of the strongest bullish tells in commodities), and **spread trading** (betting on the normalization of a price relationship between related contracts, generally safer/slower than directional bets).
- **Forex (retail)**: Elder is strongly negative on retail forex "shops," which frequently "bucket" client orders (never executing them in a real market) and structurally profit when the client loses; leverage offered (100:1, even 400:1) is described as "homicidal." His recommendation: trade **currency futures** instead of retail forex CFDs, since futures brokers execute as genuine agents rather than as the house.

## 13. Risk Management: The Iron Triangle, the 2% Rule, and the 6% Rule

Central premise: even a good system with positive mathematical expectation will produce randomness and losing streaks in the short run. **Risk management is what lets you survive long enough for a positive-expectation system to pay off** — money management can rescue a good system's rough patches but cannot rescue a system with a negative expectation.

**Businessman's risk vs. a loss**: professionals accept a capped, planned "businessman's risk" per trade (a normal cost of doing business); anything beyond that predefined cap is a genuine loss that damages survival. The line between the two is the trader's own risk-management rule, and it must never be moved once a trade is on ("giving a losing trade more room" is wishful thinking, not strategy).

### The 2% Rule (protection from "shark bites" — single catastrophic losses)
**Never risk more than 2% of account equity on any single trade.** This is not the trade's size — it is the dollar amount between entry and stop, multiplied by position size.

**The Iron Triangle of Risk Control** (three-step sizing formula):
1. **A** = maximum dollar risk for this trade (≤ 2% of equity).
2. **B** = dollar distance from planned entry to planned stop (risk per share/contract).
3. **C** = A ÷ B = maximum number of shares/contracts you may trade (not obligated to trade the max, just capped at it).

Recalculate your 2% ceiling monthly against current equity (it rises after a good month, falls after a bad one) — this automatically scales position size with performance. Applies per account if you run separate stock/futures/options accounts. Elder notes: professionals often self-impose limits well *below* 2% (0.5–1%); it's beginners who tend to argue the number should be higher.

### The 6% Rule (protection from "piranha bites" — a cumulative string of losses)
**Stop opening any new trades for the rest of the calendar month once the sum of (this month's realized losses + total risk in currently open trades) reaches 6% of the account equity you started the month with.**

Mechanics: add up realized losses this month, add the open risk (entry-to-current-stop distance × size) on every open position; once open risk is retired (e.g., a stop is moved to breakeven), it no longer counts against the 6% ceiling, freeing "available risk" for a new trade. This reframes the trading question from "do I have money for this trade?" to **"do I have available risk for this trade?"** — a subtle but important shift.

At 2% per trade, the 6% ceiling naturally caps you at roughly 3 simultaneous open-risk positions; tightening the per-trade risk below 2% (e.g., to 1%) allows more simultaneous positions within the same 6% ceiling.

### Recovering from a Drawdown
The core lesson (illustrated via a day-trading firm case study): higher position size under stress degrades performance, not the reverse — a trader making money at 100 shares can lose money at 500 shares of the *same signals*, because risk itself impairs execution (the "walking a plank on the ground vs. between two rooftops" analogy — same physical task, radically different psychological load). Elder's prescribed comeback protocol: drop to a small size (e.g., 100 shares), require two consecutive profitable weeks before stepping up one increment, and drop back a full step immediately after any losing week. Institutional traders outperform private ones largely because a manager enforces exactly this kind of externally imposed discipline (per-trade risk caps, monthly drawdown limits) — the individual trader's central task is to become their own manager.

## 14. Practical Trade Management and Record-Keeping Discipline

### Profit Targets — "Enough" as the Power Word
Every planned trade needs three numbers written down before entry: **entry, target, stop** — "a trade without these three numbers is a gamble, not a trade." Reward should generally be at least ~2:1 versus risk. Realistic, modest targets (moving averages, channel lines, prior support/resistance) reliably outperform "shooting for the moon" — Elder frames this explicitly as rabbit-hunting (frequent, reliable, modest gains) beating elephant-hunting (rare, uncertain, huge gains) as a business model.

### Setting Stops
A trade without a stop is a gamble, full stop. Key principles for placement (from *The New Sell & Sell Short*, summarized here):
- **Place stops outside the zone of "market noise,"** not so tight that ordinary fluctuation triggers them, not so loose that they offer no real protection. **SafeZone stops**: measure the average penetration below (in an uptrend) or above (in a downtrend) a reference EMA over a look-back window, then place the stop at a multiple (2x+) of that average penetration.
- **Avoid obvious stop levels** (just below a visible recent low) since the crowd clusters stops there, creating a "target-rich environment" for stop-running. **Nic's Stop** (Nic Grove): place the stop just beyond the *second*-lowest (or second-highest) recent low/high rather than the most obvious extreme — if price is sliding toward a second low, it is likely headed for the first (most obvious) one anyway, so exiting at the second saves money and slippage versus waiting to be stopped out at the crowded level.
- Avoid round numbers for stop placement (crowds cluster there too).
- **ATR stops** (Kerry Lovvorn): place/trail a stop 1-2 ATRs from the relevant extreme; a close-only (not intraday) trigger reduces whipsaws from meaningless intraday spikes.
- **Never loosen a stop against your position** — only ever move stops in the direction that reduces risk (toward breakeven and beyond as a trade moves favorably), never to "give a trade more room."
- **"Cuffing the trade"**: move the stop to breakeven once open profit reaches a predefined checkpoint (e.g., ~30% of target), then progressively lock in a defined fraction (e.g., a third) of growing open profit — the reward-to-risk ratio deteriorates as a trade matures (upside shrinks, stop distance often grows), so risk must be actively trimmed to compensate.
- **Catastrophic ("hard") stops**: even discretionary/professional traders who mostly use mental ("soft") stops should always have one actual, GTC hard stop placed at a level the market "absolutely should not reach" — insurance against a single bad decision metastasizing into a career-ending loss.

### Grading Trades Objectively
Elder proposes three formulaic grades, scored on every closed trade to build a feedback loop independent of dollar P&L (which is a poor quality measure on its own — a "lucky" bad trade and a "good" trade that lost money both need honest grading):
- **Buy grade** = (bar's high − buy price) ÷ (bar's high − low). Higher = bought closer to the low.
- **Sell grade** = (sell price − bar's low) ÷ (bar's high − low). Higher = sold closer to the high.
- **Trade grade** = (sell price − buy price) ÷ (channel's high − channel's low, measured at entry). Elder's benchmark: capturing **30%+ of the channel height = an "A-trade."** This reframes trade quality around "what was realistically available" rather than raw profit.

**"Is this an A-trade?"** — a **Trade Apgar** (named after Dr. Virginia Apgar's newborn health score) is a personal, 5-question, 0/1/2-point checklist tailored to one's own specific strategy (Elder's own version scores weekly Impulse color, daily Impulse color, price-vs-value position, presence of a false breakout, and "perfection" of the pattern in one or both timeframes). A minimum total score (Elder uses 7, with no single zero) gates whether a setup is even worked up into a full trade plan — the goal is filtering out marginal, borderline trades before they consume capital or attention, exactly as Apgar's score filters which newborns need intervention.

### Scanning
Scanning must follow — never precede — a clearly defined strategy ("if you don't have a system, what exactly will you scan for?"). A scan is a time-saving filter that surfaces *candidates*, not a magic "find the trade" tool; loosening/tightening scan parameters trades off list size against precision. Elder favors semi-automatic scans (e.g., flagging setups that are only *starting* to resemble a divergence, since true divergence patterns are visually/analog patterns that are hard for pure digital rules to catch reliably) plus negative filters (excluding illiquid names) to keep the review workload manageable — he personally narrows to single digits of live candidates per week, not dozens.

### Daily Homework and Self-Readiness Checks
A repeatable pre-market routine (checking overseas markets, economic calendar, key currencies/commodities, NH-NL, VIX, Force Index, etc.) builds the same kind of "groove" a morning routine builds for daily life — freeing mental energy for strategic judgment rather than routine data-gathering. Paired with a short (5-question, 0/1/2 scored) **personal readiness self-test** (physical state, yesterday's result, prep completed, mood, schedule load) that gates whether/how aggressively to trade that day — a very low or very high score are both flagged as reasons for caution (a perfect score risks overconfidence going into a change of luck).

### The Trade Journal
Elder identifies good record-keeping as the thread tying together psychology, market analysis, and risk management — "show me a trader with good records, and I'll show you a good trader." Three concrete benefits, each on a different time horizon:
1. **Immediate** — writing plans down (entry/target/stop, position size) imposes structure and catches impulsive decisions before they're made.
2. **Medium-term (1-2 months later)** — revisiting a closed trade with the benefit of hindsight (now visible mid-chart rather than at the "hard right edge") turns you into your own teacher; Elder does this systematically, roughly two months after each close, annotating what he'd do differently.
3. **Long-term** — segmenting equity curves by strategy, direction (long/short), and exit reason (target hit, stop hit, "couldn't stand the pain," etc.) reveals systematic strengths/weaknesses no single trade or aggregate P&L number would show (e.g., discovering stops were consistently too tight, or that short-term trades were good but bigger trends were repeatedly missed).

**Paper trading vs. real trading**: paper trading only has one legitimate use — testing whether you have the discipline to follow a system daily for months without skipping. It systematically understates real difficulty (perfect fills, no emotional stakes, and a nervous trader unconsciously self-selects the "easy-looking" trades on paper while the harder, more valuable trades get skipped) — there is no substitute for small real-money trades once basic discipline is established.
