# $100M Money Models — Framework Reference

*Pure framework reference distilled from the book. No commentary, no application notes — just the frameworks, models, and mental tools as the author presents them.*

*Source: $100M Money Models by Alex Hormozi*

---

## 1. What Is A Money Model — Core Definition And Stakes

A Money Model is a sequence of offers. Different offers solve different problems, so winning means figuring out what to offer *next*. Making one offer works better than making none. Making more offers works better than making one. Combining offers in a sequence is what makes a Money Model.

**The central answer the whole book is built on:** you build wealth by making more money from customers than it costs to get them. A good Money Model lets you get paid *more money, faster* — that's the whole game.

**Bad Money Models kill businesses.** It costs many businesses more to get somebody to buy a thing than they make in profit off the thing. The failure sequence: spend money to get customers → realize at month-end you spent more than you made → cut back on advertising → get fewer customers than you can handle because you can't afford them → cut advertising altogether → float the business with personal cash, loans, credit, and pray for profit → sell equity just to keep the lights on → wait months or years to make the money back, if ever → fall further behind → lose it all.

In traditional business, the slow drip of profit from many customers eventually pays for a single new customer — which starves the business of cash and means you can only get lots of customers through advertising *if you already have lots of customers*. If spending $100 to acquire a customer nets $500 in profit but takes two years to collect, that's a great business only if you already have cash reserves to survive the wait. Two options exist when the cash-back cycle is slow: wait and pray you don't run out of money, or get paid fast and grow as much as you want. **A good Money Model is the second option.**

**Good Money Models compound geometrically, not linearly.** If you make your customers twice as valuable, get twice as many of them, and get them at twice the speed, the business grows 8x faster (2×2×2). Triple all three levers and the business grows 27x faster (3×3×3). Big, fast, profitable growth comes from stacking a small number of changes across these three dimensions — not from one heroic change.

**The book's single teaching goal:** how to build a $100M Money Model — one so profitable that you make so much money in the first thirty days that the cost of acquiring more customers is never a problem again. With enough customers, the constraint moves from cash to everything else in the business (delivery, ops, team) — a different problem, but a much better one to have.

## 2. The Four Offer Types — Framework Overview And Operating Rules

Every Money Model combines four offer types. Each improves the Money Model differently; together they make the business close to unstoppable.

- **Attraction Offers** turn strangers into customers.
- **Upsell Offers** get people to spend more cash.
- **Downsell Offers** get people to say yes when they would have said no.
- **Continuity Offers** keep people buying.

**Why Attraction comes first:** most businesses are cash-constrained — they spend too much to get customers and make too little from them. Since cash is what buys more customers, an Attraction Offer is always the first thing to solve.

**Chapter structure used throughout the book** (useful as a mental checklist for building any offer): a doodle/visual, the origin story, a description of the mechanism, examples across business types (B2C, B2B, physical product, digital product, service), important notes and tactics, and a summary of key points.

**Seven non-negotiable rules stated before presenting any offer:**

1. **All businesses have Money Models — it's what makes a business a business.** Replace "this won't work for my business" with "how will I make this work for my business?" Every offer type works somewhere; it takes creativity to find the fit.
2. **Some Money Models work better in some businesses than others.** Copying exactly what another business does will disappoint. Design your own version.
3. **If a customer asks for a refund, give it back.** Avoid the headache. If you made a mistake, fix it. Treat customers well and spend future resources getting better customers instead of fighting this one.
4. **Hard selling is for weak products.** If someone doesn't want something, that's okay — don't convince someone against their will. Make offers available at the moment the customer has the problem, and you'll beat the competition. If they say no, find someone who says yes. It's a numbers game.
5. **Obey the law.** These plays were learned across different situations, platforms, times, and jurisdictions. Advertising law changes constantly and tends to tighten, especially around the word "free." Check with lawyers before running any specific offer.
6. **Be transparent.** State the facts. If the facts aren't compelling, change the underlying reality to make them compelling, or reframe honestly — never lie. A damaged reputation, unlike debt, cannot be discharged.
7. **Any offer can be used on its own, at any time, in any order.** A business works as long as it's profitable, and most of the offers in the book can hit that bar standalone. Used in the right sequence and at the right time, they compound into a $100M Money Model.

**Cross-cutting summary for Upsells** (previewed before the individual Upsell chapters): Upsells tend to offer *more* of what the customer just got (quantity), *better* versions of it (quality), or *new/complementary* stuff (variety). Four upsell offers are covered: The Classic Upsell, Menu Upsells, Anchor Upsells, and Rollover Upsells.

## 3. Attraction Offer: Win Your Money Back

**Description.** You set a goal for the customer and tell them how to reach it. If they reach it, they qualify to get their money back — as cash or store credit. This was the first Grand Slam Offer taught at Gym Launch and has generated over $1 billion in sales industry-wide.

There are three qualifying structures:

- **Results.** The customer wins if they hit a defined result (make $X/month, get Y customers, lose Z lbs) regardless of how they get there — they bet on their own ability to reach the goal.
- **Actions.** The customer wins if they complete required actions (attend sessions/calls, log progress, take pictures, do homework) regardless of the result — they bet on their ability to follow directions.
- **Actions and Results.** The customer must do both. Useful because people who want a goal often lack the skill to reach it alone; giving them a path and a goal together gives them a real chance.

**Bottom line:** customers put money down. If they do the required stuff, get the result, or both, they get it back as cash or store credit.

**How to build the qualifying criteria** — good criteria have three characteristics:

- **Easy to track.** Train customers on exactly what they need to do. Bonus points if tracking already happens automatically (phones track steps, word processors track word count, cameras date photos).
- **Gets customers results.** Realistic criteria work fine — mirror what your best customers already do to get the best results, and make everyone do it.
- **Advertises the business.** Build in posting about participation, tagging on social media, referring, or leaving reviews as part of the criteria.

**Important notes:**

- Works with new, current, and previous customers — new customers get the steepest possible discount (100%); current customers get mixed into the incentive; previous customers get pulled back with a bigger incentive.
- Works especially well for anything people start and quit: new businesses, new skills, weight loss, fitness, beauty regimes, self-care, time/mental-health management.
- The offer makes money *because* many people won't qualify even with realistic conditions, and those who do qualify typically stay as customers — if you have another offer ready for them (see Upsells). Refund rate benchmark from thousands of gyms: about 10% of customers ask for their money back.
- Only run this offer if you're genuinely okay giving money back. If you can't stomach roughly a 10% refund rate, don't do it.
- Store credit converts at the same rate as cash refunds in testing — offer store credit if you'd rather not give cash, and pair with an unconditional satisfaction guarantee if advertising it as "free" (check with legal counsel).
- Never fight a refund request — "I want it less than they do" if someone doesn't want you to have their money.
- Apply won credit toward a longer duration or bulk package rather than handing it over up front. Example: a $200/month service, customer wins $600 credit, apply it as $50/month off for 12 months rather than 3 free months up front — keeps them engaged with "skin in the game." (Full mechanics in the Rollover Upsell chapter.)
- Build required meetings into the money-back criteria — they double as upsell opportunities. Gym example: Nutrition Orientation ("before pictures") → supplement offer; Progress Check-in → membership offer; Transformation Feedback ("after pictures") → membership offer again, with a prepay discount if they bought at the last meeting.
- **Make everyone a winner in private.** Around the halfway point, make the next offer as if they already won ("I'll credit this program toward the next one whether you hit the short-term goal or not") — this lowers anxiety about failing and increases retention. Even for people who fail the challenge, you can credit the deposit toward staying long-term and frame it as them having won the bigger victory of "finishing what you started."

**Summary points.** Win Your Money Back works because it generates upfront cash, gets more yeses by lowering perceived risk, produces real results, creates long-term customers, and gets customers advertising the offer for you. The real money comes from people who succeed *and* have something else to buy — not from people who fail.

## 4. Attraction Offer: Giveaways

**Description.** Advertise a chance to win a big prize (a "Grand Prize") in exchange for contact information and other eligibility actions. After picking a winner, offer everyone else the same core offer at a discount (the "promotional offer"). Giveaways also go by "scholarship," "sweepstakes," or "raffle."

**Steps:**

1. **Pick a Grand Prize** — make it the exact thing you want everyone to eventually buy, and assign it a real monetary value to serve as a price anchor.
2. **Pick your promotional offer** — the "partial scholarship" equivalent: your core offer enhanced with a discount or bonus, or minorly altered from the Grand Prize to ethically justify the price cut. Bigger anchor value → more compelling discount.
3. **Ask for contact information and eligibility criteria** — permission to follow up, plus fit-screening questions ("Do you own a vet clinic?", "Why should you be selected?").
4. **Pick qualifying actions** — things entrants do to qualify (attend a call/event, make a post, join a group) that also demonstrate interest level and spread the giveaway further.
5. **Put the giveaway on a deadline** — three to seven days is the recommended window. Update entrants daily across channels with time remaining plus value reinforcement and social proof.
6. **Announce the Grand Prize winner publicly, then privately contact everyone else** who qualifies for the promotional offer — frame it as a "participation trophy," and put a second deadline (about seven days) on redeeming it.

**Cost-to-value framing:** a Grand Prize with $5,000 advertised value, sold to non-winners at $1,800 against a $2,000 retail price, is only a 10% discount off retail — but a 64% "cost-to-value" difference against the $5,000 anchor. Rule of thumb: make the core-offer discount equal to 10–30% of gross margin.

**Important notes:**

- Consult legal counsel on giveaway structure. Non-negotiable baseline: someone actually has to win the Grand Prize, rules must be clear, and it must be clear more than one person can win a prize.
- Eligibility questions ("Why should we pick you? Why this program? Why now?") double as sales ammunition — the more specific the value pitch to that person, the more likely they buy the core offer. Trade-off: harder entry = fewer but more qualified entrants.
- If a giveaway flops, the Grand Prize wasn't grand enough. A portfolio company's giveaway of event tickets barely got interest; a $50,000 equipment bundle plus a year of the core product "crushed."
- **Give away two prizes for more referred leads:** tell entrants that if someone they refer wins, the referrer wins too. This creates unlimited entries via referral and gives referrers a stake in their referrals' success (keeping referral quality high).
- Have downsells ready for people who won't buy the promotional offer even discounted — offer the same percentage discount on a different product that fits them.
- For recurring-revenue businesses, apply the discount over the longest period the customer will agree to, then auto-bill at normal rates after.

**Summary points.** Giveaways ask an audience to apply for something high-value for free; many enter, one wins, the rest qualify for a discount on the core offer. Advertise for about seven days or until lead volume exceeds what you can call in seven days, whichever comes first. Book appointments with everyone who qualifies for the promotional offer.

## 5. Attraction Offer: Decoy Offer

**Description.** Advertise something free or discounted (the decoy). When leads engage to learn more, present a more valuable premium offer side by side with it. The premium option carries more features, benefits, bonuses, and guarantees. Every lead closes one way or another: those who take the decoy stay warm for an upgrade later; those who take the premium buy immediately either way, the business gets cheap, profitable new customers.

**Steps:**

1. Advertise a lesser, smaller, or simpler version of the premium offer as the decoy.
2. When leads engage, present both options side by side.

**Important notes:**

- **Build the decoy by removing, not degrading:** fewer components, older models, less personalization, and no guarantees. The Attraction Offer's only job is to get engagement — nothing more.
- **Advertise benefits, not features.** Sell the dream outcome ("a transformation in 21 days," not "workouts and meal plans"). Product specifics belong in the sales presentation, not the ad.
- **Four equivalent ways to advertise the same discount** (all describing paying $900 for a $1,200/year offer): percentage off (25% off), absolute amount ($300 off), free portion (3 months free), or total package framing (one year for $900, normally $1,200). Test which converts best in your market.
- **Make the contrast huge.** The bigger the gap between decoy and premium, the more people take the premium.
- **Discount offers get higher show-up rates than free offers.** Free offers generate more leads; discount offers generate fewer but better-attended appointments — useful when no-shows are expensive (doctors, lawyers, dentists).
- **Present the premium offer first whenever possible.** If a lead specifically asks for the decoy, or you're legally obligated to present it, ask: *"Are you here for free stuff or lasting results?"* Most say "results" — skip straight to premium. If they say "free stuff," present the decoy, immediately contrast it with premium, then ask *"which do you think will get you to your goal faster?"*
- **Sell the premium with genuine enthusiasm** and operate from an assumed close — talk as if acceptance is already decided, almost bored by how routinely people buy.
- **Optional surprise benefit:** if someone takes the decoy, surprise them with a low/zero-cost feature normally reserved for premium — builds goodwill and raises the odds of a later upsell.

**Summary points.** Decoy Offers work because leads self-sort into premium-now or decoy-now-upsell-later, and either way the business converts them. Strip the decoy down as far as reasonable; build the premium up as far as reasonable; the size of the gap between them is what drives conversion.

## 6. Attraction Offer: Buy X Get Y Free

**Description.** Customers get free stuff in exchange for buying paid stuff — structured as "Buy X, Get Y Free" (e.g., buy 1 shirt, get 2 free; buy 3 months, get 3 free). This is a pricing reframe: "Buy 1 Get 2 Free" is mathematically identical to selling 3 units, but customers perceive the free units as more valuable than an equivalent straight discount.

**Important notes and mechanics:**

- Works best for things it makes sense to buy more of, or get longer access to (retail goods, subscription terms).
- Bigger free-to-paid ratios (more free than paid) are stronger offers than smaller ones.
- Different free items can be paired with different paid items to customize the offer.
- Some versions discount unit price directly — buying more costs less per unit than buying the same total quantity one at a time.
- **It lengthens customer tenure.** If normal customers stay 3 months, a "Buy 2 Get 2 Free" structure keeps them for 4 months (or however the offer is set) — more months means more chances to make further offers.
- **If a customer only buys once, make that one purchase as large as possible.** (Origin example: a boot factory serving one-time tourist buyers structured the offer to maximize the size of that single transaction, since there would be no second purchase to expand on.)
- If used to generate fast cash from existing recurring customers, cap how many you sell so cash flow doesn't get overextended.
- Customers who prepay long durations are the customers most likely to buy again — keep selling to them.

## 7. Attraction Offer: Pay Less Now Or Pay More Later

**Description.** Give the customer a choice: pay full price later, or pay a discounted price now. This removes essentially all risk from the customer's decision, since the "pay later" branch functions like a satisfaction guarantee combined with a delayed payment. Nearly anyone will agree to "pay later," and once they've agreed to that, a large discount plus valuable bonuses can convert many of them into "pay now" instead.

**Mechanics:** the "pay later" option gets the card on file. If the customer engages and likes the product, "pay now" is then offered as a follow-up with a 20–50% discount and larger bonuses — made easy to accept because the card is already on file. Whichever branch they choose, the business has a customer and, likely, some profit — so a further offer (see Upsells) should be ready.

**Important notes:**

- **Promise a clear yes/no result** that can be delivered inside the stated time frame (e.g., have the customer rate pain 1–10 before and after). Vague promises invite cancellations.
- **Make the satisfaction guarantee conditional**, not unconditional — the customer must meet stated participation criteria (attendance, submitting data, showing up) to qualify for the "no charge" outcome. This also filters out people who never actually tried the product.
- **Tune the two branches against each other:** if too many people take "pay later," discount "pay now" harder or add better bonuses (or both); if too many take "pay now," do the reverse.
- **If more than 10% of "pay later" customers cancel their charge**, you promised too much, set the guarantee bar too low, or priced too high. Some cancellations are a normal cost of doing business — factor it in.
- Works for recurring-revenue businesses too: offer a higher ongoing rate starting in 30 days, or a lower rate locked in for good if they pay now.

**Summary points.** The "pay later" branch pairs delayed payment with a conditional guarantee (clear, trackable, ideally value-aligned criteria). The "pay now" branch offers a 20–50% discount plus bonuses instead of the guarantee, presented only after "pay later" has been accepted.

## 8. Upsell Offers — Overview

Upsells are whatever is offered *next*, the moment a new problem becomes visible — typically more of what the customer just got, a better version of it, or something new and complementary. Four upsell types are covered: The Classic Upsell, Menu Upsells, Anchor Upsells, and Rollover Upsells. All four are described as "brutally effective" and must be used ethically.

---

## 9. Upsell Offer: The Classic Upsell

**Description.** Offer the solution to the customer's *next* problem the moment they become aware of it. This relies on knowing more about the customer's problem than they do — which a business owner should. The core offer solves one problem and creates another; the upsell immediately solves the new one, giving it a "you can't have X without Y" structure (a car needs insurance, gas, a late checkout; a bike needs a helmet, lights, puncture-resistant tires).

**Bottom line:** if a problem appears and you can solve it immediately for money, do it.

**Important notes:**

- **Actually do it.** Businesses that sell only one thing "barely have a business — they have a front end." Adding even one real upsell has driven 5x growth for businesses that previously had none.
- **Offer the more profitable upsell first** when there's a choice between two.
- **Get them to "say no to say yes."** Ask *"You don't want anything [else] do you?"* — people trained to answer "no" to that phrasing are actually saying yes to the upsell.
- **Surprise and delight with saved-up bonuses.** If a customer says yes before all planned bonuses are mentioned, give them all of the bonuses anyway — it surprises them and guarantees consistent treatment across customers.
- **Sell more during the "hyper buying cycle."** Buyers who've just committed to something new (a wedding, a new hobby, a baby, a move) enter a short window of maximum spending enthusiasm — lean into upsells during it rather than holding back.
- **Use free bonuses to create the problem the upsell solves.** A bonus that costs materials/labor (like free earmuffs with paid coat storage) can itself generate the next paid need (storage for the earmuffs).
- **Speed increases perceived value.** A $10,000 thing delivered later is worth less than the same thing delivered now — put upsells in the customer's hands as fast as possible, ideally before they've even said yes (it's harder to give something back than to decline it).
- **Bundle and name upsell packages** ("Fastest Results" bundle, "Transformation Package," "Minimum Package") so one ask sells many items at once; individual items can later be "peeled" out as downsells.
- **Integrate the next thing you want to sell into the delivery of the first thing** (optional supplement suggestions inside a meal plan; optional software suggestions inside sales training) so more customers naturally ask about it.
- **BAMFAM — Book A Meeting From A Meeting.** End every appointment by scheduling the next one; more upsell touchpoints mean more upsells, and a customer should know the next time they'll see you, and why, before they leave.
- **Upsell as many times as makes sense.** The only real cost of asking is a "no" — the far bigger cost is a "yes" you never asked for.
- **Charge for guarantees, warranties, and insurance instead of giving them away.** Adding 5–50% onto price in exchange for a guarantee converts what used to be a free give-away into pure profit (an art studio that used to replace damaged portraits free began charging 10% extra for that guarantee, and 30% of customers opted in).

## 10. Upsell Offer: Menu Upsell

**Description.** Tell customers what they *don't* need, then what they *do* need, then ask their preference between two options, then make paying frictionless. Menu Upsells combine up to four tactics, used in this order:

1. **Unselling** — explicitly rule out options the customer doesn't need, which builds trust and highlights what they *should* buy. Reframes the question from "do you want to buy" to "here's what you don't need," making the remaining recommendation land harder.
2. **Prescription Upselling** — state what they *do* need as settled fact rather than asking permission. Two components: explain how it integrates with what they already bought, and personalize exactly how to use it for maximum value. Works best when only one thing solves the problem, so offering a choice would just create friction.
3. **A/B Upselling** — ask a preference between two similar options that solve the same problem (chocolate or vanilla, morning or afternoon) rather than asking a yes/no. Removing the "no" option from the framing lowers the odds of a decline.
4. **Card On File** — ask *"Do you want to use the card on file?"* instead of asking whether they want to pay. Removes the hidden friction of re-entering payment details.

**Important notes:**

- **Almost anything can become an A/B offer:** quantity, start date, payment method, flavor, time slot, format, delivery speed, size, color, material, staff assignment, communication channel.
- **Add a nudge to A/B offers** for less-experienced customers ("this is my favorite," "a lot of people love this," "Tuesday's sessions are a little smaller") — nudge harder toward whichever option should move faster.
- **If sold out, take the payment and delay delivery** rather than losing the sale — set a clear expectation for arrival instead of carrying inventory.
- **Let employees "game the system" on customers' behalf** — employees with inside knowledge enjoy helping customers get the most value, and this builds goodwill that supports the upsell.
- **"The Economist Play"** (a visual device referenced in the book): adding a third, deliberately-inferior-looking option at the same price point as an existing option shifts more buyers toward the higher-priced anchor option — a classic decoy-pricing effect applied inside a menu.

## 11. Upsell Offer: Anchor Upsell

**Description.** Offer the premium, most expensive version first. If the customer visibly balks ("The Gasp"), offer a cheaper — but still perfectly acceptable — alternative. Presenting a much higher first offer resets the customer's reference point, so that a subsequently presented mid-tier offer looks like a far better deal than if it had been presented on its own.

**Steps:**

1. Present the Anchor — the genuinely expensive option.
2. Get "The Gasp" — expect and allow the sticker-shock reaction.
3. Come to the rescue — ask whether they actually care about what makes the anchor "premium."
4. Present the main offer — let them feel relief and see the better deal.
5. Ask how they want to pay.

**Important notes:**

- **The anchor has to be real, not a throwaway.** If you gloss over the premium offer and don't genuinely sell it, the customer never truly considered it, and the anchoring effect doesn't happen. Only move to the next offer after they pause, hesitate, or ask for something else.
- **Present a premium offer you'd genuinely be happy to deliver if someone bought it** — one operator who faked the premium option saw it fail; making it something he'd actually want to deliver tripled his profits.
- **The bigger the gasp, the more they end up buying.** A visible sticker-shock reaction is a signal the anchor worked, not a problem to avoid.
- **Anchored customers end up spending more than planned even when they decline the anchor**, and some customers buy the anchor itself outright.
- **Keep the same primary feature between anchor and main offer; vary the secondary features.** Most buyers only care about the primary function (a suit is a suit); the anchor's premium secondary features (designer, materials) are what justify its price. This makes the "downgrade" to the main offer feel like the same core value for a fraction of the cost.
- **Recommended anchor ratio: 5–10x the price of the main offer.**

## 12. Upsell Offer: Rollover Upsell

**Description.** Credit some or all of a customer's previous purchase toward a new, more expensive offer. This gets substantially more people to say yes than asking for a fresh purchase from zero. Three sub-decisions drive it: *who* to target, *what* to upsell them into, and *how* to apply the credit (up front vs. spread over time).

**Who to target (four situations):**

1. **Re-engage customers who left a while back** ("winback campaigns") — offer to apply some or all of a past purchase toward something more expensive than what they originally bought.
2. **Rescue upset customers** as a better alternative to a plain refund — credit the disputed purchase toward an upgraded offer that actually solves their complaint.
3. **"Rescue" *other businesses'* upset customers** — find competitors' dissatisfied customers (e.g., via negative reviews) and offer to credit what they still owe elsewhere toward switching to you.
4. **Upsell regular, current customers** — apply the cost of a smaller recent purchase toward a longer-term agreement.

**What to upsell into:** more of the same, something better, or something new — the same categories used for the Classic Upsell — but always priced high enough to leave a profit margin after the credit is applied.

**How to apply the credit:** front-loaded (all at once) or spread out over the term (e.g., a $600 credit becomes $50/month off for 12 months).

**Important notes:**

- **Do Rollover Upsells *before* refunding** — if the delivery fell short, offer a "do-over" or a switch to something more suited, funded by rolling the original payment forward, rather than defaulting to a refund.
- **Reach out to old customers systematically.** A winback campaign using personalized videos offering $4,000 of credit to 200 past customers converted about 20%, generating roughly $1,900,000 in additional annual revenue from one day of recording.
- **Add urgency — make it a one-time-only offer** presented live, not something to "sleep on." If declined, the customer can still buy at full price later; no real downside to asking.
- **Price the next offer at least 4x the rollover credit amount**, so that even applying the full credit produces at most a 25% discount and leaves real margin.
- **You don't have to credit the full original purchase** — roll over whatever amount seems most likely to move the customer to buy, and test to find the right level.

## 13. Downsell Offers — Overview

Downsell Offers are whatever gets offered after someone says no, turning declines into sales. Three tactics are covered: Payment Plan Downsells and Trial With Penalty (both change *how* someone pays), and Feature Downsells (changes *what* they get).

**Governing principles for all downsells:**

- **A "no" to this offer is not a "no" to everything.** Rejection of a specific offer is a chance to find out what the customer actually wants and profit from it — not a signal to give up.
- **Downsells are trades.** Work with the customer to find combinations of giving and getting until there's a match. If you're going to give something, get something in return.
- **Personalize, don't pressure.** Learn what the customer likes and dislikes, then offer more of what they like and less of what they don't, at a matching price. Asking follow-up questions to serve them better is not offensive — failing to ask would be.
- **Offer the same things in new ways rather than creating unlimited new products.** Downselling is about finding a hundred ways to offer what you already have, not building a hundred new offers.
- **Never drop price just to force a yes — that's discounting, not downselling.** If someone wants what you have but won't pay the price, a straight price cut is the wrong tool; offer a payment plan (pay less *now*, more over time) or a feature downsell (pay less *overall* for less product) instead.
- **Customers talk about price.** Ad hoc, in-the-moment discounting (versus a planned, tested price offered consistently to a defined group) creates resentment when customers compare notes, and is treated as an ethical problem, not just a tactical one.

## 14. Downsell Offer: Payment Plan Downsell

**Description.** Instead of changing the product, spread its existing cost by charging part up front and scheduling the rest as future payments. A large share of "this costs too much" objections actually mean "this costs too much *right now*" — payment plans convert those objections without discounting the total price. Payment plans are explicitly framed as a gamble: they make more money when customers who wouldn't otherwise have bought now buy and complete their payments, but lose money when customers who *would have paid in full* take a payment plan and cancel early.

**The seven-step downsell ladder** (each step is offered only if the previous one is declined):

1. **Reward paying in full rather than punishing paying over time.** Present the price as if it already includes the cost of financing, then frame prepayment as earning a discount ("It's $15, but $10 if you prepay — you save $5") rather than framing installments as an interest surcharge. Same math, better framing, plus a built-in price anchor.
2. **Offer 3rd-party financing, credit card, or layaway.** Third-party financing gets the seller paid today while the customer owes a separate finance company. Reframing credit-card use as "you decide the payment terms" gets the seller paid today while the customer manages installments with their card issuer. Layaway (pay in full before receiving the product, on the customer's own installment timeline) is the lowest-risk option for the seller.
3. **Offer half now, half on their next payday.** Ask when they're next paid, then propose splitting on that schedule. Aligning to paycheck dates (commonly bi-weekly) outperforms monthly billing for 30-day profit.
4. **Check whether they still want the product at all**, using a 1–10 desire scale, before offering further concessions. An 8+ answer justifies continued payment-plan effort; 7 or below signals a different product is the better fit (see Feature Downsells).
5. **Split into three payments**, again aligned to paycheck or monthly cadence.
6. **Spread payments evenly across the full service term** (e.g., 16 weekly payments for a 16-week program).
7. **Offer a Free Trial** in exchange for a card on file (detailed in the Trial With Penalty chapter) — this is the final fallback.

**"Seesaw" downselling** (a simpler variant for less-experienced salespeople or fewer steps): ask *"Would you rather have giant monthly payments or tiny ones?"* — most say tiny — then present prepayment as the way to a big discount and zero monthly payments. If they still can't afford it, frame it as "the more you put down now, the lower your monthly payment," which keeps nudging toward a bigger down payment while still closing the sale as a team effort.

**Important notes:**

- **Payment plans have a built-in upsell:** periodically re-offer the original prepay discount during an active payment plan — customers forget the option exists and some take it. Incentivize sales staff the same way to reward follow-up.
- **Align billing dates to paycheck dates to reduce declined payments**, and re-run declined charges multiple times the same day (payroll deposit timing varies) — this alone recovers roughly a third of otherwise-lost payments.
- **Payment plans only help if the paid-in-full rate holds steady while total sales rise.** If offering a payment plan just shifts existing full-pay buyers onto installments instead of adding new buyers, it hasn't helped. The healthy pattern: same fraction paying in full, plus additional sales from people who wouldn't have bought otherwise.
- **Billing cadence itself changes churn.** Data cited from Profitwell across 14,000 businesses: monthly billing produces roughly 10.7% monthly cancellation; quarterly billing produces roughly 5%; annual billing produces roughly 2%. Presenting pricing from most-cash-up-front to least (as this process does) also happens to produce the lowest-churn cadence.

## 15. Downsell Offer: Trial With Penalty

**Description.** The customer can use the product or service for free *so long as they meet stated terms* — the inverse of Win Your Money Back, where the customer pays and gets refunded for meeting terms. Here, the customer doesn't pay *up front*, and only pays *if they fail* to meet the terms. The terms should mirror the actions/results used in a Win Your Money Back offer, but the enforcement mechanism is a fee for non-compliance rather than a refund for compliance. This is deployed specifically as the last-resort downsell after the full Payment Plan ladder is exhausted.

**Why it beats a plain free trial:** if a business normally closes 3 of 10 leads on the paid offer, and can convert another 4 of the remaining 7 via Trial With Penalty, total conversions double (3 → 6) — plain free trials leave everyone who says no on the table.

**Important notes:**

- **Set terms that make excellent customers**, not arbitrary hoops — attendance at consulting calls, weekly progress posts, daily logging, attendance at feedback/upsell sessions.
- **Mid-trial check-ins branch into three responses:**
  - *If they like it:* meet anyway, and offer a longer-term or higher-value version — successful customers extract even more value from premium tiers.
  - *If they hate it:* take ownership without blaming them, ask what they'd have wanted differently, and pitch a better-fit higher-level offer once you understand their real need (roughly half convert this way).
  - *If they haven't used it:* reach out multiple times before penalizing, offer to waive the fee if they engage now, and use the moment to get them back on track or move them to something better suited.
- **Call it simply "a free trial"** to customers — the "penalty" framing internally shouldn't leak into customer-facing language. If asked why the trial works this way, a simple "this is just how we've always done it" or "people get the best results this way" suffices.
- **Tune the offer based on where it breaks:** if no one takes the trial, lower requirements or penalties; if people take it but don't follow through, emphasize how the fees help them and make check-in meetings mandatory; if people don't convert on the back end, improve delivery and make sure the back-end offer logically follows the front-end one.
- **Use Pay Less Now or Pay More Later for physical products and one-time services; use Trial With Penalty for recurring products or services.** The book notes this pattern has only been validated in businesses where the customer must do work to get results.
- **A low symbolic charge (e.g., "$1 for the first month" instead of fully free) both signals seriousness and validates that the card on file will work** when full billing begins.

## 16. Downsell Offer: Feature Downsell

**Description.** Lower the price by changing *what the customer gets* — lower quantity, lower quality, a lower-price alternative, or removing an optional component entirely (including guarantees). This differs from a discount: every feature carries both a price and a value, and removing a feature the customer *doesn't* care about, while cutting price meaningfully, produces a better perceived deal than a straight discount would. The core mechanic: take something away, lower the price, and ask "how about now?"

**The value-visibility effect:** customers often only see the true value of a feature *after* it's been removed and they see the resulting price gap. This means presenting a feature downsell (e.g., removing a guarantee) can cause the customer to "re-upsell" themselves back onto the original, fuller offer once they realize what they'd be giving up.

**Categories of feature to cut, roughly ordered from highest to lowest value removed:**

- **Quantity** — fewer sessions, shorter duration, smaller supply.
- **Quality** — older versions, less premium materials, lower-status materials.
- **Service-quality dimensions** — time availability (specific hours vs. anytime), location availability, cancellation flexibility, response speed, delivery speed, service ratio (1:1 vs. group), communication channel, provider seniority, live vs. recorded, in-person vs. remote, DIY/DWY/DFY tier, expiration terms, personalization level, and guarantee terms (duration, coverage, conditions).
- **Whole features removed** — e.g., dropping phone support while keeping chat and email.
- **DFY-to-DIY product swap** — when a service downsell is fully rejected, offer a lower-cost product that solves the same underlying problem (a chiropractor selling at-home tools instead of adjustments; a painter selling paint and a rented sprayer instead of the paint job).

**Important notes:**

- **Never negotiate price for the same thing** — "people who demand to pay less for the same thing are business terrorists." If they want to pay less *now*, offer a payment plan; if they want to pay less *overall*, offer a feature downsell.
- **Stay a helpful guide, not a pressuring salesperson** — framing every downsell as finding "the best deal for them" keeps the interaction collaborative and lets you offer many downsells without exhausting the customer.
- **Standardize the process once patterns emerge** — once you understand what a given customer type values most, pre-build feature combinations rather than improvising every time.
- **Cut the most valuable feature first, at a small price reduction**, to nudge the customer back to reconsidering the original offer before offering deeper cuts.
- **Name your packages** — the priciest bundle gets an aspirational name (e.g., "The Whale Package," "High Roller"); the cheapest bundle is named "The Minimum," which supports a Classic-Upsell-style close ("so nothing more than the minimum package then?").
- **Run a desire check after two declined downsells**, same 1–10 scale used in Payment Plan Downsells — 8+ means keep working the deal (now alternating between feature and payment-plan downsells makes the offer very hard to refuse); 7 or below means asking what a "10" would look like and rebuilding the package around that answer.
- **Close every downsell with "Deal?" or "Fair enough?"** — very few customers who agreed to the trade then object that it's unfair.
- **Offer a free orientation to DIY alternatives** once all DFY options are declined — attendees convert to DIY product purchases at a high rate even though they declined the full-service offer.
- **Feature-downsell your own guarantee** — removing a guarantee for a discount often causes customers to realize how much they value it and choose to keep the fuller offer instead.
- **Feature-downsell existing customers who aren't using paid-for features**, before they cancel outright — customers downsold into a lower package tailored to them become, by the book's account, the second-highest-value customer segment.
- **Barter discounts for advertising** — trade a price cut for reviews, video testimonials, milestone social posts, and referral introductions, when the advertising value exceeds the discount given.

## 17. Continuity Offers — Overview

Continuity Offers provide ongoing value in exchange for ongoing payment, until cancellation. Unlike the other three offer types, which depend on getting a customer to buy once, Continuity depends on getting a customer to keep buying.

**The core cash-vs-total-value tradeoff:** selling a $1,000 one-time offer to 10 of 100 leads produces $10,000 now and nothing later. Selling a $50/month continuity offer to 40 of 100 leads, retained an average of 20 months, produces $2,000 now and $40,000 over time — a $30,000 net gain in total revenue, but a much slower cash trickle, and 4x more customers on hand to upsell later. This is why Continuity works best placed *last* in a Money Model: Attraction, Upsell, and Downsell offers generate the cash needed to operate *today*, while Continuity captures the *majority* of lifetime value over time.

**Fit test for Continuity:** it only makes sense where ongoing value is actually delivered. Charging forever for a one-day workshop is nonsensical (that's just a slow payment plan); conversely, charging one lump sum for a service that delivers ongoing value likely underprices the relationship.

Three Continuity Offers are covered: Continuity Bonus Offers, Continuity Discount Offers, and the Waived Fee Offer — built by combining bonuses, discounts, and fees to get customers to start, and to keep them once they have.

## 18. Continuity Offer: Continuity Bonus Offer

**Description.** Give the customer something valuable *if* they sign up for continuity today — typically a bonus worth more than the first continuity payment itself. Bonuses add value (a bundled product, a program, onboarding, a feature); discounts lower cost (any bonus can also be reframed as a discount). Using both together captures the psychological pull of each.

**Two examples of the same lever from the book's origin story:** a challenge-based gym shifted from selling a six-week challenge (34/100 signed up, 17 converted to membership later) to leading with membership itself, waiving the challenge fee and adding member-exclusive perks (better class times, tanning booth, VIP events) plus a prepaid discount upsell — resulting in triple the membership sign-ups (40/100 direct to continuity) plus continued challenge revenue plus prepay upsells stacked on top.

**Important notes:**

- **Advertise the bonus, not the membership.** "Join my membership" undersells; "get this valuable thing free" pulls interest, with membership details explained after interest is shown.
- **Bonuses follow the same three upsell shapes** as the Classic Upsell: more of the same (extra newsletter back-issues), complementary (free nutrition service with a fitness membership), or an upgrade (a free higher tier with limited availability).
- **Keep bonuses tightly related to the core offer** — a mismatched bonus (a free t-shirt attached to tech services) attracts the wrong customer.
- **Favor bonuses that cost nothing extra to produce** — content or processes already created (past newsletter issues, onboarding you'd deliver anyway) make high-value, zero-marginal-cost bonuses.
- **Physical bonuses can support digital continuity and vice versa** — a physical item (hat, tool) alongside a digital membership, or a livestream-class bonus alongside a physical gym membership, can lower acquisition cost by more than the bonus itself costs.
- **Anchor bonus values honestly** — inflated, unbelievable value claims destroy trust and fail to anchor. Real historical prices of things you've sold before make credible anchors.
- **Titles as a bonus:** naming loyalty tiers (silver, gold, diamond) can become something customers value and self-identify with more than any tangible perk.
- **Sell the bonus's benefit first, then reveal how to get it free** by joining, then stack additional bonuses on top with individual dollar values called out — stacking this way increases sign-ups further.
- **Restrict bonuses to continuity signups only** if the goal is to force every customer through continuity rather than a one-time purchase.
- **Standalone (non-continuity) pricing vs. continuity pricing is a dial, not a fixed rule** — the book cites internal testing showing the smaller the standalone price relative to the continuity price, the more people choose standalone; the larger the standalone price relative to continuity, the more people choose continuity. Example figures cited: a $799 standalone option (equivalent to roughly $532/month if spread) against a $199/month membership.
- **To maximize upfront cash, price the standalone-plus-bonus option at 1.33x–2.66x the first month of the continuity+bonus offer** — the bigger that multiple, the fewer standalone buyers, but the more revenue captured from each.
- **Bulk prepaid discounts lift 30-day profit disproportionately** — an offer like "buy five months, get one free" only needs 1 in 8 people to take it to raise 30-day profit by 50%.
- **Trading a bonus for a longer commitment (3/6/12+ months) nets fewer total sign-ups but more committed customers** — a deliberate trade, not a flaw.

## 19. Continuity Offer: Continuity Discount Offer

**Description.** Give free continuity time — now or later — in exchange for a longer commitment (e.g., a landscaping or internet-style "sign a year, get months free" structure). This works broadly across recurring-revenue categories: internet, pool cleaning, gyms, landscaping, and anything rentable.

**Four ways to apply the discount:**

1. **Up front** — free months at the start of the term.
2. **At the end** — free months tacked onto the back of the committed term.
3. **Spread evenly** — the discount trickles across the whole term.
4. **After the first payment(s)** — a short delay before the discount kicks in.

**Important notes:**

- **Extend the term rather than shortening the paid portion.** Offering "3 months free with a 12-month sign-up" should mean paying for all 12 covered months' worth of value with 3 extra tacked on (15 total months), not compressing to 9 paid + 3 free inside a nominal 12-month term. Extending the term first leaves room to later Feature-Downsell a shorter version.
- **A 3% processing-fee add-on compounds meaningfully into profit** — on a 10%-margin business, adding an unresisted 3% surcharge effectively adds 30% to bottom-line profit, since almost no one declines to buy over the line item.
- **Take a second form of payment**, framed as earning a small processing-fee discount by doing so — this reduces failed-payment churn from expired cards and insufficient funds, the two biggest sources of "involuntary" churn in recurring businesses. ACH (direct bank-account debit) is the cheapest processing method to collect as the second form when available.
- **Deliver discounted time as a physical gift card** rather than an automatic account credit — it can be mailed to remote customers, gifted to a friend (creating a referral lead magnet), applied whenever the customer chooses after the first few payments, and is often simply forgotten, converting to a full-price signup by default.
- **Set a "lifetime discount" trigger at the business's known average churn point.** If customers typically drop off at month four, promise a lower lifetime rate that unlocks at month four — timed to catch people right where they'd otherwise leave. (Example cited: a rice subscription business offering a one-time price, a 5%-off subscription, or 15% off if the customer stays five consecutive months — just past the typical cancellation point.)
- **Decide a cancellation policy in advance.** The preferred structure: make the cancellation fee equal to the discount the customer originally received, so canceling simply reverts them to the undiscounted month-to-month rate — simple to explain and administer.
- **Make cancellation easy to initiate.** Customers with no clear internal path to complain or cancel will vanish and complain publicly instead. A visible cancellation path preserves the chance to save the relationship.
- **Offer an exit interview in exchange for waiving the cancellation fee.** Letting an upset customer vent, matching their frustration rather than calming it, and offering a rollover upsell into a better-fit tier when the complaint reveals unmet needs reportedly saves about a third of customers who agree to the interview.

## 20. Continuity Offer: Waived Fee Offer

**Description.** Present two paths: pay a significant one-time setup fee and keep month-to-month flexibility to cancel anytime, or commit to a longer term (typically 12+ months) and have that fee fully waived — with the fee becoming payable again only if the customer cancels before the commitment ends. Recommended fee size: 3–5x the monthly rate.

**The underlying logic:** the business takes on more risk with month-to-month customers (covered by charging the setup fee), while the customer takes on more risk by committing long-term (offset by waiving that same fee). If a committed customer wants to leave early, they simply pay the fee they would have paid from day one under the month-to-month path — symmetrical and simple to explain.

**Example structure from the book:** 12-month commitment, $1,000/month rate, $5,000 fee if month-to-month. Option A: pay $5,000 plus the first month ($1,000), then $1,000/month, cancel anytime. Option B: waive the $5,000 by committing to 12 months at $1,000/month; the $5,000 becomes payable only if the commitment is broken early.

**Important notes:**

- **Fees drive both starting and staying.** Customers commit to avoid paying the fee up front, and then keep staying to avoid triggering it later — the fee's presence shrinks the pull of whatever reason they might otherwise have for quitting.
- **Justify the fee as a real cost transfer**, not an arbitrary penalty: *"It costs us money to get you started. If you only want to test us out, you cover those costs. If you commit longer, I'll cover them for you."*
- **If more than 5% of committed customers want to cancel early, investigate the product**, not just the pricing mechanism — pricing should nudge behavior, not trap someone in a product they genuinely dislike.
- **Smaller fee → more up-front cash** (encourages more people toward month-to-month, each paying a setup fee immediately); **larger fee → more long-term commitments.** A 1.5–3x-of-monthly-rate fee is suggested when the priority is maximizing immediate cash.
- **Drop the fee obligation entirely once the customer completes the original commitment** — it doesn't follow them forever once earned out.
- **Best suited to one-year-plus commitments**, and especially to services with a long time-to-results (SEO, investing, weight loss) — the fee keeps people committed through the emotionally hard stretch before results appear.
- **Optional intensifier:** ask the customer to name a cause they actively dislike, and tell them an early-cancellation fee will be donated to that cause — adds a second, non-financial reason to stay.

## 21. Building The $100M Money Model — Three Stages And Example Breakdowns

**Description.** A Money Model is a deliberate sequence of offers — what to offer, when, and how, to make the most money as fast as possible. The stated target: earn enough from one customer, within 30 days, to acquire and service *at least two more* customers. The book breaks Money Model construction into three stages:

- **Stage I: Get Cash** — Attraction Offers acquire more customers for less.
- **Stage II: Get More Cash** — Upsell and Downsell Offers extract more money from those customers, faster.
- **Stage III: Get The Most Cash** — Continuity Offers maximize total money spent per customer over time.

**Money Models evolve in this order, never all at once:** first, get customers reliably; then make sure each customer pays for their own acquisition cost reliably; then make sure each customer pays for acquiring *additional* customers reliably; then maximize each customer's long-term value; only then, spend as much as possible on advertising to scale. No business in the author's portfolio — including Acquisition.com itself — started with a finished Money Model; all started at Stage I and built forward. A warning attached to this: as soon as the Money Model starts working, the *business* starts breaking operationally — a team capable of executing the vision becomes necessary at that point, not optional.

**Worked example — Gym Launch (Services):**

- Stage I Attraction: Decoy Offer — free DIY plan vs. premium $16,000 done-with-you licensing.
- Stage II Upsell: Classic Upsell — $42,000/year (or $36,000 prepaid) advanced business services ("Gym Lords"), plus a Continuity Bonus (a shared best-practices community).
- Stage II Downsell: Payment Plan Downsell — seesaw from $10,000 down + balance spread over 52 weeks, down to a final $800/week for 52 weeks.
- Stage III Continuity: Menu Close + Feature Downsell — full package $800/week (done-for-you advertising $300/wk + sales training $200/wk + monthly new releases $500/wk + original licensing materials with tech support $100/wk), downsellable to a $100/week "Minimum Package."
- Revenue trajectory reported: Decoy Offer alone reached $476,000/month within three months; adding the Classic Upsell + Continuity Bonus + Payment Plan Downsell + Continuity Discount reached roughly $1,500,000/month; adding Menu Upsells + Feature Downsells reached $2,300,000/month within 14 months; by month 20 (after integrating a second business, Prestige Labs), $4,400,000/month.

**Worked example — Micro Gyms (Local Business):**

- Stage I Attraction: Win Your Money Back — pay-to-enter fitness challenge, win the money back by meeting goals.
- Stage I Downsell: Payment Plan Downsell — Split Pay → Three-Pay → Free Trial With Penalty.
- Stage II Upsell: Menu Upsell — supplement bundles personalized to the customer's goal.
- Stage II Downsell: Feature Downsell — Big Bundle → Small Bundle → Monthly Subscription.
- Stage III Continuity: Rollover Upsell + Lifetime Discount — $50 off per month for life with a 12-month commitment.

**Worked example — Newsletter (Digital Product):**

- Stage I Attraction: Free Trial — $0, then $399/month after 30 days.
- Stage II/III combined Upsell + Continuity: Pay Less Now/Pay More Later + Lifetime Discount — pay $297 now and keep that rate for life. Described in the book as a "six-headed money-making monster" because it simultaneously functions as an attraction offer, an upsell offer, and a continuity offer.

**Worked example — Dog Food (Physical Product):**

- Stage I Attraction: Buy X Get Y Free — buy 4 months of food, get 2 months free.

**Building rules:**

- **Perfect one offer at a time, per stage, before moving to the next.** Pick one offer, run it until reliable, then run it until automatic, then advance. Measure progress in quarters, not weeks — building it right the first time is still faster than repeatedly rebuilding a rushed version.
- **Raise price in stages.** Launch new offers cheap to generate early yeses and usable feedback, then raise price progressively until the incremental cash from new yeses stops outweighing the cash lost from new nos.
- **Simple scales, fancy fails.** The goal is not 100 products but 100 *ways to offer* the same product (e.g., 1/2/3/4 personal-training sessions per week from one core service) — this multiplies offers without multiplying operational complexity.
- **Affiliate relationships can fill Money Model gaps without added delivery burden** — selling another company's product for a commission works at any stage: to give a brand-new business something to sell, to add an offer to an existing sequence, or to add revenue to an established business without extra operational load. Examples cited: a dental agency referring braces-manufacturer commissions; a massage therapist reselling home-massage tools shipped direct by the manufacturer; an educator earning a commission for referring clients to a software vendor.
- **Attraction Offers can be converted into Continuity Offers via automatic renewal** — e.g., a "Buy 6 Months Get 6 Months Free" offer that rolls automatically into a month-to-month subscription at the end of the free period, capturing the benefit of both offer types from a single structure.
- **There are no fixed rules for combining offer types** — Upsell tactics can appear inside an Attraction Offer, a Downsell process can be attached to any offer, a Continuity Offer can be used to attract brand-new customers. The book presents each mechanism in one default configuration but explicitly expects readers to recombine them differently as they get more experienced.

## 22. Ten Years In Ten Minutes — The Condensed Playbook

The book's own closing distillation of its entire framework:

1. A **Money Model** is a series of offers designed to increase how many customers you get, how much they pay, and how fast they pay it.
2. **A good Money Model** makes more profit from a customer than it costs to get and service them in the first 30 days. That is the bare minimum standard.
3. **A $100M Money Model** makes more profit from one customer than it costs to get and service *many* customers in the first 30 days — which removes cash as a limiter on scaling.
4. Money Models have **four offer types**: Attraction, Upsell, Downsell, and Continuity.
5. **Attraction Offers** get customers via something free or discounted, often paired with a better deal at a higher price:
   - *Win Your Money Back* — set a goal and a path to it; hitting the goal (via results, actions, or both) returns the money as cash or credit.
   - *Giveaways* — advertise a chance to win a big prize for contact info and qualifying actions; after picking a winner, offer everyone else the same thing at a discount.
   - *Decoy Offers* — advertise something free or discounted, then present a more valuable premium option alongside it once the lead engages.
   - *Buy X Get Y Free* — give free stuff in exchange for buying paid stuff; more free value drives more purchases.
   - *Pay Less Now or Pay More Later* — let the customer choose full price later or a discount plus bonuses now.
6. **Upsell Offers** are whatever's offered next — typically more, better, or newer versions of what was just bought:
   - *The Classic Upsell* — solve the customer's next problem the instant it appears ("you can't have X without Y").
   - *Menu Upsells* — tell them what they don't need, then what they do, then let them pick between two options ("you don't need that, you need this").
   - *Anchor Upsells* — offer the most expensive thing first; if they balk, offer a much cheaper, still-acceptable alternative ("if you don't care about X, this may be a better fit").
   - *Rollover Upsells* — credit prior purchases toward the next offer ("since you already spent $500, I'll credit that toward a full year").
7. **Downsell Offers** are whatever's offered after a no, turning nos into yeses:
   - *Payment Plan Downsells* — same product and price, paid over time instead of up front ("when do you get paid? let's do half now, half then").
   - *Trial With Penalty* — free access as long as stated terms are met; a fee applies only if they aren't ("if you do X, Y, Z, I'll let you start for free").
   - *Feature Downsells* — lower price by changing what's included: less quantity, lower quality, a cheaper alternative, or cut components ("if you're okay without a guarantee, I can knock off $400").
8. **Continuity Offers** provide ongoing value for ongoing payment until cancellation, boosting per-customer profit and giving one more thing to sell:
   - *Continuity Bonus Offers* — give something valuable, worth more than the first payment, if they sign up today.
   - *Continuity Discount Offers* — give free time, now or later, if they sign up today.
   - *Waived Fee Offers* — charge a setup fee for month-to-month flexibility, or waive it entirely for a longer commitment; break the commitment early and the fee becomes due.
9. **Build Money Models one stage at a time:** get customers reliably, then make sure they pay for themselves reliably, then make sure they pay for acquiring other customers reliably, then maximize each customer's long-term value, then spend aggressively on advertising once cash is no longer the constraint.

**Bottom line stated by the author:** this knowledge produced more free and profitable customers than he knew what to do with, and applying it removes cash as a constraint on business growth.
