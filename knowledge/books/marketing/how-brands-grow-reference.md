# How Brands Grow — Framework Reference

*Pure framework reference distilled from the book. No commentary, no application notes — just the frameworks, models, and mental tools, explained in original language.*

*Source: How Brands Grow: What Marketers Don't Know by Byron Sharp*

---

## 1. The Core Reframe: Old World View vs. New World View

Sharp opens by naming a set of "strategic assumptions" that most working marketers believe are true, and then spends the rest of the book showing that each one is false or badly overstated: that differentiation is a vital marketing task, that loyalty metrics reflect brand strength rather than brand size, that retention is cheaper than acquisition, that price promotions build penetration rather than just serving existing loyal buyers, that competitive sets are defined by brand positioning, that mass marketing is dead, that buyers have a special reason for choosing "their" brand, that a brand's buyers are a distinctive type of person, and that the heaviest 20% of buyers deliver 80% of sales.

He frames this as a wholesale paradigm shift, laid out as a series of "past world view / new world view" pairings that recur throughout the book:

- Positioning → Salience
- Differentiation → Distinctiveness
- Message comprehension → Getting noticed, emotional response
- Unique selling propositions → Relevant associations
- Persuasion → Refreshing and building memory structures
- Teaching → Reaching
- Rational, involved viewers → Emotional, distracted viewers
- Attitude drives behavior → Behavior drives attitude
- Brand loyals / brand switchers → Loyal switchers (everyone is a switcher, to varying degrees)
- Deeply committed buyers → Uncaring cognitive misers
- Growth through targeting brand loyals → Growth through penetration
- Obscure proprietary metrics → Predictable, transparent metrics
- Price promotions win new customers → Price promotions mostly reach existing loyal customers
- Target marketing / differentiation → Sophisticated mass marketing

The single sentence Sharp treats as the book's most important idea: no marketing activity, including innovation, should be treated as a goal in itself — its only real function is to build or protect a brand's mental and physical availability. Everything else in the book cashes this idea out chapter by chapter.

Three headline conclusions anchor the whole framework: (1) brands grow mainly by increasing their number of buyers — their popularity — most of whom will only ever be light, occasional buyers; (2) brands within a category, despite being marketed as different, actually compete much more like near-identical alternatives that simply vary in popularity; (3) brand competition and growth is fundamentally about building two market-based assets — physical availability and mental availability — and innovation or differentiation only matter to the extent that they build these assets in ways that outlast being copied by competitors.

## 2. The Double Jeopardy Law

This is the load-bearing empirical finding of the whole book. Compare brands of different sizes within the same category and a stable pattern appears: bigger brands have a much larger base of buyers (penetration), and those buyers also purchase very slightly more often (loyalty/purchase frequency) than the buyers of smaller brands — but the loyalty gap is tiny compared to the penetration gap. Smaller brands are "hit twice": they have fewer buyers, and those buyers are also marginally less loyal. Hence the name — smallness is punished twice over, not once.

This holds with remarkable consistency: it has been documented in washing powder, shampoo (UK and US), ready-mix concrete sold to building firms, cars, banking, insurance, TV programs, newspapers, radio stations, comic strips, and political parties, across many countries and decades. The near-universality of the pattern is what elevates it from a one-off observation to a scientific law in Sharp's sense — a finding stable enough across conditions that it can be used for prediction.

The practical implication is a hard constraint on strategy: if a small brand wants to reach the sales level of a market leader, it cannot get there mainly by pushing its existing buyers to buy far more often, because loyalty levels simply don't vary that much between brands of different sizes in the same category. The only realistic path to that sales level is acquiring a much larger buyer base — matching the market leader's number of buyers, at a purchase frequency similar to what buyers of that size brand normally show. Trying to "out-loyalty" a much bigger competitor without growing the customer base is, in the categories examined, essentially never observed to work.

Double jeopardy also generalizes beyond simple market share. It shows up in defection rates, cross-selling metrics, image/attitude survey scores, and even distribution: any metric that functions as a loyalty-type measure will tend to be higher for bigger brands and lower for smaller ones, roughly in proportion to size, not because bigger brands are qualitatively "better loved," but because that's the mathematical signature of how buying behavior distributes itself across brands of different popularity.

## 3. Double Jeopardy Applied to Retention and Defection

Customer defection rates follow the same double jeopardy pattern as purchase frequency: smaller brands necessarily show higher percentage defection rates than larger brands, even when nothing about their service quality, loyalty programs, or customer care differs. This is arithmetic, not sentiment. In a two-brand market where a small brand holds 20% share (200 customers) and a large brand holds 80% share (800 customers), if both are stable, the number of customers switching each way must be equal — say 100 each way. That's a 50% defection rate for the small brand and only 12.5% for the large one, from the exact same underlying switching behavior.

This pattern is confirmed repeatedly across car brands (US, UK, France) and banks: the largest brand in a market consistently shows the lowest defection percentage, but never anything close to zero, and the gap between the largest and smallest brand's defection rate is much smaller than their difference in size would suggest. A famous, widely cited Harvard Business Review claim (Reichheld & Sasser, 1990) — that cutting customer defection by 5 percentage points can boost profits by up to 100% — is shown to be a pure thought experiment (an analytic tautology about what happens if tenure doubles), not an empirical finding about what marketing interventions can actually achieve. No brand in the available data sets, however large its market share, gets anywhere near a defection rate half that of its rivals; there's no empirical case of a brand halving its defection rate through service or retention initiatives.

The growth-math implication is stark: because most categories see roughly half of all buyers switch brands at each purchase occasion, the sales potential available through acquisition (winning switching buyers) dwarfs the sales potential available through retention (reducing your own defection to zero). For an example car brand, driving defection to literally zero might net one extra percentage point of share; ordinary category switching puts 50 points of share in play every year via acquisition. Research into growing and declining brands (pharmaceutical, chocolate, banking categories) confirms that brands grow almost entirely through unusually strong acquisition, not through unusually low defection — and decline is driven by weak acquisition, not rising defection. Defection rates for both growing and declining brands stay roughly normal for their size; it's the buyer-gaining side of the ledger that moves.

Much of defection is also shown to be outside marketer control altogether — people move house, no longer need the category, or are simply reached by a competitor's marketing at the right moment. A regional bank's higher defection rate compared to a national bank, for instance, is explained almost entirely by branch coverage (a customer who relocates outside the region has no choice but to switch), not by any measurable difference in service quality or loyalty-building effort.

## 4. Penetration Is the Growth Metric That Matters

Because of double jeopardy, market share growth is overwhelmingly a penetration story: brands grow chiefly by adding new buyers (of all types, including many who will only ever buy lightly), not by extracting dramatically more purchases from an already-loyal base. Multiple independent large-sample studies back this: a meta-analysis of 207 US split-cable ad weight tests found that having an explicit goal of increasing penetration was the only strategy variable correlated with larger sales effects. An analysis of 880 IPA Effectiveness Award entries found that campaigns targeting new-customer acquisition/penetration were more than twice as likely to win awards and to report large, measurable gains in sales and profit than campaigns targeting loyalty or retention — even though entrants set loyalty-focused goals more than twice as often. A later, larger replication (996 campaigns over 30 years) found campaigns targeting new customers were three times as effective, on average, as campaigns targeting existing customers. Internal analysis at Kraft found the same pattern: the majority of internal brand plans were, in Sharp's phrase, "swimming upstream" by chasing purchase frequency, when penetration was empirically shown to be the dominant driver of the company's own brand growth and decline.

The takeaway is a specific, falsifiable prediction: if a brand is genuinely growing share, its marketing metrics will show a jump in penetration with only a marginal rise in purchase frequency — never a purchase-frequency-led growth story, because that pattern essentially never survives contact with the double jeopardy law. Marketers who set purchase-frequency or loyalty-uplift targets are (often unknowingly) setting themselves up to fight a pattern that reliably reasserts itself.

## 5. Why the Usual Alternative Growth Strategies Underdeliver

Several other "obvious" growth levers turn out to be structurally limited once tested against real buying-behavior data:

**Teaching new uses / growing the category.** The classic textbook case is Arm & Hammer teaching consumers to use baking soda as a fridge deodorizer or exfoliant. In practice, category-habit change through advertising is extremely difficult: habits are hard to shift (dentists have pushed twice-daily brushing for decades with limited effect), non-obvious mental links are hard to build (people who mentally file a product under "baking" rarely re-file it under "deodorizing"), and few single brands have advertising budgets large enough to reshape an entire category's habits. Where categories do grow or shrink over time, it's rarely because one brand's advertising drove it.

**Cross-selling.** CRM and loyalty-program logic assumes existing customers are easy incremental sales. Real cross-sell metrics (insurance, banking) show very little difference between competing brands — again, differences that do appear mostly track market share (double jeopardy), not cross-sell skill. Claims of superior cross-selling (e.g., Wells Fargo's "six products per household") are frequently inflated by counting methodology, and independent analysts have pushed back on such claims. Genuine cross-sell resistance also has a simple structural cause: many non-buyers of a product genuinely don't need it (you can't sell car insurance to someone with a company car), and many service customers already hold their highest-value product with you.

**Targeting a brand's heaviest buyers.** The "Heavy Buyer Fallacy" — the idea that heavy current buyers are the best target for extra marketing spend because they're already proven valuable — confuses past buying with future growth potential. Heavy buyers are usually already buying essentially all they ever will; the real growth potential sits with light and non-buyers. Related research (Reinartz & Kumar) found that customer tenure is a surprisingly poor predictor of lifetime profitability — long-standing customers don't reliably become more profitable or less price-sensitive over time.

**Niche strategies.** True niche brands (small buyer base, but genuinely and proportionally more loyal) are far rarer than marketing folklore suggests, because if many brands in a category behaved this way, double jeopardy itself would break down — and it doesn't. Most brands that look "niche" are better understood as suffering a penetration deficit relative to their loyalty level, usually due to a real gap in physical availability (missing from a distribution channel or geography) or mental availability (simply not thought of/noticed by part of the population), not because they've successfully carved out a small, devoted following. A lack of penetration is very rarely something to be proud of.

**The "customer profitability" retention myth.** Reichheld and Sasser's widely repeated claim about retention driving profitability is, per Sharp, a tautological thought experiment dressed up as an empirical finding, built on an unrealistic assumption (halving defection at zero cost) that has never been demonstrated to be achievable in practice.

## 6. The NBD Distribution and the Real Shape of the Pareto Law

Buyer purchase frequencies for any brand follow a highly skewed statistical pattern called the negative binomial distribution (NBD), first documented by Andrew Ehrenberg in 1959. The shape is consistent across brands: a small number of very heavy buyers, and a very large number of light buyers who purchase only once or twice in a year (or not at all in a given period). Coca-Cola, a category-leading giant, still gets roughly half its buyers purchasing themselves only one or two cans a year, and around 30% of cola-category buyers don't buy Coke at all in a given year. A brand comparison of Coca-Cola and Pepsi shows the same NBD shape for both — Pepsi's distribution simply looks like Coke's slid slightly to the left (a somewhat smaller, slightly less frequently-bought base), not a different-shaped distribution.

This NBD shape is what underlies marketing's most misapplied law: the 80/20 rule, or Pareto's Law, which claims 80% of sales come from the heaviest 20% of buyers. In real data this ratio is almost never as extreme as 80/20. Measured over a 3-month window, a typical FMCG brand's "Pareto share" is closer to 35%; over a full year it usually rises to just over 50%, rarely approaching 80%. Professor Gerald Goodhardt's 20:30:50 law offers the more accurate real-world shape: the heaviest 20% of buyers deliver about 50% of purchases, the lightest 50% of buyers deliver about 20% of purchases, and the middle 30% deliver the remaining 30%. The Pareto share also isn't a fixed brand property — it depends heavily on the length of the measurement window; short windows understate polarization (everyone looks similarly "light"), long windows overstate it (heavy buyers reveal their true frequency, and more incidental light buyers enter the count).

The strategic implication: if the lightest half of your buyers still deliver roughly a fifth of your sales, and the middle 30% deliver another third, ignoring anyone but your heaviest 20% throws away most of your volume. The traditional read of Pareto ("focus resources on the valuable 20%") is built on an exaggerated ratio and, even where true, ignores that what matters for growth isn't a buyer's current weight but their responsiveness to marketing and how many of them there are in total.

## 7. The Law of Buyer Moderation

A related and widely misunderstood statistical regularity: buyers classified as "heavy," "light," or "non-buyers" of a brand in one period will, on average, look markedly different in the next period — heavy buyers appear to buy less, light buyers appear to buy more, and some "non-buyers" turn out to buy after all — even when no one's underlying loyalty or preferences have actually changed. This is regression to the mean, driven by natural period-to-period "wobble" in individual purchase timing (a visit from relatives, a change in routine, simple randomness in when a repurchase falls). A two-year US tomato sauce panel study, for a brand with flat overall sales, showed 14% of one year's sales coming from households the brand's own team would have called "non-buyers" the year before, while the heaviest-buying group's share of volume fell from 43% to 34% — despite total brand volume staying essentially level.

The consequence for strategy: any attempt to build a marketing plan around currently-observed heavy buyers (e.g., "target our top 20% and ignore the rest") is chasing a moving target, because next period's "heaviest 20%" will substantially overlap with, but not be identical to, this period's, and a meaningful share of "non-buyers" will simply buy anyway without any marketing intervention aimed at them. This law is also the reason many loyalty-program evaluations mistakenly credit the program for what is actually just statistical reversion of previously-misclassified light buyers.

## 8. Why Light Buyers Matter More Than Marketers Think

Because of the NBD shape, every brand — even category giants — is overwhelmingly made up of light, occasional buyers, and this population is where most of the brand's growth potential and much of its current sales volume actually sits. Ultra-heavy buyers (Coca-Cola's "morning, noon, and night" drinkers) are comparatively easy to reach (the category is central to their lives) but also largely non-responsive to marketing — their consumption is a fixed habit that neither advertising nor promotion moves much, and it ends only through some major life event outside marketing's control.

Light buyers are the harder marketing problem for the opposite reason: it's hard to justify individualized spend on any one of them, yet collectively they matter enormously for two reasons — there are vastly more of them than heavy buyers, and because they buy so infrequently, they can easily forget the brand exists between purchase occasions. This is the buyer population that continuous reach, broad-based advertising, and wide physical availability are built to serve, and it is the segment most textbook "target marketing" approaches quietly write off.

## 9. Brand User Bases Are Nearly Identical (Segmentation Doesn't Work the Way Textbooks Say)

A large body of research spanning demographics, psychographics, media habits, and values consistently finds that competing brands within a category sell to nearly identical types of people. The customer bases differ mainly in size (how many buyers), not in composition. This has been documented for cars (UK, decades apart), beer (Canada), credit cards (UK), tourism destination choice (global survey — nearly identical stated motivations regardless of destination), and even in cases explicitly designed to attract a niche demographic (a baby-charity credit card in Australia expected to skew heavily female barely differed from the category norm; a chocolate bar explicitly test-marketed to men, Yorkie, was bought about as often by women).

Sharp names this the "I love my Mum" phenomenon for attitudes specifically: buyers of Brand A hold essentially the same opinions about Brand A that buyers of Brand B hold about Brand B — because attitudes largely track usage/loyalty (how much you buy a brand), not brand-specific truths about that brand's actual character. Even product variants marketed on an apparently obvious functional difference (unscented, low-allergy fabric conditioner) mostly sell to ordinary category buyers who occasionally feel like the variant, not to a dedicated niche of allergy sufferers — the double jeopardy law applies to variants too.

The strategic reframe: when your brand's customer base skews toward a particular demographic, the right question isn't "that's our target market" — it's "why are we failing to reach the rest of the market, and is this skew actually something to fix?" A brand whose customer base looks unusually different from its category's is more likely evidence of a marketing gap (missing distribution, missing mental availability among part of the population) than evidence of successful positioning. The positive framing: because your competitors' buyers are demographically just like yours, there is no structural reason your brand can't win more of them — your "niche" is really the whole category, constrained mainly by loyalty and competitor effort, not by some type of person who is permanently unreachable for you.

## 10. The Duplication of Purchase Law

A "duplication of purchase" table shows, for each brand in a category, what proportion of its buyers also bought each other brand during a given period. The consistent finding across categories: every brand shares customers with every other brand roughly in proportion to that other brand's market share — big brands are shared with heavily by everyone, small brands are shared with only lightly by everyone. In an ice cream category example, every brand (regardless of its own size or positioning) shared roughly 40% of its buyers with the category's largest brand, Carte D'Or, and comparatively few buyers with the smallest brand, Mars — a pattern that repeats almost identically across brand pairs once ranked by size.

This law is a direct empirical challenge to segmentation theory: if brands truly served separate, meaningfully different segments, sharing patterns would show strong partitions (brands clustering tightly with "their" segment-mates and barely overlapping with others). Instead, sharing tracks size almost mechanically. Perceptual maps — the standard tool for visualizing brand positioning — routinely exaggerate how partitioned a market really is, because the statistical techniques behind them (e.g., correspondence analysis) are specifically built to highlight differences and are sensitive to outliers; real purchase-overlap data for the same brands and buyers shows a much less segmented reality than the perceptual map implies. Where genuine partitions do appear (e.g., two brands sharing far more customers with each other than the law predicts), they nearly always trace back to a real, obvious functional difference (zero-sugar variants sharing more with each other; premium ice cream brands sharing more with each other) rather than to brand image or "positioning" as usually understood.

## 11. Market Partitions and Brand Portfolio Strategy

The duplication of purchase law gives marketers a consumer-behavior-based (rather than production-based) way to define what a "category" actually is, which matters because production-oriented category definitions (dividing chocolate into blocks, bars, pieces, wrapped, coated, etc., or breakfast cereal into "hot" and "cold") routinely blind managers to real competitors and understate real growth potential. Narrower category definitions flatter a brand's apparent market share (nobody wants to rank seventeenth) but artificially shrink the penetration opportunity that's actually available.

The law also predicts where a new product launch will cannibalize sales from (mostly from larger brands, roughly proportional to their size) and gives a benchmark for judging customer gains/losses to specific competitors — if you're losing unusually more customers to a particular rival than its size would predict, that signals some real overlap in marketing strategy or distribution worth investigating.

For portfolio management, the implication is: don't worry that a company's own brands overlap heavily in customer base or "tread on each other's toes" (Coke/Diet Coke, Mars Bar/Snickers, Tampax/Always) — this is the normal, expected pattern for any set of brands competing in the same category, and if a company could acquire any two major global soft drink brands, it should pick the two biggest (Coke and Pepsi) rather than two brands with supposedly complementary positioning (Coke and Fanta), because market-based assets (size, availability) matter more than manufactured differentiation between owned brands. What genuinely deserves worry is whether each brand is distinctive enough to be noticed and correctly attributed — and whether price promotions on one owned brand are cannibalizing full-price sales of a sibling brand more than intended. Decisions to discontinue a brand should rest on its standalone viability and cost structure, not on how similar it looks to another brand the company owns.

## 12. Polygamous Loyalty and the Natural Monopoly Law

Real buyers are "polygamously loyal" — they routinely maintain a personal repertoire of several brands within a category and move between them, rather than buying one brand exclusively. Genuinely 100%-loyal buyers exist but are a minority, and where they do appear in large numbers for a specific brand (e.g., Walkers crisps in the UK, with roughly a third of buyers 100% loyal over a year), this is explained by the brand's sheer market dominance and availability, not unusual emotional commitment — it's the double jeopardy law again, at the far end of the scale. This 100%-loyalty rate itself shrinks the longer the measurement window runs, because more time gives buyers more opportunities to reveal disloyalty.

The **Natural Monopoly Law**: bigger brands attract a disproportionate share of light category buyers — not because light buyers specifically prefer big brands, but as a pure statistical consequence of size. If you only buy one soft drink this year, the odds favor Coca-Cola; if you only buy one bag of crisps in the UK, the odds favor Walkers. Conversely, someone observed buying a small brand is statistically more likely to be a heavier-than-average category buyer overall. The strategic implication runs directly against "super-consumer" targeting fashions: chasing only the heaviest category buyers is, in effect, choosing to compete like a small brand, because growth requires recruiting the much larger pool of light category buyers who default to whichever brand is easiest to notice and obtain.

## 13. The Myth of Passionate, Emotionally-Bonded Brand Loyalty

Sharp directly challenges the marketing-and-academic fascination with "brand love," "lovemarks," and deep emotional consumer-brand relationships, using both the underlying survey data and case studies of brands most often cited as loyalty exemplars.

Apple and Harley-Davidson are the two most commonly invoked "proof" cases for passionate brand loyalty, yet the actual purchase data tells a more ordinary story. Harley-Davidson's share-of-category-requirements is only around 33% (Harley buyers buy other motorcycle brands twice as often as they buy Harleys) — a normal loyalty figure for its size. Segmentation research on US Harley owners found the single largest ownership segment (about 40% of owners, representing roughly half of Harley's revenue) reports low psychological attachment to the brand, describes the bike as "mostly parked," and doesn't identify with biker culture at all; the small, stereotypically "fanatical" segment (tattoos, heaviest accessory spend, least reading) represents under 10% of owners and only about 3.5% of revenue. Apple's computer repeat-purchase rate (roughly 33-55% across several studies, replicated a decade apart across three separate US panels) is only modestly above what double jeopardy would predict for its market share — explained largely by the real switching cost of changing operating systems and software, not by unusual emotional devotion.

Broader survey evidence backs this up: an "Emotional Branding Pays Off" study found only 4% of an average beer brand's male buyers agreed with a strong "I love this brand" statement, and even those few "lovers" bought other brands roughly half the time. A widely cited academic "Brand Love" study, when its methodology is examined closely, amounts to college students naming a favorite consumer electronics brand (overwhelmingly the iPod) and answering a battery of highly intercorrelated attitude questions — with the paper itself conceding, almost in passing, that "brands consumers really love are likely to be a small minority of the total brands they purchase." Every brand attracts a handful of genuine fanatics (Sharp's example: a Wisconsin man who has eaten more than 23,000 Big Macs), but these individuals are numerically trivial and not meaningfully more common for brands marketed as "loved" than for ordinary brands.

The book's alternative account of ordinary loyalty: brand loyalty is best understood as a natural, largely habitual buying strategy that balances risk and saves time and cognitive effort — not a market imperfection and not usually a passionate bond. A classic 1964 experiment (Tucker) showed women quickly forming loyalty-like preferences among identical, unbranded loaves of bread distinguished only by arbitrary letter labels, evidence that loyalty behavior emerges readily even without any real product or emotional difference to justify it. Attitudes and stated brand image beliefs are shown to be highly unstable at the individual level from one survey to the next (even though aggregate scores look stable) — people don't reliably hold fixed beliefs about brands; they intermittently recall or assert them, which is consistent with attitudes being a weak, largely reflective byproduct of buying behavior ("we like what we buy") rather than a strong driver of it.

## 14. Differentiation vs. Distinctiveness

This is the book's central strategic reframe, replacing the textbook obsession with "meaningful, perceived differentiation" as the source of brand preference and loyalty.

**The differentiation theory's core claims**, as taught in mainstream marketing texts: brands succeed by being genuinely, perceptibly different from competitors in a way buyers value and are motivated by (a "unique selling proposition"); undifferentiated new entrants are expected to fail; differentiation should show up as distinct market partitions, distinct user profiles, and above-average loyalty for differentiated brands.

**What the data actually shows**, across dozens of categories and countries: on average only around 10% of a brand's own regular buyers describe that brand as "different" or "unique" when asked directly — and this figure is fairly consistent whether measured with a pick-any question or a 5-point rating scale. Even Apple, repeatedly held up as differentiation's poster child, scores only modestly above other computer brands on this measure, and three-quarters of Apple buyers do not describe it as different or unique. Genuine outliers exist and are informative by contrast — Aldi (67% "different," reflecting a real, obvious structural difference: no national brands stocked) and Subway (50% "unique" among fast-food brands, reflecting its real sandwich-only positioning) — proving respondents can and do recognize differentiation when it's really there; most brands, most of the time, simply aren't perceived as differentiated by their own buyers, and this isn't a measurement artifact. Brand "personality" research (Aaker's scale and its successors) finds the same pattern: only a small minority of buyers associate any given personality trait with a brand, and those associations are themselves unstable from one interview to the next. Studies of "unique associations" across 130 brands and 13 categories found true single-brand-exclusive image associations only about 3% of the time, and uniqueness correlates simply with how few competitors a category has — not with brand strength or preference.

Sharp's synthesis: real differentiation exists (brands do have different names, functional features, and enormous situational differentiation — "this one has my size," "I know where this store is") but perceived, meaningful, brand-level differentiation of the textbook kind is weak, varies little between rival brands, and does not drive the loyalty or growth differences it's assumed to. If it did, it would directly contradict two laws already established elsewhere in the book: double jeopardy (loyalty should vary far more between brands if some are genuinely much more differentiated than others) and duplication of purchase (real differentiation should produce sharp market partitions, and it mostly doesn't).

**Distinctiveness is offered as the empirically supported alternative.** Its purpose is not to give buyers a reason to prefer the brand on the merits, but to make the brand easy to notice, correctly identify, and correctly attribute advertising to — reducing the buyer's cognitive load rather than persuading them of anything. Distinctive assets (a logo, color, tagline, character, celebrity association, or consistent advertising style) are legally protectable trademarks precisely because they identify source, unlike "meaningful differentiation," which is a much fuzzier, unprotectable concept. The consumer benefit is different in kind from differentiation's benefit: distinctiveness makes the buying decision effortless; differentiation (in theory) makes the buyer prefer you on the merits. Sharp's conclusion: branding practice should shift emphasis from hunting for unique selling propositions to building and rigorously protecting unique identifying characteristics.

## 15. Building and Valuing Distinctive Brand Assets

Two criteria determine how valuable a specific distinctive asset (a color, shape, tagline, character, sound, etc.) actually is, and both require real consumer research to assess rather than internal judgment:

- **Fame** — how many people, across the market, associate the brand with that asset at all.
- **Uniqueness** — of the people who do make that association, how many associate it with your brand only (rather than also or instead with a competitor).

Marketers and agencies routinely and substantially over-estimate both, which leads them to over-estimate how "branded" their advertising really is and how easy their product actually is to spot on shelf. An asset that's unique but not famous fails because too few people will recognize it as a trigger for the brand at all; an asset that's famous but not unique risks reminding people of a competitor instead — actively working against the brand that paid for it.

Distinctive assets must be *learned* over time through sustained, consistent exposure — building the link between an element and a brand name typically takes years to decades (Nike's swoosh, introduced in the 1970s, needed a long period paired with the brand name before it could function alone). Consistency has to operate across two axes that are often confused: consistency across media within a single campaign (widely practiced, thanks to integrated marketing communications thinking) and consistency across campaigns and over time (much rarer, and much more important, since a brand's many light, infrequent buyers barely register any single campaign and are easily confused by discontinuity — a new-look campaign that abandons prior branding elements effectively looks, to most of the audience, like an ad for an unrelated brand). Packaging changes are named as a particularly common way brands unintentionally sabotage their own distinctive assets and trigger unexpected sales drops.

The research emphasis this implies: study a color's or symbol's fame and uniqueness for your specific brand, not (as most brand research does) which colors people find aesthetically appealing in the abstract — the value of "owning" blue isn't that people like blue, it's that consistent, exclusive use of blue lets that color substitute for the brand name itself in any communication.

## 16. How Advertising Actually Works: Memory, Not Persuasion

The dominant mechanism by which advertising affects sales, per the book's synthesis of decades of single-source panel research, is by refreshing — and much more rarely, building — memory structures associated with a brand: what it does, what it looks like, where and when it's bought and used, who uses it. This is fundamentally different from the traditional model in which advertising informs, persuades, and shifts attitude toward a purchase intention. Instead, advertising's main effect is closer to "opening people's eyes" so a brand has a better chance of actually being noticed at the point of purchase — nudging an already-existing but very low purchase probability slightly upward, for a very large number of people, rather than dramatically converting a smaller number of people through persuasion.

Because most category purchases happen infrequently and advertising exposures are scattered across a long stretch of time before the next relevant purchase occasion, advertising's sales effect is thinly spread and very hard to see in short-term sales figures — unlike price promotions, whose effect is concentrated entirely in the promotion window and therefore easy to measure. Sharp's central diagnostic point: never mix advertising and price-promotion effects in the same econometric model, because their time signatures are structurally different (advertising: small, delayed, spread over a long horizon; price promotion: large, immediate, fully contained within the discount period). This is also why brand advertising is frequently and wrongly judged a failure by looking for a visible bump in next week's sales.

A parallel foundational fact, established early in the book (Chapter 1) and repeatedly referenced: in a large Australian TV study, only about 40% of viewers who had the opportunity to see a given ad actually noticed it, and of those, only about 40% correctly attributed it to the right brand — meaning roughly 84% of advertising exposures are wasted on the two most basic requirements (being noticed at all, and being correctly branded) before any question of persuasion or memorability even arises.

## 17. Mental Availability's Mechanics — Beyond Simple "Awareness"

Mental availability (also called brand salience) is defined as a brand's propensity to be noticed and/or come to mind in buying situations — a broader and more useful concept than standard "brand awareness" metrics (which typically measure only a single cue, like unprompted recall against the category name, or recognition of the brand name itself). Mental availability is better modeled as a network of memory associations linking a brand to many different possible retrieval cues: a branch location, a use-occasion, a color, a feeling, a person you know who uses it, and so on.

Two properties determine the strength of this network: **quantity** (how many distinct associations a buyer holds for the brand) and **quality**, which itself has two components — the *strength* of each association (how reliably it triggers the brand when encountered) and its *relevance* (how likely that cue is to actually occur in a real buying situation). A memory link can be strong but irrelevant to buying (Sharp's deliberately odd example: some people strongly associate "Elvis Presley" with peanut butter sandwiches, but that cue rarely arises while shopping for peanut butter). Buyers also use different, sometimes quite abstract, retrieval cues at different times — "something healthy," "something quick," a color, a pack shape — and rarely stick to one consistent criterion, which is why segmentation research built around a single fixed "need state" tends to misrepresent how buyers actually behave.

This reframes competition around **"cue competitors"** rather than category look-alikes: whichever brands or options are linked to the cue a buyer happens to be using in the moment are the real competitive set for that occasion — which can cut across conventional category lines entirely (a coffee, a Red Bull, a brisk walk, and a swim can all compete as answers to "something to wake me up").

Building and protecting a brand's distinctive assets (see Section 15) is what allows advertising to correctly refresh the right brand's memory structures rather than being screened out entirely or misattributed to a competitor.

---

## 18. Physical Availability

Physical availability means making a brand as easy as possible to notice and buy, for as many people as possible, in as many potential buying situations as possible — encompassing not just retail distribution (percentage of stores stocking the brand) but in-store prominence, hours of availability, and ease of completing the purchase (including online). A documented empirical pattern (a strongly convex, "reverse-L-shaped" relationship between weighted distribution and market share, observed across countries and categories, and over time for single brands) shows that gains in distribution deliver disproportionately large share gains once a brand reaches very broad distribution — partly because the last few points of distribution are often in low-competition environments (vending machines, convenience stores), and partly because bigger, more available brands are also allocated more shelf space where they're already stocked.

Crucially, high physical availability is necessary for high market share but does not guarantee it — plenty of widely-distributed brands still have low sales, which points to a mental availability deficit, a price/quality mismatch, or limited underlying appeal, rather than a distribution problem. Sharp is skeptical of marketers who claim "practically 100% availability" based on broad retail listing; the real standard he holds up is something closer to the famous Coca-Cola aim of being "no more than an arm's length away."

## 19. Mental and Physical Availability as Market-Based Assets

Mental and physical availability, together with a brand's distinctive assets, function as genuine market-based assets: they cost money and time to build, they can be bought and sold (part of what makes an acquired brand valuable), and they provide a real degree of future-sales security, because they take a long time to build and also a long time to fade — which is precisely why large, established brands show muted, sluggish reactions to short-term marketing changes (harder-to-detect price elasticities, harder-to-detect advertising effects) compared to small brands, where the same intervention produces a clean, visible reaction.

This creates a specific, repeatable opportunity: a brand that once had strong mental and physical availability but has been neglected (advertising switched off, distribution allowed to lapse) can often be revived quickly and cheaply, because the underlying memory structures and brand recognition haven't fully decayed — Sharp's examples include a small, dormant Australian wine label (Queen Adelaide) relaunched with a modern chardonnay and immediately becoming the country's best-selling chardonnay, and McDonald's recovering share in the 2000s not through genuine innovation but through unglamorous "catch-up" moves (salads, McCafé espresso coffee, comfortable seating) that simply removed accumulated reasons not to buy and let its existing, still-substantial market-based assets do the rest of the work.

A practical planning tool offered for auditing marketing activity is a "balance sheet" that sorts tactics by whether they build or merely spend down these assets: broadening distribution, gaining new distribution channels, consistent use of distinctive assets, consistent advertising, and broad-reaching media are classed as very likely to build mental/physical availability; coupons, price promotions, packaging changes, loyalty programs, competitions, and bursty/pulsed advertising schedules are classed as low-reach and unlikely to build these assets; and things like advertising containing genuinely new information, limited-edition variants, comparative advertising, and "hidden brand name" suspense advertising are classed as unknown or risky.

## 20. The NBD-Dirichlet Model

The NBD-Dirichlet (Goodhardt, Ehrenberg & Chatfield, 1984) is the mathematical model that ties the whole empirical picture together: it treats brands within a category as effectively undifferentiated choice options of varying popularity, and from that single starting assumption it successfully predicts the observed patterns for penetration, purchase frequency, 100%-loyalty rates, and duplication of purchase across dozens of product categories, multiple countries, and extended time periods. Its predictive success across such varied conditions is presented as the strongest piece of evidence in the book that brands genuinely compete primarily as differently-sized, largely substitutable alternatives — because a model built on that premise keeps successfully forecasting real-world buying metrics that a strong-differentiation view of the world would not be able to explain nearly as well.

## 21. The Sales Response to Price Promotions

Price promotions produce a real, visible, and immediate spike in unit sales, but the underlying mechanism and its limits are well documented. Ehrenberg, Hammond & Goodhardt (1994) found that almost everyone who buys a brand during a promotion had already bought that brand before — promotions overwhelmingly recruit existing buyers, not new ones. Where promotions do reach infrequent buyers of the brand, the effect is a one-off pull-forward: those buyers purchase during the discount window and then simply resume their normal (low) buying rate afterward, exactly as if the promotion had never happened — the "purchase reinforcement effect" that some marketers hope for essentially does not appear in the data. As one team of researchers summarized it: "price promotions produce only temporary benefits for established brands."

On negative after-effects: the "reference price effect" (a discount lowering a consumer's expectation of the normal price, dampening willingness to pay full price afterward) is real but has to be reconciled with equally strong evidence that most consumers have quite poor recall of actual prices paid (only about half of intercepted US shoppers, and about a third of French shoppers, could recall a price to within 5% accuracy). The reconciliation: consumers hold fuzzy, zone-based price knowledge and are more attuned to *relative* price signals between brands than to precise absolute prices — so a single promotion has little lasting effect, but *repeated, frequent* promotion measurably raises consumers' price sensitivity and price salience over time, per several US studies. Competitor over-reaction to price moves (documented empirically) is a further risk, illustrated by the Greyhound/Peter Pan Trailways bus-fare price war, where a fare fell from $25 to $4 (below its level 40 years earlier) over roughly three weeks.

## 22. Price Elasticity: What Actually Drives It

Average price elasticity for temporary promotions clusters consistently around -2 to -4 across independent large studies (Danaher & Brodie: -2.3 across 26 categories; Scriven & Ehrenberg: -2.6; Steenkamp et al.: -4.0 across 442 categories), with most individual brand elasticities falling between roughly -1 and -4, though outliers exist. Elasticity, however, is not a fixed brand property — Scriven and Ehrenberg identified five specific contextual factors that systematically raise it:

- **Crossing a competitor's price** — switching happens far more when a brand's price moves past (not just narrows toward) a specific rival's price, and the effect is bigger the larger that rival brand is. Pricing just below the market leader can outperform either deep undercutting or simply narrowing the gap without crossing it.
- **Explicit price signaling** — clearly flagging a price cut (signage, "was $X now $Y," feature advertising) massively amplifies the sales effect, sometimes lifting sales 200–500% versus an unsignaled shelf price change alone.
- **Small brand share** — elasticity is mechanically larger for small brands (the same percentage-point share gain represents a much larger relative jump for a small brand than a large one), so small brands see bigger promotional uplifts but also suffer more, proportionally, from price rises.
- **Price increases vs. decreases** — increases have measurably bigger volume effects than equivalent-sized decreases, consistent with loss aversion; this asymmetry is even sharper for private-label brands.
- **Proximity to the category's average price** — a brand priced close to the category average shows bigger elasticity than one priced well above or below it, because a price move is more likely to flip its ranking relative to nearby competitors (urban gasoline pricing, where stations cluster tightly, is the clearest real-world illustration).

Category-level factors that independently raise elasticity: fewer brands in the category, goods bought very regularly, and goods that can be stockpiled.

## 23. The Profitability Math of Price Cuts

Because a price cut reduces the contribution margin on every unit sold — including the large "baseline" volume that would have sold at full price anyway — even a very large volume increase can fail to produce any additional profit. A 45%-deep price cut (with feature/display support) can drive roughly 280–400% more volume while simultaneously wiping out nearly the entire contribution margin, meaning the brand is effectively selling near cost. The exact break-even point depends on three factors: the brand's normal contribution margin, the depth of the cut, and its price elasticity. As a rule of thumb, a brand with a 30% margin needs a 50% volume increase just to break even on a 10% price cut; a brand with a 50% margin needs only a 25% volume increase to break even on the same cut — meaning shallower discounts (management restraint) generally protect profit better than deep ones, even when deep cuts generate more headline volume. A further, frequently ignored cost: sales made during a promotion that will be matched by competitor retaliation represent full-price future sales that will now simply never occur.

## 24. Comparing Price Promotion and Advertising as Growth Levers

Price cuts have a large, direct, easily measured effect but very restricted reach (limited to buyers already in-market for the category during the promotion window, and — for in-store-only promotions — limited further to shoppers of that specific retailer that week). Advertising has the opposite profile: broad reach, but a very small, hard-to-measure direct effect per individual, working through slowly-accumulating memory structures rather than an immediate purchase trigger. Advertising is also argued to have the more durable long-term effect, because it works on underlying propensity to buy rather than a temporary price incentive that evaporates the moment the discount ends. The reach math for in-store promotions is often much lower than managers assume: a promotion run in a retailer with 10% market share reaches only 10% of that retailer's category-buying customers that week — a small fraction of the category's total buyer base. Given a straight choice between a promotion emphasizing deep price cuts versus one emphasizing broad retailer advertising support, the book recommends the latter, since it behaves more like ordinary reach-building advertising and can be paired with a shallower, less margin-destructive discount.

## 25. Why Loyalty Programs Underperform

Direct empirical study of large-scale loyalty programs (a major Australian program studied by the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute; replications in France, the Netherlands, and financial services) consistently finds real but very weak effects on customer loyalty, and no evidence linking loyalty programs to outstanding business results — a striking gap given how widespread and expensive these programs are.

The structural reason: loyalty programs recruit disproportionately from a brand's *existing* buyers, not from new customers, for two compounding reasons. First, mental and physical availability bias recruitment — more frequent shoppers/buyers simply encounter the sign-up opportunity (usually in-store) far more often by pure chance. Second, the economic incentive to join is strongest for buyers who are already loyal, since they can see they'll be rewarded for behavior they were going to do anyway ("something for nothing"). A simple 2x2 framework captures the resulting problem: category-light/brand-low-loyalty buyers (the largest group, and the most numerous non-joiners) are the biggest pool but least likely to join; category-heavy/brand-low-loyalty buyers are the most desirable recruits (real behavior change is possible) but are rare joiners; both high-loyalty quadrants are likely to join but have little room to change behavior, meaning the program pays out rewards for no behavioral shift. Empirical loyalty-program membership data confirms this: buying rates for program members and non-members are nearly identical across supermarkets, fuel retail, department stores, credit cards, and telecoms — loyalty programs are shown to be good at recruiting existing brand buyers (heavy and light alike) and poor at recruiting heavy category buyers who are not already brand customers.

Studies attempting to detect a genuine loyalty-program effect must also control for the law of buyer moderation (Section 7): comparing a customer's later behavior against their earlier, mis-classified baseline behavior will spuriously make it look as if the program increased loyalty, when much of the apparent shift is just regression to the mean. Firms are also documented to be unusually reluctant to shut down underperforming loyalty programs, due to consumer resistance to losing accumulated (over-valued) points, contractual/legal lock-in, and sunk-cost management attachment to the program.

## 26. The Seven Rules for Growing a Brand

Sharp's practitioner-facing synthesis of the whole book, offered as an applied growth checklist:

1. **Reach.** Continuously reach every buyer of the category — not just current brand buyers — with both physical distribution and marketing communication. Avoid narrow target-market definitions that don't match who actually buys the brand; most of a brand's growth potential sits with light and non-buyers, not with the existing loyal core.
2. **Be easy to buy.** Physical and mental availability drive share because they reduce the effort required to buy the brand, for more people, in more situations. This requires real research into how people actually buy and where "reasons not to buy" (missing pack sizes, inconvenient stores, unclear web checkout) are quietly suppressing sales — "convenience" is not a self-evident concept and has to be empirically investigated per category.
3. **Get noticed.** Reach and availability are worthless if the brand isn't actually noticed — on shelf, in the street, or in an ad. Consumers actively and passively screen out most marketing; creative work exists to earn attention, and likeable, emotionally engaging advertising measurably improves both attention and correct brand attribution.
4. **Refresh and build memory structures.** Noticed advertising still fails if it doesn't reinforce useful memory links to the brand. New brands must establish the basics (what it is, what it looks like, where it's sold); established brands need continual refreshing of existing links rather than chasing a new positioning each year — even new product information should still "tell the old story" for the brand.
5. **Create and use distinctive brand assets.** Branding lets natural buyer habits (loyalty) attach to something recognizable, and lets advertising correctly refresh the right brand's memory rather than a rival's. Consistent, deliberately protected assets (colors, characters, sonic branding, taglines) function as "coat hangers" other associations can form on.
6. **Be consistent, yet fresh.** A brand cannot be distinctive without consistency. Long-lived brands typically got that way by staying recognizable over decades, not by repeatedly repositioning. Packaging changes are named as a particularly common way brands unintentionally damage their own recognizability among the large population of light, infrequent buyers who barely register any single change.
7. **Stay competitive — don't give people a reason not to buy.** Because buyer evaluation happens mostly through a small, largely pre-filtered consideration set, "reasons not to buy" (an unfashionable ingredient, an uncompetitive price, negative publicity) can do more damage than "reasons to buy" can help, since a buyer only weighs positive features among the tiny set of brands they've already failed to screen out. Excessive over-pricing is specifically flagged as self-defeating: it creates a reason to reject the brand outright, encourages private-label competition, and cannot be fixed by occasional discounting alone.

The chapter's closing framing: there is no single "magic key" to growth, and every genuine growth strategy (product/quality improvement, price cuts, or investment in market-based assets) trades off against margin in the short run — but only investment in mental and physical availability compounds, because those assets, once built, make every future pound of marketing spend more productive. Market penetration is recommended as the single best proxy metric for tracking whether these assets are actually growing, precisely because raw sales figures can be distorted by promotions and other short-term tactics aimed at the existing loyal base.

## 27. The Complete List of Named Empirical Laws

Sharp's own closing summary names the specific, reusable "laws" the book establishes, several of which are elaborated above but are gathered here as the book's own canonical list:

- **Double Jeopardy (and its retention/defection form):** brand size drives both penetration and loyalty-type metrics (including defection rates) in a consistent, proportional way — bigger brands lose more customers in absolute terms but a smaller percentage of their base.
- **Pareto Law, corrected (roughly 60/20, not 80/20):** a brand's top 20% of buyers typically deliver a little over half of sales, not the mythical 80%.
- **Law of Buyer Moderation:** buyers classified as heavy, light, or non-buyers in one period regress toward the category/brand norm in the next period, absent any real underlying behavior change — a statistical artifact of period-to-period variation in purchase timing.
- **Natural Monopoly Law:** bigger brands carry a disproportionate share of light category buyers in their customer base, purely as a function of their size.
- **Customer bases seldom vary:** competing brands within a category sell to nearly identical types of buyers, differing mainly in the size of that buyer base.
- **Attitudes reflect behavioral loyalty:** buyers know and say more about brands they use and little about brands they don't; larger brands therefore always score higher on attitude/image surveys simply because they have more (and slightly more frequent) buyers among respondents.
- **Usage drives attitude ("I love my Mum"):** buyers of different, competing brands report strikingly similar attitudes and perceptions about their own respective brands.
- **Duplication of Purchase Law:** a brand's customer base overlaps with every other brand in the category roughly in proportion to that other brand's market share.

Sharp adds a short applied FAQ addressing common practitioner objections to this whole framework: that the laws apply equally to small brands and large ones (a small brand simply faces the same "mostly light or non-buyers" reality at a smaller scale); that even a brand near 100% annual penetration still has enormous room to grow within shorter time windows (quarter or month), because the underlying NBD-style skew reasserts itself at any measurement interval; that "everyone who buys the category" is, with due allowance for current physical availability, the brand's real target market — multiple owned brands or SKUs in one category legitimately share the same target market, which simplifies (and reduces the cost of) research; that broad, mass-reaching creative can still be distinctive and resonant without being customized per segment, in the same way globally successful storytelling (his example: Disney) crosses age, culture, and background; that reaching light buyers is not prohibitively expensive — mass media and mass retail remain, in his words, in a "golden age" of low-cost per-person reach; and that market partitions are not simply another name for segments — partitions describe brands that happen to compete somewhat more directly with each other, which may or may not track a demographic pattern, and are usually mild enough that they rarely justify a dedicated separate SKU or sub-brand.
