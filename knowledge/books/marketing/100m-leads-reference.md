# $100M Leads — Framework Reference

*Pure framework reference distilled from the book. No commentary, no application notes — just the frameworks, models, and mental tools, explained in original language.*

*Source: $100M Leads: How to Get Strangers To Want To Buy Your Stuff by Alex Hormozi*

---

## 1. The Core Growth Model

There are only two ways to grow any business: get more customers, or get existing customers to buy more/more often. Getting more customers means getting more leads — the entire book is built on one governing idea: doubling your leads doubles your business, all else held constant.

The core funnel is: **Leads → Engaged Leads → Buyers → Repeat Buyers.** A lead is simply any person you can contact — a name in your phone, an address on a list, a stranger on the street, as long as you have a way to reach them. An engaged lead is a lead who has shown interest in what you're selling. Engaged leads, not impressions or followers or reach, are the true output of advertising. Every method in the book exists to turn strangers into leads, leads into engaged leads, and engaged leads into buyers.

---

## 2. The Core Four Channels

Every method of getting attention from another human being reduces to two binary choices: do you reach people who already know you (warm) or strangers (cold), and do you do it one-to-one privately (outreach) or one-to-many publicly (content/ads). Crossing those two axes produces exactly four channels — nothing else is available to a single advertiser:

- **Warm Outreach** — contacting people who already know you, one at a time
- **Content** — posting publicly, for free, to reach many people at once
- **Cold Outreach** — contacting strangers who don't know you, one at a time
- **Paid Ads** — paying to reach many strangers at once

The recommended sequencing for a new business: start Warm Outreach on day one (zero cost, fastest proof of concept), run Content in parallel from day one (slow to compound, so delaying it is a permanent cost), add Cold Outreach once Warm Outreach has proven the offer works, and add Paid Ads last, only once the offer and message are already validated. Paid Ads amplify what already works — they don't fix an offer that doesn't convert.

## 3. The Value Equation, Applied to Lead Gen

Every offer — including a free lead magnet — is judged by prospects on the same four levers, two to maximize and two to minimize:

- **Dream Outcome** (maximize) — what they want, in the exact way they want it
- **Perceived Likelihood of Achievement** (maximize) — how confident they are they'll actually get it
- **Time Delay** (minimize) — how long before they see results
- **Effort & Sacrifice** (minimize) — what they have to give up or do

The book's compressed offer template for stating this to a prospect: "I help [avatar] get [dream outcome] in [time period] without [effort/sacrifice]," then adding proof to raise perceived likelihood. Ways to raise perceived likelihood include your own transformation story, proof from people in a directly comparable situation, sheer volume of documented outcomes, third-party credentials, and live demonstrations or guarantees.

When a prospect says no, the real blocker is usually a **hidden cost** — an unstated time, effort, or risk concern, not the stated price objection. Asking "what would have to be true for this to be worth it to you?" surfaces the actual objection, which either tells you what to fix in your offer or what to say differently to the next prospect.

## 4. Warm Outreach — The Ten-Step Process

Warm Outreach is contacting people who already know you and asking not "do you want to buy" but whether they know someone who might want the result you offer. The ten-step process:

1. **Build your list** — compile every contact across every platform you're on
2. **Pick a platform** — start with whichever has the most contacts
3. **Personalize the opener** — reference something real and specific about the person, 2-3 sentences
4. **Reach out in volume** — a benchmark target of 100 attempts per day
5. **Warm them up before pitching** — use the ACA framework (below) rather than pitching immediately
6. **Ask for the referral, not the sale** — "do you know anyone who...", not "do you want to..."
7. **Make the ask trivially easy to accept** — the lowest-friction version of your offer (e.g. free first uses)
8. **Prioritize your best contacts first**
9. **Start charging** — once early free/donation-based clients get results, transition them to paying customers, then begin charging new prospects directly rather than working for free
10. **Keep the list warm over time** — periodic value delivery, then a low-friction re-engagement message

A specific re-engagement tool is the **nine-word email** (credited to Dean Jackson): "Are you still looking to [4-word desire]?" — sent to a warm list to surface who is ready to act now; the replies become your priority outreach list.

**The ACA framework** for warming up a reply before pitching:
- **Acknowledge** — restate what they said, in your own words, to show you're listening
- **Compliment** — connect what they said to a genuine positive trait
- **Ask** — ask another question that steers the conversation toward the underlying problem you solve

People enjoy talking about themselves and being complimented; ACA builds trust quickly while keeping you in control of where the conversation goes.

**Getting more cash up front:** offering a guarantee only to prospects who pay in full up front (rather than on a payment plan) increases both commitment and cash collected — framed to the prospect as a choice: "would you rather pay less today [payment plan, no guarantee] or get all your money back [pay up front, with a guarantee]?" Most people choose the up-front option once it's framed this way.

**Volume rule ("Count in 100s"):** don't set a daily outreach goal below 100 attempts, and don't stop before 100 days straight — the book states plainly that 100 reach-outs a day for 100 days will produce new engaged leads.

## 5. Content — The Content Unit

Free content works because the audience it builds, not any single piece of content, is the actual compounding asset. Content itself disappears; the audience that discovered you through it keeps growing and keeps being reachable for free, forever, unlike each individual paid or one-to-one outreach attempt.

Every unit of content, regardless of length or format, does three things in sequence — the **Hook, Retain, Reward** structure:

**Hook (get attention).** The measure of a hook's effectiveness is what percentage of people who see it choose to consume more. Topic selection draws from five categories of life material: Far Past (life lessons, told as story rather than lecture), Recent Past (what happened this week — meetings, conversations, observations), Present (ideas as they occur, captured immediately), Trending (your expertise applied to what's currently being discussed), and Manufactured (content you deliberately create — highest effort, highest ceiling). Headlines draw their pull from seven components, usable individually or combined: Recency, Relevancy, Celebrity, Proximity, Conflict, Unusual, and Ongoing (an unresolved story).

**Retain (keep attention through the middle).** The core retention driver is curiosity — an unresolved question the audience wants answered. Three structural tools create and sustain it: **Lists** (loosely ordered items under a theme), **Steps** (a fixed, sequential process), and **Stories** (events with a lesson, carrying the strongest emotional pull). These can be layered — a story containing steps, a list of short stories — and the more layers of unresolved tension, the longer attention is retained.

**Reward (deliver on the hook's promise).** Content is "good" exactly to the degree it satisfies the expectation the hook created. The audience, not the creator, is the judge; growth is the feedback signal that reward is landing.

**The Give-Ask Ratio.** Mature media platforms run roughly 3-5 units of pure value for every 1 promotional ask (e.g., a feed showing about four organic posts per one ad). A business trying to grow rather than merely sustain an audience should skew further toward giving, since the more an audience is rewarded without being asked for anything, the faster it grows — captured in the instruction to "give until they ask," meaning give so consistently that the audience itself starts requesting a way to pay you. The practical rule is to **give in public, ask in private**, letting the audience self-select into your outreach or offers rather than pitching everyone publicly.

Two structures for weaving in promotion: **Integrated** (a brief ask embedded within a longer piece, e.g. a short ad inside a long podcast, appropriate for long-form platforms) and **Intermittent** (occasional dedicated promotional posts between a majority of pure-value posts, appropriate for short-form platforms). Only one call to action should be made at a time — asking for shares, follows, and purchases simultaneously produces none of them.

Two sequencing strategies for scaling across platforms: **Depth then Width** (maximize one platform fully before adding a second — faster compounding, higher single-channel risk) and **Width then Depth** (establish presence on every relevant platform first, then maximize all simultaneously — broader reach and content-repurposing efficiency, at a higher labor cost).

## 6. Cold Outreach

Cold Outreach is contacting strangers who match your target avatar, one at a time, without the benefit of an existing relationship. Unlike warm outreach, trust doesn't exist yet, so the order of problems to solve is: get a way to contact them, figure out what to say, then contact them repeatedly until they're ready and able to listen. It is inherently a volume game — the book's own benchmark math, from real campaigns:

- **Phone:** 100 cold calls/day, ~20% pick-up rate, ~25% of those take the lead magnet → roughly 4 engaged leads for that day's calling.
- **Email:** 100 personalized emails/day, ~30% open rate, ~10% reply rate → roughly 3 engaged leads (3% of the list).
- A cold-calling team example: paying $15/hour plus $50 per shown appointment, with leads costing $0.10 each, 200 calls/day yielding ~2 shows/day, works out to about $120 cost per show; at a 33% close rate and $3,600 profit per sale, that's roughly a 10:1 return.

Personalization (referencing something specific and real about the prospect) is what gets a cold message read at all — one tested rewrite of a cold outreach script to below a third-grade reading level produced a 50% lift in response rate. Follow-up matters more than the first message: contacting someone multiple times, multiple ways, communicates seriousness and increases response. The call to action should read as a genuine, low-pressure question rather than a pitch — asking whether something would be useful to the recipient, not asking them to buy.

## 7. Paid Ads

Paid Ads should only be introduced once an offer has already been proven to convert through warm and/or cold outreach — ads amplify an existing message rather than fix a weak one. The recommended sequence within paid advertising itself is to start with retargeting a warm audience before spending on cold traffic, and to spend more testing effort on the creative/hook than on the landing page, since the hook determines how many people ever reach the page at all.

**Three phases of scaling paid ad spend:**
- **Track Money** — establish accurate cost and return tracking before increasing spend
- **Lose Money** (deliberately, briefly) — spend to acquire data and initial customers even at a short-term loss, because the resulting customer and performance data is what makes profitable scaling possible
- **Print Money** — once the offer and targeting are proven and tracked, scale spend as the ratio holds

**Client-Financed Acquisition** is the fix when ad spend isn't profitable on the front end: restructure the offer (e.g. pricing, payment terms, or an order bump/immediate upsell) so that the cash collected from a new customer at or near the point of sale covers the cost of acquiring them, removing the need to fund acquisition purely from existing capital.

## 8. Lead Magnets — The Seven-Step Creation Framework

A lead magnet is a complete solution to one narrow problem, offered free or nearly free, whose resolution reveals a further problem that your core paid offer solves — the **Problem-Solution Cycle**. People give time before they give money, so getting someone to spend time on your lead magnet is a genuine, if smaller, commitment that predicts later willingness to pay.

**Step 1 — Pick the problem and who it's for.** Choose narrowly and make sure solving it surfaces exactly the next problem your paid offer addresses.

**Step 2 — Choose the lead magnet type.** Three types: a magnet that **reveals** a problem the prospect didn't realize they had (works best when the problem worsens with delay); a **sample or trial** offering full but limited access to the real product (works for recurring solutions to recurring problems); or **one step of a larger process** given away free while the remaining steps are sold (works when the core offer is inherently multi-step). The strongest lead magnets combine all three at once — described as building a "dressing room": trying something on (trial), discovering a related need (reveal), while being partway through outfitting a full solution (one step of many).

**Step 3 — Choose the delivery method.** Four vehicles, each compatible with all three types above: **Software** (a tool that does the job for the prospect), **Information** (teaches something — courses, guides, checklists), **Service** (you personally perform work for free), and **Physical product** (something tangible). Three types crossed with four delivery methods produce up to twelve distinct lead-magnet variants for a single problem, worth testing against each other.

**Step 4 — Test the name.** The headline/name of a lead magnet must be noticed before it can be consumed, so it is tested first, ahead of images or sub-headlines — via polls, posts, or direct pre-launch messages, watching for people proactively asking when it will be available.

**Step 5 — Make it effortless to consume.** Deliver in every format the audience might prefer (e.g., text, audio, and video versions of the same information) and remove every point of friction between wanting it and getting it.

**Step 6 — Make it genuinely good.** The market judges a free offering exactly as it judges a paid one; a weak free lead magnet signals a weak paid offer and drives prospects to competitors. The operating principle: give away the secret, sell the implementation — the lead magnet should be valuable enough that the creator could plausibly have charged for it.

**Step 7 — Make the next step obvious.** A strong call to action states clearly what to do next and gives a reason to act now, built on scarcity (a real, natural supply limit), urgency (a real time limit), or simply any explicit reason at all — research cited in the book found people act more readily when given any stated reason, even a weak one, than when given none.

## 9. The Engagement-to-Offer Sequence

Once a prospect has engaged with a lead magnet or piece of content, the sequence that carries them toward a purchase: deliver the promised value immediately; follow with one or two further pieces of pure value with no ask attached; introduce the specific problem your paid offer solves, better than any alternative; then make the offer. The book's guidance is that most sequences move to the ask too quickly — nurturing over a longer stretch of days or weeks, not hours, produces better conversion than rushing the ask.

---

## 10. Content-to-Sales Handoff

High-performing content becomes a reusable sales asset: pieces that get strong engagement are catalogued by the specific objection or problem they address, then sent by salespeople before or after calls to pre-empt objections the prospect hasn't even raised yet. Content also functions as a retention tool after the sale — a paying customer who continues consuming free content develops stronger affinity and stays longer, independent of the paid product's own performance.

---

## 11. Get Lead Getters — The Internal Core Four (Employees)

Beyond the four external channels for reaching customers directly, the book extends the same Core Four logic inward: employees, agencies, affiliates, and referrers can each become a source of leads, using the same warm/cold, outreach/content structure applied internally rather than externally. Employees generate leads when they are systematically asked to (e.g., referring candidates, customers, or partners from their own networks), trained on how to do it, and compensated for the leads they produce — treated as a distinct, measurable channel rather than an informal hope.

---

## 12. Referrals — The One Question

The book frames referral generation around a single governing habit: **always ask.** The recommended timing is to ask immediately after a customer experiences a win or a positive result, when satisfaction is highest — "you just got [result], who else do you know who wants that?" — rather than waiting for a natural mention. Making the ask easy (providing exact language the referrer can forward) and, where appropriate, incentivizing it (commission, credit, or a gift) both raise response rates. Referral leads are framed as effectively free customer acquisition layered on top of whatever paid or organic channels are already running, which mechanically improves overall acquisition-cost ratios without any additional spend.

---

## 13. Agencies — Buy, Build, or Both

On outsourcing lead generation to an agency, the book frames the decision as a build-versus-buy trade-off rather than a universal recommendation either way: agencies can compress the time to a working channel but require careful vetting, since agencies are themselves marketing their own capability and their claims should be evaluated with the same Value Equation lens (dream outcome, likelihood, time delay, effort) applied to any other offer being purchased.

---

## 14. Affiliates — The Six-Step Army-Building Process

Building an affiliate channel (partners who generate leads or sales in exchange for compensation) follows six steps: **find** the right affiliate profile (people already in front of your exact avatar); **make them an offer** that clearly states what's in it for them; **qualify** prospective affiliates before onboarding them, rather than accepting all comers; decide **what they get paid for** (leads, sales, or a hybrid); decide **how much they get paid**, calibrated so the economics work for both sides; and support/equip them on an ongoing basis so their output compounds rather than decaying after the first push. Affiliate arrangements are presented as a scalable way to convert other people's existing warm audiences into a lead source, without directly employing the people doing the outreach.

---

## 15. More, Better, New — Scaling an Existing Channel

Once a channel is producing any results, the book proposes a fixed order of operations for growing it further, applied to whichever of the Core Four is already working: first get **More** of what's already being done (simply increase volume in the same channel with the same method); then get **Better** at it (improve conversion rates, creative, or targeting within the same volume); only then look for **New** — a genuinely different channel or method. The rationale is that most operators abandon a channel and search for a new one before they've exhausted the compounding returns available from doing more, and doing it better, in the channel they already have.

---

## 16. The Referral Growth Equation

The book frames referrals mathematically as a multiplier on existing acquisition spend rather than a separate program: because a referral typically costs nothing or very little to generate compared to a paid or cold-outreach acquisition, even a modest referral rate (e.g., an extra quarter to a third of paying customers on top of whatever a paid channel already produces) meaningfully improves the blended cost-to-acquire across the whole customer base, since the "free" referred customers pull down the average cost per customer earned through all channels combined.

---

## 17. Ongoing Value and Long-Term List Health

Beyond any single campaign, the book stresses maintaining "on-going value" with a list or audience over time — continuing to deliver free value to people who have not yet bought, rather than treating anyone who doesn't convert immediately as a dead lead. A previously unresponsive contact re-engaged later, after continued value delivery, converts at meaningfully higher rates than a genuinely cold contact, which is the underlying justification for never fully abandoning a warm list.

---

## 18. Execution Priorities — What to Do Right Now

The book's closing operational guidance collapses the whole framework into a decision rule for where to focus at any given moment: work whichever Core Four channel is already showing the best signal, in the sequence Warm Outreach → Content → Cold Outreach → Paid Ads, apply More/Better/New within that channel before switching channels, and only add complexity (a new channel, a new lead-getter type) once the current one is genuinely maximized rather than merely tried.
