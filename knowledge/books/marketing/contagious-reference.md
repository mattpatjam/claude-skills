# Contagious — Framework Reference

*Pure framework reference distilled from the book. No commentary, no application notes — just the frameworks, models, and mental tools, explained in original language.*

*Source: Contagious: Why Things Catch On by Jonah Berger*

---

## 1. The Core Premise — Contagiousness Is Engineered, Not Born

Berger's starting claim: word of mouth, not advertising, drives most consumer decisions, and the vast majority of that word of mouth happens offline, face to face, not on social platforms (research he cites puts online word of mouth at roughly 7% of the total, far below the 50%+ most people assume). Two things make word of mouth more persuasive than advertising: it is inherently more credible (a friend has no incentive to oversell), and it is self-targeting (people naturally share things with the specific person they think will care, rather than broadcasting to everyone).

The book's central argument is that virality is not a fixed property of a product or idea. It's not about the product being inherently exciting (boring products like a blender or Cheerios can go viral) and it's not about finding a handful of unusually influential or persuasive "connector" people to seed the message (the influential-people theory associated with word-of-mouth folklore). Instead, contagiousness is a property of the *message itself* — engineered in the same way a recipe is engineered, using a repeatable set of ingredients. A sufficiently well-built message will spread regardless of who happens to be telling it, the same way a truly great joke lands even from a mediocre joke-teller.

This produces the book's operating thesis: any product, idea, or behavior — no matter how mundane — can be made more contagious by deliberately building in the right ingredients. Virality isn't born, it's made.

## 2. The STEPPS Framework — Six Drivers of Contagiousness

Across a large body of research (analysis of thousands of New York Times articles, hundreds of word-of-mouth ad campaigns, YouTube virality, baby-naming trends, car purchases, and more), Berger and collaborators identified six recurring ingredients present in contagious content. These form the acronym STEPPS:

- **Social Currency** — sharing things that make the sharer look good.
- **Triggers** — cues in the environment that keep a product or idea top of mind.
- **Emotion** — when we care, we share; certain emotions drive sharing far more than others.
- **Public** — the more observable a behavior or product is, the more it can be imitated.
- **Practical Value** — useful, valuable information that people feel compelled to pass along.
- **Stories** — information travels wrapped inside narratives people already want to tell.

Two framing points Berger is explicit about. First, these six drivers are not sequential steps or a strict checklist — they behave more like independent salad toppings than a recipe's fixed ingredient list. A message doesn't need all six to spread; even one or two, deployed well, can be enough. Second, some drivers are easier to apply to certain categories of product than others (nonprofits often have built-in Emotion; physical products more easily achieve Public visibility), but the book's repeated point, illustrated through unglamorous products like blenders, cheesesteaks, toilet paper, and candy bars, is that any of the six can be engineered into almost anything if you dig for the right angle.

---

## 3. Social Currency — Finding Inner Remarkability

The foundational insight behind Social Currency: what people choose to talk about functions the same way as the clothes they wear or the car they drive — it signals identity and shapes how others perceive them. People consistently prefer to share things that make them look smart, in-the-know, or interesting rather than dull, so the practical question becomes how to give people something that makes them look good by association when they talk about it.

**Inner remarkability** is the first lever. Remarkable things are unusual, novel, surprising, or extreme enough to be genuinely "worthy of remark." Berger stresses that remarkability is not an inherent property some products have and others lack — it can be manufactured in almost anything by finding what breaks a pattern people expect (a low-cost airline that unexpectedly offers generous legroom and free live TV), introducing mystery or controversy (a film whose promotional premise deliberately left audiences unsure whether the footage was real), or simply surfacing a surprising fact about an ordinary product (trivia printed under a beverage bottle cap; an unexpectedly dyed household product). The core diagnostic question: what about this product or idea, if surfaced correctly, would make someone telling a friend about it seem sharper or more interesting for having known it?

A related mechanism is that stories drift toward remarkability over time and across retellings, because people unconsciously exaggerate size, quantity, and drama when recounting an experience to an audience they want to impress — the same distortion at work in a "fish that got away" story growing with each telling.

---

## 4. Social Currency — Game Mechanics

The second lever for minting Social Currency is borrowing the structural elements of games — points, levels, badges, leaderboards — that make progress and status visible and comparative. Game mechanics work on two separate levels: they give people an internal sense of tangible progress (a coffee-shop loyalty punch card motivates because each purchase visibly closes the gap to a reward), and they trigger social comparison, because status is inherently relative — people would rather earn less money overall while outperforming their peers than earn more while falling behind them.

For game mechanics to generate word of mouth, achievement has to be both measurable and, ideally, displayable to others (frequent-flier status tiers, location-based "mayor" badges, publicly displayed loyalty tiers). Berger notes that effective status systems need to be instantly interpretable even by outsiders — a silver medal communicates rank immediately to anyone, whereas a confusing hierarchy of colored credit-card tiers (platinum vs. sapphire vs. diamond) fails because the relative status isn't obvious. When people brag about an achievement earned through a brand or platform, they are simultaneously advertising that brand — this is how "gamifying" an otherwise unremarkable activity (checking in at a venue, accumulating miles nobody redeems) manufactures Social Currency and, as a byproduct, free marketing.

---

## 5. Social Currency — Scarcity, Exclusivity, and Making People Feel Like Insiders

The third lever is deliberately restricting access. Berger distinguishes two related but separate mechanisms:

- **Scarcity** — limiting *how much* of something is available (a bar with a fixed, tiny number of seats; a flash-sale site where inventory disappears within minutes).
- **Exclusivity** — limiting *who* can access something, based on criteria other than price (invitation-only access; hidden or unadvertised locations; membership requirements).

Both work through the same underlying psychology: when something is hard to get, people infer it must be valuable, and having access to it (or knowledge of it) becomes something worth telling others about, because it signals insider status. This is why an unmarked, phone-booth-entrance speakeasy and an invitation-only flash-sale shopping site can generate outsized word of mouth from the exclusivity alone, even when the underlying product is unremarkable or identical to a competitor's.

A key caveat Berger raises: scarcity and exclusivity work best when they are difficult but not impossible to overcome — a determined customer should eventually be able to get in — and businesses using this tactic should train staff to soften refusals ("no, but...") so that heightened desirability doesn't curdle into a reputation for being unhelpful or unwelcoming. He also notes the deliberate design move of using restriction early to build demand and then loosening access once momentum is established.

Separately, Berger observes that paying people to talk about a product (rather than letting Social Currency motivate them intrinsically) tends to backfire: once money enters the exchange, people's motivation to share shifts from genuine enthusiasm to a calculation of whether the payment is worth the effort, and the appearance of authentic recommendation is lost. Social currency-driven sharing is a more durable, self-sustaining engine than paid referral because it taps into a desire for image, not a price people are willing to sell their opinion for.

## 6. Triggers — Top of Mind, Tip of Tongue

Where Social Currency explains what makes people want to share once they're thinking about something, Triggers explain why they're thinking about it at all. A trigger is any stimulus in the environment — a smell, a color, a season, a routine, a nearby object — that brings a related idea to mind. The central finding: how frequently a product or idea is triggered in someone's everyday environment predicts how often it gets talked about far better than how inherently interesting it is.

Berger draws a sharp distinction between **immediate word of mouth** (talk generated right after exposure to something novel or interesting) and **ongoing word of mouth** (talk sustained over weeks or months). Novelty and surprise reliably drive immediate word of mouth but fade quickly — a one-off remarkable event gets discussed today and forgotten by next month. Frequent, well-designed triggers are what sustain ongoing word of mouth over time, which is why an unglamorous, frequently-encountered product (a breakfast cereal eaten daily) can out-talk a spectacular but rarely-experienced one (a theme park visited once a year): the cereal has far more environmental cues reminding people of it.

This is the mechanism behind the book's often-cited finding that mundane, high-frequency products generate more sustained word of mouth than "exciting" but rarely-encountered ones — accessibility (how easily a thought comes to mind) drives conversation more reliably than the raw interest value of the underlying content.

---

## 7. Triggers — Growing the Habitat

Berger borrows the biological concept of a **habitat** — the set of environmental cues that keep an idea or product naturally top of mind — and argues that products and ideas each have their own habitat of associated triggers, some already existing (a candy bar coincidentally sharing a name with a planet) and some that can be deliberately manufactured. "Growing the habitat" means intentionally forging a new, repeated association between a product and something people already encounter often, so that encountering the everyday cue reliably brings the product to mind (an underperforming confection reviving sales by repeatedly pairing itself with a much more frequently consumed daily beverage, rather than choosing a more "clever" but rarer pairing).

A frequency/strength trade-off governs how well this works: the cue chosen needs to occur often (linking a brand to something people encounter daily beats linking it to something seasonal or rare), but it also needs to not already be overloaded with other associations — an already crowded, generic cue (a common color, a common word) creates only a weak link to any one specific thing, the same way water pressure drops the more holes are poked in a container.

A separate consideration is **proximity between trigger and desired action** — a message is more effective when the trigger occurs physically close to where the behavior it's trying to prompt would actually happen, rather than being memorable but disconnected from the moment of decision (a vivid warning encountered somewhere far from where the relevant purchase or behavior occurs is far less effective than one delivered at the point of decision itself).

---

## 8. Triggers — The Poison Parasite Strategy

A specific competitive tactic under the Triggers driver: deliberately linking your own message to a rival's much more visible or better-funded messaging, so that every time the audience encounters the competitor's message, it also triggers yours. Researchers call this the "poison parasite" strategy — injecting your own "poison" (message) into a stronger opponent's "carrier" (their existing, high-frequency messaging), turning their marketing reach into an unwitting distribution channel for your competing idea. This is most useful for outmatched or underfunded challengers (public health messaging up against a much larger commercial advertiser, for instance) who can't out-advertise a rival but can piggyback on the rival's existing frequency.

## 9. Emotion — Arousal as the Real Driver of Sharing

Berger's research initially expected that positive emotion would drive sharing and negative emotion would suppress it. The actual pattern found in large-scale analysis of shared news content was more specific: **sadness reduced sharing**, while **anger and anxiety increased it** — despite both being negative. The resolution to this puzzle is a second, independent dimension of emotion beyond positive/negative: **physiological arousal** (activation).

Emotions split along two axes:

- High-arousal, positive: awe, excitement, amusement/humor.
- Low-arousal, positive: contentment.
- High-arousal, negative: anger, anxiety.
- Low-arousal, negative: sadness.

High-arousal emotions — regardless of whether they feel good or bad — put the body into an activated, ready-for-action state that spills over into behavior, including the impulse to talk, share, and forward. Low-arousal emotions, even pleasant ones like contentment, tend to produce a settled, passive state that suppresses the impulse to act. This is why sadness (low arousal) reliably kills sharing while anger and anxiety (high arousal) reliably boost it, and why simple physical arousal — even something as unrelated as having just exercised — measurably increases a person's likelihood of sharing unrelated content immediately afterward (demonstrated experimentally: people who had just jogged in place were roughly twice as likely to forward an unrelated article as people who had been sitting still).

The named emotion that anchors the book's discussion of science and awe-driven virality is **awe** — the sense of being confronted with something vastly larger than oneself, whether through scale, beauty, ingenuity, or achievement. Content that evokes awe (a striking scientific visualization, an unexpectedly transcendent performance) can go viral even when it scores low on Social Currency or Practical Value, because awe alone generates enough arousal to drive sharing.

---

## 10. Emotion — Focus on Feelings, Not Features

A practical implication Berger draws from the arousal research: marketing and messaging built purely around information, features, and rational argument routinely underperforms messaging built around feeling. Even categories that seem inherently unemotional (search engines, insurance, financial services, B2B software) can be engineered toward high-arousal emotional appeal by asking, repeatedly, "why does this actually matter to the person," peeling back through layers of stated purpose until the emotional core underneath the function is exposed (a technique the book borrows and cites: asking "why is this important" three times in succession to drill from feature down to feeling).

The design implication is to deliberately select for high-arousal emotions on both the positive side (excitement, inspiration, awe, humor) and, when appropriate, the negative side (anger, anxiety, disgust) — while actively avoiding low-arousal registers (sadness, mere contentment) if the goal is sharing. Negative-arousal emotions used deliberately (an ad deliberately built around tension, fear, or disgust rather than warmth) can outperform conventional positive advertising specifically because of the arousal they generate, provided the brand can tolerate the tonal risk.

A related caution: because anger and anxiety are high-arousal, negative buzz and complaint cycles about a brand can spread just as fast, or faster, than positive buzz, for the identical underlying reason. Sadness-driven complaints tend to stay contained; anger-driven and anxiety-driven ones tend to escalate and spread. Monitoring for early signs of these specific high-arousal negative emotions (rather than negativity broadly) is the practical early-warning signal for a brewing backlash.

## 11. Public — Observability Drives Imitation

The Public driver rests on a simple mechanical claim: people can only imitate behavior they can actually see. "Monkey see, monkey do" requires the seeing part first. This makes a product or idea's raw visibility, independent of its quality, a major lever on whether it spreads, because visible adoption creates **social proof** — the inference that if many other people are visibly doing or choosing something, it must be a reasonable or good choice.

Social proof is a genuinely powerful decision shortcut precisely because most day-to-day choices (which restaurant, which product, which career path) are made under real uncertainty, and observing others' choices is a cheap, fast way to reduce that uncertainty. Berger illustrates its downside as well as its upside: patients on transplant waiting lists disproportionately decline viable kidney offers once they learn many people ahead of them already declined the same organ, inferring (often wrongly) that the refusals signal a quality problem rather than simple list-order coincidence — an error rate the book quantifies as costing a meaningful share of patients a transplant they should have accepted.

The strength of social-proof effects tracks directly with how observable the underlying behavior is. Comparing product categories, Berger notes that highly visible choices (cars, clothing) are far more susceptible to neighbor-to-neighbor influence than private, hidden ones (toothpaste, socks) — people simply have no way to imitate a purchase they never see. Geographic and situational differences in visibility (a city where most people drive and can see others' cars versus one where most commute underground) measurably change how much social influence operates on that category.

## 12. Public — Making the Private Public

Because so many valuable behaviors and preferences are naturally private (charitable giving, political opinion, health choices, even simple wine or software preference), a major design task under the Public driver is converting an invisible behavior into a visible signal. Berger's central example is a men's health fundraising effort built entirely around growing a moustache for a set period each year — an inherently visible, walking advertisement for a cause that would otherwise be invisible (there's no way to see who has or hasn't donated to a health charity, but there's no missing a sudden moustache). Every wearer becomes a conversation-starter, prompting exactly the "why do you have that?" question that surfaces the cause and passes along awareness organically.

A second, more subtle technique described is correcting *false* social proof by making the true (and often reassuring) private norm visible. A campaign to reduce college binge drinking succeeded not by publicizing the dangers of alcohol, but by simply publishing the actual, more moderate average number of drinks students consumed — correcting a shared false belief (each student assuming everyone else was drinking more heavily than they personally were comfortable with) that had been sustaining the very behavior it was trying to fix. This works because behavior is public but the thoughts and discomfort behind it are private — everyone was making the same overestimation of their peers' enthusiasm, with no way to see that thought mirrored in others.

---

## 13. Public — Products That Advertise Themselves and Behavioral Residue

Two design patterns let a product generate its own observability without a paid ad budget:

**Self-advertising products** embed a visible brand signal directly into the act of using them — a signature line automatically appended to every outgoing email announcing the service that sent it, a distinctively colored component (headphone cords, shoe soles) that stands out and identifies the brand at a glance even to someone who knows nothing about it, a distinctive sound or shape. Every use of the product becomes a passive advertisement and a small piece of social proof to everyone who witnesses it, at zero incremental marketing cost — a technique especially valuable for small organizations without an advertising budget, since existing customers substitute for paid media.

**Behavioral residue** is the physical trace a behavior or purchase leaves behind even after the moment of use has passed — a wristband, a bumper sticker, a distinctive shopping bag reused for other errands, an "I Voted" sticker worn the rest of the day. Because the underlying behavior itself (donating, voting) is often private and momentary, residue extends its visibility across time, letting it keep generating social proof long after the original act. Berger notes that residue is far more effective when the item itself is naturally reused in public (a durable, reusable shopping bag) rather than something typically discarded or used in private (a cosmetics sample used behind a bathroom door) — the choice of *what* residue to hand out should be evaluated specifically on how publicly and how often it will be re-encountered.

## 14. Public — When Making Something Public Backfires

Berger devotes explicit attention to the failure mode of this driver: making a behavior more visible can *increase* an undesirable behavior if the visible signal being sent is "lots of people are already doing this," even when the surrounding message is a warning against it. His central case is anti-drug public service messaging aimed at teens, found in controlled analysis to have modestly *increased* drug experimentation rather than reduced it — because the ads' implicit subtext ("someone will offer you drugs, and you'll need to know how to refuse") communicated that drug use among peers was common and normalized, and that signal proved more persuasive than the explicit warning layered on top of it.

The corrective principle: if the goal is to *discourage* a behavior, don't make the frequency of that behavior more visible or salient, even in a cautionary frame — because doing so still functions as social proof for the behavior. A cleaner alternative demonstrated in a park-conservation study: a sign warning that "many past visitors have removed [protected materials]" nearly doubled the targeted theft, by advertising that the behavior was common, whereas a sign that simply asked people not to remove the material, without referencing how many others already had, reduced it. The rule of thumb: highlight the desired behavior directly, rather than surfacing the very statistic you're trying to suppress.

## 15. Practical Value — News You Can Use

Practical Value is the most straightforwardly intuitive of the six drivers: people share things that are genuinely useful to the receiver, because helping others through information is a low-cost, high-reward social behavior — it strengthens relationships, signals care, and reflects modestly well on the sharer, even though the primary motive is altruistic rather than self-promotional (in contrast to Social Currency, which is centered on the sharer's own image). Berger frames the psychology of sharing useful information as a modern equivalent of a barn-raising: a low-friction way for people who are otherwise disconnected from close-knit community structures to still materially help one another.

Because almost any product or idea has *some* genuine practical angle (time saved, money saved, health improved, a problem solved), Berger treats this driver as one of the easiest to locate but one of the hardest to make stand out, given how saturated the average person's information environment already is with tips, deals, and advice. The design task under Practical Value is less about inventing usefulness and more about packaging and framing it so it cuts through.

---

## 16. Practical Value — The Psychology of a Good Deal (Prospect Theory Applied to Sharing)

Whether a promotional offer feels worth sharing is governed less by the objective size of the discount than by how the offer is *framed* relative to a **reference point** — the price or quantity a person expected to pay or receive. Two identical final prices can produce very different perceived deal quality depending on the starting price used as the anchor (a mid-range discount off a much higher "was" price reads as a better deal than a small discount off an already-low price, even when the final price paid is higher in the first case). This reference-point effect explains several concrete deal-framing tactics: setting a high anticipated price before revealing a lower actual one (the infomercial technique of naming an inflated expected price before the "real" price), labeling something "on sale" even without changing the underlying price, and "throwing in" extra quantity at the same price rather than lowering the price itself.

**Diminishing sensitivity** is the related principle explaining why an identical absolute discount (say, $10 off) feels significant on a cheap item and negligible on an expensive one — the further a number sits from the reference point of zero, the smaller the felt impact of any fixed change to it. This is why people will drive across town to save $10 on a $25 item but won't bother to save the same $10 on a $650 item.

**The Rule of 100** is Berger's practical shortcut for deciding whether to frame a discount as a percentage or as an absolute dollar amount: below a $100 price point, percentage framing makes the discount look larger (a $3 discount on a $30 item looks bigger stated as "10% off" than as "$3 off"); above $100, absolute dollar framing looks larger (a 10% discount on a $2,000 item looks smaller than stating it as "$200 off"). The crossover point is exactly where the percentage-off number and the dollar-off number become numerically equal in magnitude.

Beyond price framing, Berger notes that restricting an offer — by time window, by quantity per customer, or by eligibility — reliably increases its perceived value and its shareability, for the same scarcity/exclusivity reasons discussed under Social Currency: restriction signals that the deal must be unusually good, prompting people to both act on it and tell others quickly before it disappears.

## 17. Practical Value — Packaging Knowledge and the Narrow-Audience Effect

Two additional, more tactical observations round out this driver. First, usefully packaged information (a short, focused list of tips rather than a sprawling, unstructured document) is shared more, because it's easier for the receiver to grasp the value quickly and easier for the sharer to summarize when passing it on — this is the underlying logic behind list-formatted advice content ("Five Ways to X," "Ten Tips for Y") outperforming long-form advice with the same underlying value.

Second, and counterintuitively, content aimed at a *narrower*, more specific audience can spread more than content aimed at a broad one, because broad relevance paradoxically makes no single recipient feel personally, urgently "made for" — whereas content clearly tailored to a specific, narrow interest instantly calls to mind the one particular friend who cares about exactly that thing, creating a much stronger compulsion to send it directly to that person.

A final caveat under this driver: the drive to share useful information is strong enough that it can propagate false information just as efficiently as true information, because sharers are motivated by the desire to help, not by any process of independently verifying accuracy first — a caution against assuming shared "practical" content is automatically vetted.

## 18. Stories — Information Travels Inside Narratives

The final STEPPS driver rests on the observation that people are fundamentally narrative-oriented: they retain, are persuaded by, and voluntarily retell stories far more readily than they do isolated facts or claims. A story engineered around a product or idea functions the way the Trojan Horse functioned in the myth that gives this chapter its name — the outer narrative shell is what captures attention and gets voluntarily carried past normal defenses, while the payload (the lesson, the brand information, the persuasive point) rides along hidden inside it, absorbed by the listener as a side effect of following the plot rather than as a direct sales pitch.

Stories carry practical, transmissible information more effectively than direct claims for a few concrete reasons: a specific narrative about what happened to a specific person is much harder to argue with than an abstract or advertised claim (an ad's "we have great service" invites skepticism; a friend's specific story about being generously helped doesn't); and the sheer engagement of following a plot leaves less cognitive room to critically evaluate or resist the information embedded within it, so persuasion happens somewhat below conscious scrutiny.

---

## 19. Stories — Building a Trojan Horse and Valuable Virality

The practical design task under this driver is to construct a **Trojan Horse**: a narrative genuinely worth telling on its own merits (surprising, funny, emotionally engaging, or socially useful to repeat) that happens to have the desired product or brand information woven inescapably into its core plot, rather than merely mentioned or logo-stamped on top of an otherwise-unrelated story.

Berger draws a sharp distinction between mere virality and what he calls **valuable virality**. A stunt or story can spread widely and still deliver essentially zero benefit to the sponsor if the sponsoring brand is incidental to the narrative rather than load-bearing within it — illustrated by a widely covered publicity stunt where audiences discussed the spectacle itself at length but almost never mentioned, let alone patronized, the sponsoring company whose name happened to be printed on the stunt's costume, precisely because the brand had nothing structurally to do with the story being told. The same failure mode is shown in a heavily viewed viral ad campaign whose imagery had no logical connection to the product it was ostensibly promoting, and which coincided with a market-share decline rather than a gain.

The corrective principle is to make the brand or desired message **integral** to the story, not decorative — embedded so deeply into the plot that the story literally cannot be told, or cannot make sense, without mentioning it. Berger's clearest positive illustration is a recurring ad scenario built around a single absurd, consistent device that is inseparable from the joke itself: retelling the story requires naming the device, which requires naming the brand, so the retelling mechanically carries the brand forward every time. The same principle explains why a demonstration built around a product literally destroying test objects works as valuable virality: the story cannot be told without describing the product's actual, defining capability.

---

## 20. Stories — Which Details Survive Retelling

Drawing on classic research into how rumors and stories change as they pass from person to person (a sequential-retelling exercise akin to a game of "telephone"), Berger notes that a large share of the original detail in any story is lost within the first several retellings, but the loss is not random. Details that are *structurally essential* to the plot's internal logic tend to survive intact across many retellings, while details that are incidental to the core action are reliably dropped, regardless of how memorable or interesting they might have seemed to the original teller.

The design implication ties directly back to valuable virality: whatever information a communicator most wants preserved through many rounds of retelling needs to be built into the structurally essential core of the story, not attached as an incidental decoration — because incidental details are exactly what gets filtered out first as a story propagates through a population, while plot-critical details are what survives.

## 21. Synthesis — The STEPPS Checklist and Its Underlying Claims

Berger's closing synthesis makes three claims that tie the whole framework together. First, virality is domain-agnostic: the same six-driver structure explains the spread of blenders, candy bars, bars, cheese commercials, immigrant occupational clustering, health campaigns, and political voting behavior alike, because the underlying mechanism in every case is human psychology, not the specific category of the product or idea.

Second, contagious spread is driven by the cumulative, largely anonymous action of many ordinary people passing something along, not by a small set of unusually influential or persuasive individuals — a forest fire's ultimate size is a function of how many individual trees catch and carry the flame, not the size of the original spark. A charismatic originator may supply the initial idea, but the content or behavior itself has to be independently capable of spreading through ordinary people for the effect to scale.

Third, and most practically, the six STEPPS drivers can be applied as a diagnostic checklist against any existing product, message, or campaign: does talking about this make the sharer look good (Social Currency)? What in the surrounding environment would remind someone to think or talk about this (Triggers)? Does it evoke a genuine, ideally high-arousal emotion (Emotion)? Can other people actually observe someone using or doing it (Public)? Does it offer a clear, useful benefit worth passing on (Practical Value)? And is it wrapped in, or capable of being wrapped in, a narrative people want to tell for its own sake, with the message inseparable from that narrative (Stories)? A message doesn't need to score well on all six to spread — but each one it satisfies independently increases the odds.
