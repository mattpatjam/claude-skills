# Positioning — Framework Reference

*Pure framework reference distilled from the book. No commentary, no application notes — just the frameworks, models, and mental tools as the author presents them.*

*Source: Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind by Al Ries & Jack Trout*

---

## 1. Positioning Defined — The Core Concept

Positioning starts with a product: a piece of merchandise, a service, a company, an institution, or even a person. But positioning is not what you do to a product. **Positioning is what you do to the mind of the prospect.** You position the product in the mind of the prospect. It is therefore incorrect to call the concept "product positioning" — you are not doing something to the product itself.

Positioning does often involve change, but changes made to the name, the price, and the package are not changes in the product at all. They are cosmetic changes made for the purpose of securing a worthwhile position in the prospect's mind.

Positioning is described as the first body of thought that comes to grips with the difficulty of getting heard in an overcommunicated society. The basic approach of positioning is not to create something new and different, but to manipulate what's already up there in the mind, to retie the connections that already exist.

Key premises that follow from this definition:

- You look for the solution to your communication problem not inside the product, not even inside your own mind. You look for the solution inside the prospect's mind.
- Since so little of any message gets through, you ignore the sending side and concentrate on the receiving end — the perceptions of the prospect, not the reality of the product. "In politics, the perception is the reality" (John Lindsay), and the same holds in advertising, business, and life.
- The premise that the sender is "wrong" and the receiver is "right" (an inversion of "the customer is always right") is not a claim about objective truth. It is a practical necessity: you have no other choice if you want your message accepted by another human mind.
- By turning the process around and focusing on the prospect rather than the product, you simplify the selection process and gain principles that increase communication effectiveness.

Historical framing: advertising moved through three eras. The **product era** (1950s) centered on product features and the "Unique Selling Proposition" (Rosser Reeves), which collapsed under an avalanche of me-too products. The **image era** (associated with David Ogilvy — "every advertisement is a long-term investment in the image of a brand") centered on reputation, which collapsed as me-too companies drove up the noise level. The **positioning era** requires that a company create a position in the prospect's mind that accounts for both its own strengths and weaknesses and those of its competitors. In this era it is not enough to invent or discover something — it may not even be necessary — but you must be first to get into the prospect's mind (IBM did not invent the computer; Sperry-Rand did, but IBM built the computer position first).

## 2. The Overcommunicated Society

The foundational condition positioning responds to: America has become the world's first overcommunicated society. Per-capita advertising consumption is $376.62/year in the U.S. versus $16.87 in the rest of the world. Spending $1 million/year on advertising bombards the average consumer with less than half a cent of message spread across 365 days, against a backdrop of $376.61½ of other advertising already received.

**Core mechanism — the mind as filter, not sponge:**

- The mind screens and rejects most information offered to it; it accepts only what matches prior knowledge or experience.
- Once a mind is made up, it is almost impossible to change with a weak force like advertising ("don't confuse me with the facts, my mind's made up").
- People will sit still for something they know nothing about (why "news" works as an advertising approach) but cannot tolerate being told they are wrong — mind-changing is the road to advertising disaster.
- The average mind is "a dripping sponge that can only soak up more information at the expense of what's already there."
- Per Harvard psychologist Dr. George A. Miller, the average human mind cannot deal with more than seven units at a time — which is why seven is a popular number for lists meant to be remembered, and why asking someone to name brands in a category rarely yields more than seven (often only one or two for low-interest categories).

**The oversimplified message** is the only viable response: in communication, as in architecture, less is more. You must sharpen the message, jettison ambiguities, simplify, then simplify again. Because so little material about a candidate, product, or company will ever get into the prospect's mind, the job is not a "communication" project in the ordinary sense — it's a **selection project**: choosing the material with the best chance of getting through.

**Supporting phenomena named in the text:**

- **The product explosion** — a typical U.S. supermarket carries roughly 12,000 products; hypermarkets carry far more (Biggs in Cincinnati: 60,000). Half a million active U.S. trademarks are registered, with 25,000 more added yearly.
- **The media explosion** — television, radio, outdoor, print, and moving vehicles have multiplied the channels competing for the same fixed mental capacity.
- **The advertising explosion** — as effectiveness declines, usage rises, and even professions once considered above advertising (law, medicine, dentistry) have entered the pool.
- **Sensory overload** — beyond a certain point of stimulation, the brain "goes blank and refuses to function normally."

You see what you expect to see, and taste what you expect to taste (demonstrated by blind taste tests, labeled abstract art, and beer/wine relabeling experiments). One prime objective of advertising is to heighten expectation and let the mind supply the confirming experience — which cuts both ways: negative expectations (e.g., Gablinger's diet-beer advertising implying bad taste) get confirmed just as reliably.

## 3. Getting Into the Mind: The Law of Firstness

**The easy way into the mind is to be first.** The first name, first mountain, first person to occupy a position in the mind is extremely hard to dislodge: Kodak in photography, Kleenex in tissue, Xerox in plain-paper copiers, Hertz in rent-a-cars, Coca in cola, General in electric. People readily recall the first person to fly solo across the Atlantic (Lindbergh) or walk on the moon (Armstrong) but not the second.

The biological analogy offered is **imprinting**: a newborn duckling fixes indelibly on the first moving creature it encounters as its mother, even if that creature is a dog, cat, or human, and even if the flock is later mixed up. Falling in love works similarly — what counts most is receptivity (both parties having an "open window"), not exhaustive comparison. Marriage, like brand loyalty, depends on the concept of **first being better than best**.

**The hard way into the mind is second — and second is nowhere.** The corollary examples: the second-largest-selling book ever published, the second cargo port in the U.S., the second person to fly the Atlantic solo — nobody knows or cares, even if that person was objectively better. If you didn't get into the prospect's mind first, you have a positioning problem, and in a mental contest (unlike a physical one), the odds favor the first, not necessarily the best.

**Corollary principle:** it's better to be a big fish in a small pond (and then grow the pond) than a small fish in a big pond. First make sure you can't find something to be first in before resorting to a No. 2/No. 3 strategy (see Section 8, "Repositioning of a Follower").

**Illustrative historical case — Columbus vs. Vespucci:** Columbus discovered America but kept quiet and looked only for gold; Amerigo Vespucci arrived five years later but (1) positioned the New World as a distinct continent from Asia and (2) wrote and published extensively about it (one letter translated into 40 languages over 25 years). Europe credited Vespucci and named the continent after him. Columbus died in jail — illustrating that being first to communicate a discovery can outweigh being first to make it.

**Product-era proof cases:** Michelob was not the first premium domestic beer, but was first to build the premium position in the beer drinker's mind. Miller "Lite" succeeded (spawning me-too brands including, ironically, Schlitz Light) by literally positioning as a light beer rather than hiding the claim in poetic copy the way Schlitz did ("real gusto in a great, light beer"). Beck's beer positioned itself directly against Löwenbräu by naming it: "You've tasted the German beer that's the most popular in America. Now taste the German beer that's the most popular in Germany."

## 4. The Product Ladder

To cope with the product explosion, people rank products and brands on mental ladders — imagine a series of ladders in the mind, one rung per brand name, one ladder per product category. Some ladders have many rungs (seven is "many"); others have few.

**Operating rules of the ladder:**

- A competitor seeking more share must either dislodge the brand above it (usually impossible) or relate its own brand to the position already held by the brand above.
- Companies that run marketing programs as if the competitor's position doesn't exist advertise into a vacuum and are disappointed when messages fail to land.
- Introducing an entirely new product category means carrying in a new ladder — the mind has no room for what's new and different unless it's related to the old. This is why a genuinely new product is often better introduced by stating what it is *not* rather than what it is (the first automobile was called a "horseless carriage"; "off-track" betting, "lead-free" gasoline, "sugar-free" soda follow the same pattern).

**The "against" position** — establishing your rung by explicit reference to the rung above — is illustrated by the Avis campaign: "Avis is only No. 2 in rent-a-cars, so why go with us? We try harder." After 13 straight years of losses, admitting to being No. 2 turned Avis profitable within a year ($1.2 million, then $2.6 million, then $5 million in successive years). The lesson: Avis succeeded not because it "tried harder" but because it explicitly related itself to Hertz's position — capitalizing simultaneously on the product ladder and on natural sympathy for the underdog.

**The "uncola" position** — worming onto a ladder owned by someone else by defining yourself in relation to it. 7-Up's "uncola" position established it as the alternative to a category (cola) with roughly two-thirds of all U.S. soft-drink consumption, without inventing anything new. The three rungs on the cola ladder: Coke, Pepsi, 7-Up. A parallel case: beautiful-music radio station WLKW (an also-ran in Providence, RI) became No. 1 with the theme "the unrock station."

**The F.W.M.T.S. trap ("Forgot What Made Them Successful"):** having executed a brilliant positioning coup, companies often abandon it. After being sold to ITT, Avis ran ads declaring "Avis is going to be No. 1" — wrong psychologically (advertising your own aspiration) and wrong strategically (Avis had no way to actually dislodge Hertz, and the new campaign discarded the underdog sympathy the old one traded on). The lesson: successful positioning requires consistency, year after year — you cannot ignore the competitor's position, nor can you walk away from your own ("play it as it lays" — Joan Didion).

## 5. You Can't Get There From Here

Some positions cannot be reached from where a company currently stands, no matter how much effort or money is applied — analogous to the farmer's joke ending in "you can't get there from here." A great product, sales force, and ad campaign can still fail if the company occupies a position that is fundamentally out of phase (just as a 55-year-old executive VP is too old to be tapped as successor when the retiring CEO is followed by someone a decade younger).

**Case study — RCA vs. IBM in computers.** In a 1969 article, the authors predicted that "a company has no hope to make progress head-on against the position that IBM has established" in computers. RCA proceeded anyway ("RCA fires a broadside at No. 1" — Business Week; "RCA goes head-to-head with IBM" — Fortune), with the chairman publicly targeting a "firm No. 2 position." Less than a year later: "The $250 million disaster that hit RCA" (Business Week). General Electric's computer division folded into Honeywell around the same time.

**Prescribed alternative strategy for challenging an entrenched leader:** don't act like the leader ("fight fire with water," not fire with fire — Howard Gossage). Instead, take advantage of a position you already own and relate it to the new category:

- RCA, strong in communications, should have positioned a computer line that related to communications rather than attacking IBM head-on.
- GE, a heavy computer user, might have succeeded by concentrating specifically on time-sharing computers (notably, GE's only computer unit not sold to Honeywell was its profitable time-sharing network).
- NCR, strong in cash registers, made real progress by concentrating on retail data-entry systems (computerized cash registers).

Where the situation is genuinely hopeless, the correct move is to abandon the effort rather than pour in more resources ("no problem is too big to run away from" — Charlie Brown). Outright failure is often preferable to mediocre success, because effort applied late to a lost position produces little; effort applied early, before positions solidify, is what builds product leadership. **The extra-effort principle applies to timing, not raw hustle** — it is "not what, but when."

The takeaway generalizes: whatever governs positioning dynamics in computers (a dominant leader plus a host of also-rans) recurs across virtually every industry (Xerox in copiers, GM in automobiles).

## 6. Positioning of a Leader

**Establishing leadership** follows directly from firstness: history shows the first brand into the mind gets, on average, roughly twice the long-term market share of the No. 2 brand, and twice again the share of No. 3. These relationships are not easily changed (Hertz over Avis, GM over Ford, Goodyear over Firestone, McDonald's over Burger King, GE over Westinghouse).

**The instability of equality:** categories where two brands run neck-and-neck are inherently unstable. One eventually gains the upper hand and opens a lead that stabilizes around 5:3 or 2:1. Consumers are "like chickens" — more comfortable with an accepted pecking order. The moment to apply extra effort is precisely when the situation is in doubt, because winning a single year's leadership battle can lock in decades of advantage (Chevrolet overtook Ford in 1931 and has lost the lead only four times since, through depression and wars).

**Strategies for maintaining leadership — what leaders should NOT do:**

- Don't run "We're No. 1" advertising. Psychologically it backfires either way: if the prospect already knows you're No. 1, they wonder why you're insecure enough to say so; if they don't know, the claim raises the question of why not.
- Don't try to build leadership "on your own terms" (e.g., "the best-selling under-$1000 high-fidelity system east of the Mississippi") — leadership must be built in the prospect's terms.

**What leaders SHOULD do:**

- Reinforce the original concept rather than announce market share. Coca-Cola's "The real thing" works because it plants the brand as the standard by which everything else is judged — different from claiming to be the biggest seller (which could just mean lower price or wider distribution).
- Claim invention where true ("We invented the product" — a powerful force behind Xerox, Polaroid, Zippo).
- **Cover all bets.** Leaders should swallow pride and adopt every promising new development early, treating the cost as cheap insurance against the unexpected. GM spent $50 million covering the Wankel engine (never dominant, but cheap insurance against a rival buying the rights first). By contrast, Kodak and 3M declined to buy rights to Carlson's xerography process ("nobody would pay 5 cents for a plain-paper copy..."), and the license went to Haloid, which became Xerox — a $9 billion company and, per Fortune, maker of "probably the single most profitable product ever manufactured in the United States" (the 914 copier).
- **Cover with multibrands.** This is really a **single-position strategy**, not a multibrand strategy: each brand occupies a distinct, fixed position, and when technology or taste changes, a new product is introduced rather than repositioning the old one. Procter & Gamble exemplifies this — Ivory stayed soap; when heavy-duty detergent was needed, P&G created Tide rather than "Ivory Detergent"; the dishwasher product became Cascade, not "Dishwasher Tide." Each P&G brand (Joy, Crest, Head & Shoulders, Sure, Bounty, Pampers, Comet, Charmin, Duncan Hines) has a wholly separate identity — "not a Plus, Ultra, or Super in the lot."
- **Cover with a broader corporate name** when change threatens to strand the old name (e.g., Haloid → Haloid Xerox → Xerox; Eastman → Eastman Kodak). Government agencies are especially adept at this (Housing and Home Finance Agency → Department of Housing and Urban Development), since broader names justify larger scope, staff, and budget.
- **Broaden the range of applications** for the existing product (Arm & Hammer promoting baking soda for the refrigerator; Florida Citrus promoting orange juice beyond breakfast; Business Week successfully repositioning itself for consumer advertisers).

**The core misconception leaders must avoid:** believing the power of the product is derived from the power of the organization. It is the reverse — organizational power is derived from the position the product owns in the prospect's mind. Coca-Cola has power; the Coca-Cola Company merely reflects it. Outside the cola field (e.g., Mr. Pibb vs. Dr. Pepper) the company's size confers no advantage, because the position has to be earned fresh in every new category. The same applies to Xerox: massive size did not translate into computer, word-processor, or office-duplicator success, because Xerox owned the copier rung, not those rungs.

Leadership is not the end of a positioning program — it's the start. Leaders should constantly exploit the power of leadership to extend their lead, since short-term they are nearly invulnerable ("you can't get pinned when you're on top") but their real risk horizon is 5–10 years out.

## 7. Positioning of a Follower: Cherchez le Creneau

What works for a leader does not work for a follower. When a follower copies a leader, it isn't "covering" — it's a **me-too response**. Most products fail because the accent is on "better" rather than "speed": the No. 2 company wastes time perfecting the product, launches with a smaller budget than the leader's, and slaps the house name on it for a "quick" share — all deadly in an overcommunicated market.

**The organizing principle: *cherchez le creneau* — "look for the hole."** This runs directly against the "bigger and better" and "power of positive thinking" instincts most people are trained into. Finding a creneau requires thinking in reverse: if everyone else is going east, look west.

**Named creneau strategies, each with a worked example:**

- **Size creneau.** Volkswagen Beetle against a Detroit industry racing toward longer/lower cars: "Think small" stated the position in two words and challenged the assumption that bigger is better. Preemption mattered — other small cars existed, but none had claimed the position first. (The opposite direction — largeness — is equally available where no one has preempted it, e.g., projection television.)
- **High-price creneau.** Michelob (first premium-priced domestic beer position), Joy ("the costliest perfume in the world"), Piaget ("the world's most expensive watch"), and even mundane categories (Whitney's Yogurt, Orville Redenbacher's popcorn, Mobil 1). The rule: high price only works if you are first to establish the position, with a valid product story, in a category where consumers are receptive to paying more — otherwise the price just drives people away. The high price should be established in the advertising, not discovered as a surprise in the store.
- **Low-price creneau.** Works especially well for new, risk-feeling categories (facsimile equipment, videotape players at the time) and for generic/no-name goods; less available in mature categories where retailer discounting has already claimed the ground.
- **Sex creneau.** Marlboro was the first national brand to claim a masculine cigarette position (rising from fifth to first in sales over a decade); timing mattered — Lorillard's "Luke" (1973) had the name, packaging, and advertising right but arrived roughly 20 years too late and was killed. Virginia Slims succeeded with femininity; the me-too "Eve" failed from being too late. Charlie perfume became the largest-selling perfume in the world by taking a *masculine* name and pantsuit advertising in a category assumed to reward delicacy — proof that the "obvious" approach in an established category is not always the open creneau.
- **Age creneau.** Geritol (older adults), Aim toothpaste (children — captured 10% of a market dominated by Crest and Colgate).
- **Time-of-day creneau.** Nyquil, the first nighttime cold remedy.
- **Distribution creneau.** L'eggs, the first hosiery brand distributed through supermarkets and mass merchandisers, became the category leader.
- **Heavy-user creneau.** Schaefer: "The one beer to have when you're having more than one."

**Traps to avoid when hunting for a creneau:**

- **The factory creneau trap** — filling a hole in the factory (internal product-line logic) rather than a hole in the mind. The Edsel is the classic case: good internal logic (filling the gap between Ford/Mercury and Lincoln) but no position in a market already saturated with heavily chromed, medium-priced cars. The National Observer (Dow Jones's weekly newspaper, built to use idle Wall Street Journal presses) suffered the same fate — there was no hole in the reader's mind between existing newsweeklies.
- **The technology trap** — even a genuine R&D breakthrough fails without a mental creneau. Frost 8/80, "the first dry, white whisky" (1971), collapsed within two years despite a real technical first, because there already were several "white whiskies" in the prospect's mind: gin, vodka, rum, tequila. Its own advertising (positioning it as a substitute for those spirits in the same drinks) undercut the "first" claim. Lesson: "advertising is not a debate, it's a seduction" — the prospect won't sit still for fine points of logic.
- **The everybody trap** — refusing to accept a narrow position because it seems to limit sales or opportunity, and trying instead to be all things to all people. This may keep an already-dominant incumbent afloat, but it is fatal when trying to build a position from nothing, because in a crowded field you cannot win by avoiding making enemies.

## 8. Repositioning the Competition

Where no open creneau exists (which, given the sheer number of brands per category, is most of the time), the underlying strategy must shift to **repositioning the competition**: to move a new idea into the mind, you must first move an old one out. The rule: never be afraid of conflict — conflict, even personal conflict, can build a reputation overnight (Sam Ervin needed Nixon; Nixon needed Alger Hiss; Ralph Nader became famous by attacking the world's largest corporation).

**Mechanics of repositioning, per the worked cases:**

- **Tylenol vs. aspirin.** The ad spent 60 words describing aspirin's downsides (stomach irritation, allergic/asthmatic triggers, hidden GI bleeding) before mentioning Tylenol at all. Result: Tylenol became the No. 1 analgesic, ahead of Anacin, Bayer, Bufferin, and Excedrin — repositioning an entire institution (aspirin), not just a competing brand.
- **Royal Doulton vs. Lenox.** "Royal Doulton. The china of Stoke-on-Trent, England vs. Lenox. The china of Pomona, New Jersey" repositioned Lenox — widely assumed to be imported — as domestic. Credited with a 6-point market-share gain. Principle: the repositioning claim must change the prospect's mind about the *competitor's* product, not just praise your own (Howard Gossage: the goal of advertising is sometimes "to terrorize your competition's copywriters").
- **Stolichnaya vs. American vodkas.** By naming competitors' actual manufacturing locations (Samovar: Schenley, PA; Smirnoff: Hartford, CT; Wolfschmidt: Lawrenceburg, IN) against Stolichnaya's genuine "Made in Leningrad, Russia," the ad exposed a category-wide pretense, driving a sales surge (temporarily interrupted, then resumed, after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan).
- **Wise vs. Pringle's.** Borden's Wise struck back at P&G's newfangled Pringle's (which had grabbed 18% share on a $15 million launch) by simply reading the ingredient labels on air: potatoes/vegetable oil/salt (Wise) vs. dehydrated potatoes/mono- and diglycerides/ascorbic acid/butylated hydroxy-anisole (Pringle's). Pringle's share fell from 18% to 10%, and the common complaint that Pringle's "tasted like cardboard" followed directly from the chemical-sounding label — taste, esthetic or otherwise, is in the mind. Once a brand is filed in the mental "penalty box marked loser," reversal is extremely hard, "as hard to bring Pringle's back as to bring Jimmy Carter back."
- **Scope vs. Listerine.** Two words — "medicine breath" — undercut Listerine's own long-running "the taste you hate, twice a day" positioning and knocked competitors Micrin and Binaca out of the market and eroded Lavoris. (The case is also used as a cautionary note on naming: "Scope" itself was judged a weak name, sounding like a board game rather than something that helps you with the opposite sex, limiting the campaign's full potential — see Section 10.)

**Repositioning vs. comparative advertising — a critical distinction.** "We're better than our competitors" is not repositioning; it's comparative advertising, and it's weak, because the prospect immediately asks "if your brand is so good, how come it's not the leader?" True repositioning changes what the prospect believes about the competitor's product; comparative advertising merely uses the competitor as a self-serving benchmark ("Ban is more effective than Right Guard, Secret, Sure...", met with "what else is new?").

**Is it legal? Is it ethical?** Named-brand comparative advertising became broadly permitted through a sequence of regulatory and industry shifts (NBC dropped its ban in 1964; FTC pressure led ABC and CBS to allow named-brand ads by 1972; the 4A's reversed its comparative-ad guidance in 1974; the UK's IBA allowed "knocking" ads in 1975). On ethics, the book's position: the old rule ("ignore competition, present every claim as if preemptive") assumed the prospect entered blank; the positioning era assumes the prospect already knows generic category benefits and must be reached by explicit reference to what's already lodged in the mind. The industry's discomfort with disparagement is treated as inconsistent with human nature ("people are ready to believe the worst about a product or person, but balk about believing the best") — the communication industry runs on bad news the way gossip does, and playing by that rule, done "honestly and fairly," keeps competitors on their toes.

## 9. The Power of the Name

**The name is the hook that hangs the brand on the product ladder.** In the positioning era, choosing the product's name is described as the single most important marketing decision. "A rose by any other name would not smell as sweet" — you smell (and taste, and judge) what you expect to, based on the name (a hypothetical "Alfred" perfume would not have sold like "Charlie"; renaming Hog Island to Paradise Island transformed its fortunes).

**How to choose a name:**

- Don't reach for arbitrary or backward-looking names (a French race-car driver's name, a rep's daughter's name). A name should begin the positioning process by telling the prospect the product's major benefit — Head & Shoulders, Intensive Care, Close-Up, DieHard, Shake 'n Bake, Edge.
- A descriptive/generic-leaning name is more effective than a vague one, but risks going "over the edge" into full genericness, which can cost you exclusive trademark rights (Miller "Lite" was corrupted into common usage — "Miller Lite" — and Miller lost exclusive claim to "light"/"lite" for beer; hence Schlitz Light, Coors Light, Bud Light).
- A strong, generic-adjacent, descriptive name is nonetheless the best long-term defense against me-too competitors muscling into your territory (People vs. the me-too Us).
- Names can also be too clever or too subtle: "Time" is memorable but ambiguous (could be a watch trade magazine); "Newsweek," the more generic name, has closed the gap. "Fortune" is similarly ambiguous versus the more direct "Business Week." Names also go obsolete as the world changes around them: "Esquire" lost ground to "Playboy" once nobody signed letters "Esq." anymore; "Yachting" is losing ground to "Sail" as ownership patterns shift; "Printer's Ink" gave way to "Advertising Age" once print stopped being the whole industry. "The Wall Street Journal" is judged a weak name for what is actually a general business paper, because it implies narrow financial scope.
- Avoid meaningless insider names (XD-12) and names that are technically accurate but psychologically wrong (Mennen E — "Vitamin E, incredibly, is a deodorant" — failed because prospects are literal).

**When to use a coined (meaningless) name:** only when you are unambiguously first in the mind with a product millions of people are certain to want — Coca-Cola, Kodak, Xerox all earned secondary meaning this way. (Coca-Cola's nickname "Coke" acquired such strong secondary meaning that its negative literal associations — coal residue, a narcotic — became irrelevant.) Absent that unique-and-certain-demand condition, stick to common descriptive words. As a naming mechanic: the five most common initial letters in English are S, C, P, A, T; the five least common are X, Z, Y, Q, K — relevant to how "findable" and pronounceable a coined name will feel.

**Negative names can be made positive by "bringing the product out of the closet."** A name that hides a product's true nature or origin (margarine hiding that it isn't made from milk) breeds suspicion. Deliberately using a more honest, origin-revealing name (the book's suggested "soy butter," paralleling "peanut butter") polarizes the situation but enables "pride of origin" and a long-term campaign of soy vs. cow butter. The same logic is applied to the shift from "colored" to "Negro" to "Black" as a self-naming strategy — "colored" under-polarizes ("the less colored the better"), while "black" enables "pride of blackness." Corn Products' shift from "corn syrup"/"dextrose" to "corn sugar" (aligned with cane and beet sugar) is offered as a commercial parallel.

**Rename the opposition before their name takes root, or reposition their existing name — but don't try to invent a rival label for an established term.** Attempts to counter "fair trade" by renaming it "price maintenance" failed and fair-trade laws persisted for years; a better tactic is turning the existing words inside out ("fair to the trade, but unfair to the consumer").

**Names affect people, not just products.** Experiments (Harari & McDavid) showed identical fourth/fifth-grade compositions graded a full letter grade higher when attributed to "David" or "Michael" than to "Hubert" or "Elmer." U.S. presidential history is cited as informal corroborating evidence (a run of "winner" first names — Jimmy, Jerry, Richard, Lyndon, John, Dwight, Harry, Franklin — with only "Herbert" breaking the pattern, and losing badly to "Franklin"). The general mechanism: a bad or inappropriate name triggers a self-confirming negative expectation ("Elmer is a loser... see, he's not doing that job very well... I told you, Elmer is a loser").

**It's not the name's aesthetic quality but its appropriateness that matters — the Eastern Airlines case.** Eastern is a large, established, heavily advertised carrier that nonetheless consistently ranks fourth among the top four U.S. domestic airlines on passenger surveys. The regional name "Eastern" places it in the prospect's mind alongside Piedmont, Ozark, and Southern rather than alongside national carriers like American and United — regardless of the airline's actual route network (which in fact extends well beyond the East Coast). Because expectation shapes interpretation, passengers attribute a bad American or United flight to "one of those things," but a bad Eastern flight to "that Eastern Airlines again."

**Name-collision problems between similarly named competitors** (the "Akron twins," B.F. Goodrich vs. Goodyear; the "Toledo triplets," Owens-Illinois / Owens-Corning Fiberglas / Libbey-Owens-Ford) show that even genuine technical firsts (Goodrich was first with domestic steel-belted radials) can be credited to a confusable, better-known rival (56% of surveyed buyers named Goodyear, which didn't even make them domestically, versus 47% naming Goodrich, which did). The remedy prescribed is simply: change the name. Objections about "equity in the present name" are answered with counter-examples of companies that changed successfully and profitably (Standard Oil of New Jersey → Exxon; Olin, Mobil, Uniroyal). **There is only negative equity in a bad name.**

## 10. The No-Name Trap

**The underlying mechanism: the mind works aurally, not visually**, even though we experience most business communication as text. Reading is a translation process from written symbol to internal sound; this is why beginning readers move their lips, and why names, headlines, and slogans should be evaluated for how they *sound*, even in print-only contexts.

**Phonetic shorthand explains when initials get adopted in everyday speech and when they don't.** People default to whichever form (full name or initials) is phonetically shorter. "Radio Corporation of America" (12 syllables) naturally compresses to "R-C-A" (3 syllables); "General Electric" (6 syllables) compresses to "G-E" (2). But "Ford Motor" is almost never "FM" because the single syllable "Ford" already says it all, and "New York" is almost never spoken as "N.Y." because both forms are two syllables. Where a shorter *word* already exists (Frisco for San Francisco, Jersey for New Jersey), people use the word, not the initials.

**Corporate initialism is a trap because it inverts cause and effect.** Companies see famous firms use initials (IBM, GE, AT&T, 3M) and copy the form, but the initials became usable *because* the full name was already famous — not the other way around. A 1980s Business Week subscriber survey found average brand awareness of 49% for "initial" companies versus 68% for a matched set of "name" companies — a 19-point gap. Using initials before achieving fame (GAF for General Aniline & Film; USM for United Shoe Machinery; SCM for Smith-Corona-Marchant; CPC for Corn Products Company) locks in obscurity rather than curing it, since the underlying full name never gets reinforced. Even acronym coincidences can misfire (GAF phonetically resembles "gaffe").

**Personal-name analogue:** young executives who adopt middle initials (E.G. Brown, J.S. Smith) to look established are borrowing a privilege (FDR, JFK, Ted Kennedy vs. E.M. Kennedy) that only comes *after* fame, not before it.

**Practical test:** in a phone directory, initial-named companies get filed together at the front of their letter grouping, competing directly with dozens of other initial-named entities (MCI Telecommunications sits 48 pages from where you'd intuitively look, competing with seven other "MCI" listings) — a purely mechanical cost of the initials strategy.

**Rules of thumb that emerge:**

- A company (or person) must already be extremely well known before initials will work (FBI, IRS work because "Federal Bureau of Investigation" and "Internal Revenue Service" are already known; HUD did not work initially because the full name wasn't yet well known).
- Name changes are sometimes necessary and can succeed spectacularly when done as an actual rename rather than an initialing (U.S. Rubber → Uniroyal; Eaton Yale & Towne → Eaton; Socony-Mobil → Mobil) — "forgetting the past" positioned these companies against the future, versus producing initialed "marketing monstrosities" (USR Corp, EY&T, SM Inc.).
- When two phonetically similar options exist (Pan American → "Pan Am" vs. the initials "PAA"; Trans World Airlines → "Trans World" vs. "TWA"), the shorter *pronounceable* word beats initials of equal or even shorter length, because initials require the listener to still mentally reconstruct the full name.

## 11. The Free-Ride Trap

The free-ride trap is line extension's precursor: using an established brand name on a new product specifically to save on the cost of building a new name, rather than to serve the prospect's mental map. The worked example: "Alka-Seltzer Plus," created to compete against Dristan and Contac, instead cannibalized Alka-Seltzer's own market — the label's "Plus" has grown over successive redesigns while "Alka-Seltzer" has shrunk. The counterfactual offered: naming it "Bromo-Seltzer Plus" would have taken share from the actual target competitors instead of self-cannibalizing.

**The conglomeration problem underlying this trap:** in the simpler product era, one company made one kind of thing and the name told the whole story (Standard Oil, U.S. Steel, U.S. Rubber, United Airlines). As companies diversified by internal development or external acquisition, two divergent naming instincts emerged — and corporate ego, more than logic, decides between them:

- Internal development tends to put the corporate name on the new product (General Electric computers).
- External acquisition tends to keep the acquired name (RCA kept Hertz; ITT kept Avis) — though not always (Xerox renamed its acquisition, Scientific Data Systems, to Xerox Data Systems).

**Divide and conquer — separate names outperform house names at scale.** Contrasting Procter & Gamble (Tide, Cheer, Bold — each positioned distinctly) against Colgate-Palmolive (Colgate toothpaste, Colgate toothbrushes, Palmolive Rapid Shave, Palmolive dishwashing liquid, Palmolive soap — all riding the house name): P&G does twice the business and five times the profit of Colgate-Palmolive with fewer brands, precisely because each P&G brand occupies its own unique mental niche rather than sharing — and diluting — one.

**A genuinely new product needs a genuinely new name**, because a well-known name is well known *because* it stands for something specific and sits atop a sharply defined ladder; a new product needs its own new ladder. Xerox's billion-dollar acquisition of Scientific Data Systems, immediately renamed "Xerox Data Systems," is offered as the classic counter-example.

**The teeter-totter principle:** one name cannot stand for two distinctly different product positions simultaneously — when one meaning rises, the other falls. "Xerox means copier, not computer" (an actual Xerox computer ad ran the self-defeating headline "This Xerox machine can't make a copy" — signaling trouble rather than reassurance). Heinz illustrates the same mechanism working in both directions: Heinz successfully became "ketchup" (the #1 brand) but in the process lost its prior pickle leadership to Vlasic.

**Anonymity is a squandered resource when spent too early or too diffusely.** "You can't beat somebody with a nobody" is treated as an obsolete political maxim (Gary Hart's rapid rise is the counter-case); publicity is compared to eating — "nothing kills the appetite quite as much as a hearty meal," and nothing kills a product or person's publicity potential quite as fast as a premature cover story. The prescription: conserve anonymity until ready to spend it, then spend it big, always with the explicit objective of building a mental position rather than generating publicity or communication for its own sake.

## 12. The Line-Extension Trap

Line extension — taking an established product name and applying it to a new product (Dial soap → Dial deodorant; Life Savers candy → Life Savers gum; Kleenex tissue → Kleenex towels) — is named the single most significant marketing trend of its decade, and the free-ride trap "carried to its ultimate conclusion." It is favored by seemingly airtight logic: trade acceptance, consumer familiarity, lower advertising costs, and corporate-ego satisfaction.

**Inside-out vs. outside-in thinking.** Inside-out logic (Dial soap customers will trust Dial deodorant because it comes from "the makers of the great Dial soap") sounds compelling from behind the desk but ignores how the prospect's mind is actually organized. Outside-in thinking works backward from the prospect: a strong brand name has become a **surrogate for the generic name itself** ("Where is the Bayer?" "Hand me the Dial."). Line extension **blurs that substitution** — the prospect can no longer say "Bayer" and mean aspirin unambiguously once "Bayer nonaspirin" exists, which paradoxically educates the prospect that Bayer is "just a brand name," destroying the illusion of category ownership. (Bayer nonaspirin never captured meaningful acetaminophen share, and Dial's own deodorant share stayed tiny relative to its dominant soap share.)

**JCPenney vs. DieHard — what actually gets driven into the mind is the *name*, used as a hook to hang attributes on.** "DieHard" is a strong hook for a long-lasting-battery claim; "JCPenney" (as a battery name) is a weak hook for the same claim, however logical it seemed to the retailer ("with our name on it, everyone will know where to buy it"). Prospects rank batteries by product attribute, not retailer identity — hence DieHard tops the mental battery ladder despite being sold only at Sears, while JCPenney's battery sells "in spite of," not "because of," its name.

**Two points of view on the same name.** Manufacturers and consumers see brand names completely differently: to Atlanta, Coca-Cola is a company, an institution, a brand, a workplace; to the consumer, it's a sweet dark carbonated drink — full stop. The strength of a truly generic-feeling brand name (Bayer = aspirin in the consumer's mind, with every rival brand read as "imitation Bayer") is precisely what line extension puts at risk, because "Bayer nonaspirin" contradicts the very identity ("if Bayer is aspirin, how can Bayer also be nonaspirin?").

**Documented casualty list, each illustrating a distinct failure mode:**

- **Protein 21 shampoo** (Mennen) — went from a 13% share as a focused product to 2% after extension into Protein 21 hairspray (regular/extra hold, scented/unscented), Protein 21 conditioner (two formulas), Protein 21 concentrate, and Protein 29 (for men).
- **Scott paper products** — ScotTowels, ScotTissue, Scotties, Scotkins, BabyScott diapers all undercut the base "Scott" name, which the text argues exists "in limbo," attached to no single product ladder. ScotTissue, once the #1 toilet-tissue brand, lost the top spot to Charmin. A large share of *market* did not mean Scott owned a share of *mind*.
- **Life Savers gum** — despite genuinely strong brand-attribute research ("excellence in flavor, outstanding value, dependable quality"), the extension flopped because those attributes, when asked directly, evoked no brand recall at all; asked instead "what's the candy with the hole?", people reliably answered "Life Savers." The name's real equity was narrow and specific, not broadly transferable. (By contrast, Life Savers Inc.'s **unrelated**, non-extended brand Bubble Yum — the first soft bubble gum, launched with its own identity — became a runaway success that came to outsell Life Savers candy itself.)
- **Eveready → the Duracell lesson.** Union Carbide initially refused to create a new name for its alkaline battery ("we've already got the best name in the battery business"), while P.R. Mallory's **Duracell** succeeded specifically because the name itself came to mean "alkaline power cell" (Duracell packaging needs no descriptive subtitle). Union Carbide eventually renamed its own alkaline line the **Energizer**, effectively conceding the naming lesson.
- **Pall Mall vs. Benson & Hedges (100mm cigarettes)** — Pall Mall Gold was the actual first 100mm cigarette, but Pall Mall then extended into Pall Mall Menthol, Pall Mall Extra Mild, Pall Mall Light 100s, diffusing its position; Benson & Hedges moved into the vacated territory and became known as the inventor of the format. Pall Mall's U.S. market share fell from 14.4% (1964, when it was the #1 brand) to 3.8% within about two decades, with the decline beginning the very year it first line-extended (1965).
- **Mazola vs. Fleischmann's (corn-oil margarine)** — Mazola corn oil was the leading liquid corn oil, and "Mazola corn-oil margarine" seemed the obviously logical extension; Fleischmann's, unencumbered by that internal logic, became the #1 corn-oil margarine brand instead.
- **Maxim vs. Taster's Choice (freeze-dried coffee)** — General Foods invented the freeze-dried category and named its entry "Maxim" as a spinoff of "Maxwell House" (an internal-logic pun most consumers never decoded). Nestlé's **Taster's Choice**, positioned with "tastes like ground roast" against the standard of quality coffee, ultimately outsold Maxim 2 to 1 despite entering the category second.
- **Jergens vs. Intensive Care** — Jergens introduced "Jergens Extra Dry" into a market of liquid lotions, but the new cream-lotion innovation was obscured by an insufficiently distinct name. Chesebrough-Pond's Intensive Care, with a name that clearly signaled the new form factor, became the #1 hand-lotion brand, outselling Jergens, Jergens Extra Dry, and Jergens' belated counter-launch (Direct Aid) combined.

**Reverse line extension ("broadening the base") can work, unlike forward extension.** Johnson's Baby Shampoo became a leading *adult* shampoo brand by promoting the existing product's mildness to a new audience — same product, same package, same label, only the application (not the name or form) changed. Had Johnson & Johnson instead line-extended into a separately named "Johnson's Adult Shampoo," the text argues it would have been far less successful. Blue Nun white wine (promoted as good with both fish and meat) and Arm & Hammer baking soda (promoted for refrigerators and drains) follow the same broadening-the-base logic — distinguished from the "everybody trap" (Section 7) by being the *first and only* brand in its category to make that specific broader claim.

## 13. When Line Extension Can Work

Line extension persists because it carries genuine **short-term advantages**: an extension name achieves an "instant flash of understanding" by riding the recognition of the parent name (Diet Coca-Cola), and it typically produces an immediate flash of *retailer* sales as the distribution pipeline fills — even before consumers have decided whether they actually want the product (booking $1 million in "sales" can require selling only $35 worth to each of many supermarkets). Early results can look excellent for roughly six months, purely from pipeline-fill, before reorders reveal the truth.

**The long-term disadvantage is that extension names remain "satellites"** with no independent mental position, and are correspondingly easy to forget (Schlitz Light, Pall Mall Extra Mild, Jergens Extra Dry "slide into — and out of — the mind effortlessly"). Their only lasting effect is to blur the original brand's position. Named historical casualties beyond the ones in Section 12: Ralston's "Ralston 1, 2, 3" (Shredded/Regular/Instant), Rinso White vs. Rinso Blue (which reportedly made David Ogilvy "break his pencil" writing the copy), Sara Lee's foray into frozen entrées (an $8 million loss, undermined by the dessert-brand associations of the Sara Lee name), Saturday Review's four-way magazine split (a $17 million loss), Levi's for Feet, Avis flowers, Zenith watches, Old Grand-Dad tobacco, Bic pantyhose, Kleenex diapers, Pierre Cardin wine, Chanel for Men — plus even Liquid Tide, introduced by P&G at a cost of $50 million despite decades of otherwise resisting the fad, which was expected to cannibalize Tide powder without dislodging category leader Wisk.

**Two practical diagnostic tests for whether a name still holds a clean position:**

- **The shopping-list test.** Write brand names on a shopping list and predict what a shopper would actually bring home. "Kleenex, Bayer, Dial" still reliably resolve to tissue, aspirin, and soap. "Heinz, Scott, Kraft" do not resolve cleanly at all — which product line will your spouse bring back?
- **The bartender test.** Ordering "J&B on the rocks," "a Beefeater martini," or "a bottle of Dom Perignon" reliably produces the right drink. Ordering "Cutty on the rocks" leaves ambiguous whether you meant Cutty Sark or the pricier Cutty 12 — and "step-up" extensions like Cutty 12 or Johnnie Walker Black Label (versus Red Label) usually generate anemic sales at the premium tier, because customers reaching for status default to an unambiguous premium name (Chivas Regal) rather than risk being misheard ordering a modifier.

**Step-down extensions are the mirror-image trap: instantly successful, then fatal.** Packard, once the premier American status car (more prestigious than Cadillac; armored Packards were built for heads of state including FDR), introduced the cheaper Packard Clipper — its best-selling car ever, and the beginning of the end for Packard's prestige position and, ultimately, the company itself (absorbed by Studebaker in 1954).

**A name is compared to a rubber band: it will stretch, but the more you stretch it, the weaker it becomes** (the opposite of the intuitive expectation that more usage strengthens a name). Whether to stretch is partly a straightforward economics call — a canned-vegetable line (Del Monte) may reasonably use one house name across peas, corn, and beans because separate names for each aren't commercially justified — until a focused single-category competitor (Dole, in canned pineapple) wins outright against the diffused generalist. Dole's own subsequent extension into fresh bananas illustrates that the same teeter-totter risk applies to the challenger once it succeeds: making "Dole" mean bananas risks weakening "Dole" as pineapple.

**Five formal "rules of the road" for when to use the house name vs. a new name:**

- **Expected volume.** Potential winners should not bear the house name; small-volume products should.
- **Competition.** In a vacuum (no real competitors), the brand need not bear the house name; in a crowded field, it should.
- **Advertising support.** Big-budget brands should not bear the house name; small-budget brands should.
- **Significance.** Genuine breakthrough products should not bear the house name; commodity products (e.g., chemicals) should.
- **Distribution.** Off-the-shelf items should not bear the house name; items sold through direct sales reps should.

The underlying logic: not every product is actually *sold* by being *positioned* — many low-interest categories (canned peas) are simply picked up without a going-in brand preference, in which case any well-known name outperforms an unknown one, and a company with thousands of small-volume SKUs (3M is the example given) cannot realistically mint a distinct name for each.

## 14. Positioning a Company: The Xerox Case Study

Companies get "bought" in more contexts than a literal acquisition: a prospective employee "buys" the company by accepting a job, and the strongest-positioned employers (General Electric over "the Schenectady Electrical Works") get the best graduates; an investor buying stock is really paying for a piece of the company's *position*, now and projected forward, and the multiple they'll pay (six times earnings vs. sixty) tracks the strength of that position in the buyer's mind.

**The name problem recurs at the corporate level.** Pullman is barely a factor in railroad cars anymore, and bus revenue is a small fraction of Greyhound's actual business — yet public perception, anchored to the name, has scarcely budged despite Greyhound spending millions telling the financial community it is "more than a bus company." As long as the dog logo is on buses on the interstate, that corporate messaging is largely wasted; the actual fix would be a new name.

**A corporate position needs to "stand for something" specific — and "our people" and "diversification" are named as the two most common, least effective corporate themes.** "Our people are our greatest resource" (used, with variations, by Gulf and Grumman in the text) fails because it implicitly concedes nothing distinctive — bigger, more successful companies are simply assumed to have better people by default, so claiming it only works if you're already on top; if you're not on top, claiming better people creates an unresolved inconsistency in the prospect's mind. Diversification fares no better, because **strong mental positions are built on major, singular achievements, not broad product lines**: GE is known as the world's largest electrical manufacturer, not a diversified maker of many product types (and its unsuccessful ventures, like computers, have tended to be its nonelectrical ones); GM is the world's largest automaker, not a diversified industrial conglomerate; IBM is the world's largest computer manufacturer, not a broad office-machine maker. Even the stock market is said to consistently undervalue true conglomerates (ITT, Gulf & Western) relative to their parts.

**Case: Xerox and the "third-leg" strategy.** Xerox already owns an enormously valuable position — "the Coca-Cola of copiers" (nobody else's copier brand comes quickly to mind, even though Sharp, Savin, Ricoh, Royal, Canon, IBM, and Kodak all make them). Xerox's actual strategic problem was how to grow beyond that single rung without destroying it. Its first attempt — buying Scientific Data Systems and renaming it Xerox Data Systems, explicitly to broaden into "information systems" the way IBM was broadening into graphics — folded within six years and cost an additional $84.4 million write-off, without denting the underlying lesson that "Xerox means copier, not computer" (Section 12's teeter-totter principle applied at corporate scale).

The prescribed alternative framing: picture the "office of the past" as three legs — a telephone (AT&T), a typewriter (IBM), a copier (Xerox). The "office of the present" shows nearly all disruption happening in the typewriter leg (supplanted by computers), while the telephone and copier legs stay comparatively stable. Rather than betting (as most competitors were) on a single-vendor "office of the future" controlled by one company (widely assumed to be IBM) — a bet the text argues against by analogy to failed single-vendor predictions in high-fidelity audio and kitchen appliances — Xerox is advised to bet on a **persistent three-leg future**: AT&T's telephone leg becoming a broader *communication* leg (voice mail, facsimile), IBM's typewriter leg becoming an *input/processing* leg (computers, workstations, networks), and Xerox's copier leg becoming an **output leg**.

**Cross-leg difficulty as supporting evidence:** Xerox has struggled in computers/workstations/networks (IBM's leg); IBM has struggled with copiers (Xerox's leg); nobody, including Xerox, has done well with facsimile (AT&T's leg, until AT&T itself pushes it); AT&T has struggled with computers (IBM's leg) and IBM/Rolm has struggled with telephones (AT&T's leg, e.g., Satellite Business Systems losing $100 million/year). The recurring cross-leg failure pattern is treated as evidence that the copier/computer gap, rather than being Xerox's weakness, could become its strongest long-run ally if it commits to the output leg instead of chasing the input leg.

**The proposed vehicle for the output-leg position: a coined technology word, "lasography".** Modeled explicitly on the pattern of two prior office technologies that entered the dictionary — thermography (3M) and xerography (Xerox itself) — "lasography" is proposed to describe laser-based printing, scanning, storing, and communicating of optical/printed material. Its stated advantages: it connects verbally to Xerox's own prior technology-naming pattern ("-ography"), it signals genuine newness (which businesses respond to), and it lets Xerox claim the *next* generation of the same output category it already dominates, rather than trying to annex someone else's leg. General lesson stated explicitly: **in the positioning game you can't sit still — you must constantly keep your position targeted to today's problems and today's markets.**

## 15. Positioning a Place: Country and Island Case Studies

**Case: Sabena / Belgium.** Sabena Belgian World Airlines flew nonstop from North America to exactly one European city (Brussels), and Belgium itself occupied one of the bottom rungs (if any) of the mental "country ladder" for prospective travelers — most Americans thought Waterloo was a Paris suburb and could not place Belgium's most important product beyond waffles. Sabena's actual advertising strategy (selling in-flight food and service — "Do I have to be a bon vivant to fly Sabena?") was diagnosed as strategically backwards: no amount of onboard charm compensates for a destination the traveler doesn't want to go to. **The moral stated explicitly: "Whether you're selling colas, companies, or countries: out of mind, out of business."**

**The reframe: position the country, not the airline** (as KLM had already done for Amsterdam). The method for finding a country's position: successful countries and cities all carry strong, specific mental picture-postcards (England → pageantry, Big Ben, Tower of London; Italy → the Coliseum, St. Peter's; Amsterdam → tulips, Rembrandt, canals; France → food, the Eiffel Tower, the Riviera; New York → a skyline; San Francisco → cable cars and the Golden Gate Bridge). The correct sequence is explicitly *not* (1) sift through postcards for the perfect image, nor (2) go shoot thousands of new photos hoping one clicks — both approaches were tried and failed. The correct sequence is (3) **first look into the prospect's mind for images that already exist, then tie the new subject to one of them.**

Because London, Paris, and Rome already own the top-of-the-ladder positions for first-time European travelers (an unwinnable head-on fight — Section 5's principle applied to tourism), the identified opening was the *next tier* of experienced travelers looking for destinations like Greece or Switzerland. The winning device: an existing external authority (the Michelin Guide) rating cities, not just restaurants — Michelin's Benelux guide listed six three-star "worth a special journey" cities, five of them in Belgium (Bruges, Ghent, Antwerp, Brussels, Tournai), versus only one (Amsterdam) in Holland. The resulting ad, "In beautiful Belgium, there are five Amsterdams," (1) related the unknown (Belgium) to a destination already fixed in the mind (Amsterdam), (2) borrowed credibility from a separate, already-trusted authority (Michelin), and (3) turned "five cities" into proof that Belgium was a legitimate multi-day destination, not a pass-through. The program's eventual failure to sustain itself is attributed not to the strategy but to a change in management commitment — **illustrating that a successful positioning program requires sustained, long-term commitment from whoever is in charge, which is especially hard to guarantee in a politically changing environment.**

**Case: Jamaica.** Commissioned amid a broader push (backed by a 25-company Rockefeller-organized investment group) to develop the island following a change in government. The strategic question of investment vs. tourism first was resolved by recognizing they are linked: favorable personal tourist experiences by traveling executives feed back into corporate investment decisions (illustrated by the asymmetry that almost no one invests in wintertime Alaska but plenty invest in the Caribbean — "who wants to go to Fairbanks in the wintertime to check out the plant?").

Jamaica's four main tourist competitors (Bahamas, Puerto Rico, U.S. Virgin Islands, Bermuda) mostly share one generic visual cliché: a couple in bathing suits under palm trees on a beach — a cliché so undifferentiated the text warns that if your visuals resemble everyone else's, "the mind will simply dump all those visuals in a slot marked 'Islands in the Caribbean' and tune out." The one competitor that broke the cliché, Bermuda (built around motorbikes on pink sand), ranked second in desirability only to the U.S. Virgin Islands, and would likely have ranked first if not disadvantaged by its cooler, more northerly climate — direct evidence that a distinct visual position beats the generic category cliché even for a smaller destination.

The positioning solution again followed the "look into the mind first" method (not "search for the one perfect postcard," not "shoot new photos and hope"): **the "Hawaii of the Caribbean."** Jamaica is factually larger and higher-elevation than all four listed competitors (given in comparative mileage/height figures in the source), supporting genuine "more to see, more to do" claims consistent with the Hawaiian analogy (big green island, cool mountains, deserted beaches). The borrowed-position tactic transfers years of Hawaii's already-built mental imagery onto Jamaica at a fraction of the time and cost it would take to build a wholly original image, while simultaneously differentiating Jamaica sharply from its four direct Caribbean rivals and opening a distinct pitch to European travelers (for whom Hawaii itself is a long, unattractive haul, but the Caribbean is comparatively close).

## 16. Positioning a Product: The Milk Duds Case Study

**Step one of any positioning program: look inside the mind of the actual prospect**, not the product itself — described elsewhere as a search for the obvious that is easy to miss "because it's too easy to see" (an explicit nod to Poe's "The Purloined Letter"). For Milk Duds, the real prospect was identified as a sophisticated ten-year-old candy buyer: someone who has been in and out of candy stores hundreds of times, and functions as a cautious, shrewd purchasing agent constantly checking value received — not "some little kid who doesn't know the score."

**What's actually occupying that prospect's mind when "candy" comes up is not Milk Duds — it's candy bars** (Hershey's, Nestlé's, Mounds, Almond Joy, Reese's, Snickers, Milky Way), reinforced by the sheer weight of those brands' advertising budgets, which dwarfed Milk Duds'. Given that budget mismatch, building a wholly separate identity for Milk Duds was judged hopeless. The strategic move instead: **reposition the entire candy-bar category**, turning the competition's own massive ad spend into unpaid groundwork for Milk Duds by setting the brand up as the *alternative* to the category, not another entry within it.

**The exploited weakness: candy bars don't last.** As candy bars had shrunk in size over the years, consumer dissatisfaction had grown correspondingly, evidenced by informally gathered consumer complaints quoted directly ("my allowance doesn't last very long when it comes to candy bars"; "either I'm eating faster or candy bars are getting smaller"). Milk Duds, by contrast, come in a box of 15 individually slow-eating chocolate-covered caramels rather than a single fast-consumed bar — already the reason the product had organically done well in movie theaters.

**Resulting position: "America's long-lasting alternative to the candy bar."** The text notes with some irony that in roughly 15 years of prior Milk Duds television advertising, the long-lasting angle had never once been used — despite being, in the author's framing, the obvious answer sitting in plain sight. The actual 30-second commercial built around this position is walked through beat by beat (a big-mouthed character disappointed when candy bars run out too fast, delighted by long-lasting Milk Duds, culminating in the jingle "When a candy bar is only a memory, you'll still be eating your Milk Duds"). Result: a reversed downward sales trend and the highest sales volume in the company's history to that point.

**Stated general lesson: the solution to a positioning problem is usually found in the prospect's mind, not in the product.**

## 17. Positioning a Service: The Mailgram Case Study

**Products and services are positioned by the same underlying strategic logic; the differences are mainly technique-level, not principle-level.** One recurring technique difference given: product advertising tends to lean visual (the picture *is* the message — a big car photo signals a car ad, not a car-rental-service ad), while service advertising tends to lean verbal, because a service has no inherent physical form to photograph. Accordingly, Milk Duds (a product) was primarily advertised on television (a visual medium), while Mailgram (a service) tested best on radio (a verbal medium) across a four-way media test against newspapers, magazines, and television — though the text stresses this generalization has plenty of exceptions (a well-known visual symbol, like O.J. Simpson for Hertz, can make a service effectively visual).

**A cautionary pattern named explicitly: getting lost in your own service's technical complexity and forgetting the customer.** Mailgram — a genuinely novel piece of infrastructure (developed jointly with the U.S. Postal Service, launched experimentally in 1970, transmittable via phone, telex, TWX, magnetic tape, computer, facsimile, or communicating typewriters) — is used to illustrate how the natural instinct of the people who built something complex and expensive is to lead with that complexity ("a new, automated, computerized electronic communication service"), which the text treats as a near-universal marketing failure mode: the more interesting and technically complex the service, the more likely the marketer is to talk about the service instead of the customer's actual problem.

**The correct approach: relate the new thing to what is already fixed in the prospect's mind — in this case, the Telegram.** Saying "Western Union" reliably conjures the iconic yellow Telegram message, an association the "-gram" suffix in "Mailgram" deliberately reinforces rather than avoids. This produced two live, competing positioning strategies, both grounded in the same principle but pointed at different existing mental anchors:

- **"The low-cost telegram"** — "Mailgram: Impact of a Telegram at a fraction of the cost," directly comparing the new blue-and-white Mailgram (roughly one-third the price) against Western Union's own established yellow Telegram, on the shared attributes of telegraphic format and demand for immediate attention.
- **"The high-speed letter"** — an alternative approach relating Mailgram instead to the U.S. mail, positioning it as a faster substitute for a letter rather than a cheaper substitute for a telegram.

**The internal objection to the first strategy is recorded explicitly, along with why it was overridden.** The stated concern was that positioning Mailgram against the Telegram would cannibalize Western Union's own declining Telegram business, and that comparing a modern new service against an old, fading one seemed backwards. The text concedes the logic is "impeccable" but insists that **logic is not necessarily the best strategy for dealing with human minds** — because regardless of internal cannibalization concerns, the Telegram association is what genuinely already lives in the prospect's head, and a positioning strategy has to start from where the mind already is, not from where internal business logic says it should be protected.

## 18. Positioning a Regional/Retail Service: Bank Case Studies

**Positioning a bank (or any local retail service) requires genuine, granular knowledge of the specific competitive territory — it is not a national, one-size-fits-all exercise.** Two contrasting cases are used to show that the *same* underlying threat (encroachment by big money-center banks) can call for different specific strategies depending on the local bank's actual competitive standing.

**Case: Long Island Trust.** For years the largest bank on Long Island by branch count and profit, its position was disrupted when a New York State law permitted unrestricted statewide branch banking, letting Citibank, Chase Manhattan, and Chemical Bank move directly into its home territory — compounded by the fact that many Long Island residents already commuted into, and banked in, New York City. Formal **semantic differential research** (asking prospects to rank each competing bank, typically on a 1–10 scale, against a defined set of attributes) was used to map exactly where Long Island Trust stood relative to the invading city banks. The attributes tested: many branches, full range of services, quality of service, large capital, helps Long Island residents, and helps the Long Island economy — the first four being conventional bank-choice criteria, the last two specific to the local competitive situation. This kind of formal mapping is presented as valuable less for generating brand-new insight (some positioning conclusions are simply intuitive, needing no research) and more for **building internal conviction and buy-in with management**, since a chief executive who has spent thirty years inside a company will perceive it completely differently than an outside prospect whose total lifetime exposure to it might total only minutes.

The resulting strategy exploited the one dimension where "helps Long Island" outscored the invading banks: local commitment and rootedness. Advertising directly contrasted Long Island Trust's decades of local presence and the fact that 95% of its loans and services went to Long Islanders, against the city banks' comparatively thin, generic local presence and (pointedly) their far-flung foreign branch networks (one execution used a literal photo of palm trees in front of a Citibank branch sign to make the point that "a branch in Nassau isn't necessarily *your* Nassau" — it might be the Bahamas, not Nassau County).

**Case: United Jersey Bank.** A meaningfully different situation: unlike Long Island Trust, United Jersey was not the largest local bank in its own territory (ranking third, behind First Fidelity and Midlantic), and it was squeezed geographically between the big New York City banks to the north and the big Philadelphia banks to the south. Because state and federal regulation made the actual services on offer largely undifferentiable between competitors, the same underlying method used for Tylenol (Section 9) was applied explicitly: **you don't find the answer by studying your own bank — you find it by studying the competition's actual weakness.**

The identified structural weakness of the giant city banks was slow decision-making and slow service, simply as a mechanical consequence of size (echoing an old Avis line: "Rent from us. The line at our counter is shorter."). The resulting positioning theme, **"the fast-moving bank,"** had two explicit halves that had to work together:

- **Exploit the competitor's actual weakness** (slow reaction time at large, bureaucratic city banks) in the advertising.
- **Force the bank's own real operations to match the advertised promise before running the advertising**, via seven concrete internal commitments: decentralized lending decisions (with a lending team meeting daily, able to approve loans up to $10 million), cross-training staff across all banking services (so customers aren't shuffled between specialists), heavy commitment to electronic infrastructure (the state's largest private ATM network, direct computer access for corporate customers), speedy lock-box check-collection service (with its own dedicated ZIP code), fast electronic point-of-sale authorization terminals for merchants, an explicit commitment to responsiveness on rates and new products, and a centrally located headquarters (Princeton) chosen specifically to minimize travel time to any business customer in the state.

The advertising itself (in a recurring television format) contrasted real United Jersey bankers against a fictional, deliberately named "Lethargic National Bank," dramatizing the competitor weakness directly (endless loan approval waits, a banker who disappears when needed, an installment loan handled in exaggerated slow motion), under the tagline that United Jersey "values your time as much as your money." Print carried parallel lines ("Haste makes money," "Banks shouldn't hold up people"), and internal "Time Is Money" desk signs were distributed to bank officers as a non-subtle internal reinforcement of the same promise.

**Results and the stated internal-culture payoff:** unaided awareness of United Jersey nearly tripled within about a year; earnings rose $30 million (a 26% increase) the following year. A United Jersey officer's quoted internal reaction is treated as the most significant result of all — staff visibly began trying to live up to "Fast Moving" in their actual daily behavior (faster approvals, less sitting on requests) — offered as proof of the book's broader claim that **the essence of a good positioning strategy is that it transcends every part of a company; you know you have a winner "when you run it up the corporate flagpole and everybody salutes."**

## 19. Positioning With Borrowed Authority: The Stowe Ski Resort Case Study

Even an already well-regarded product can benefit from a formal position, because reputation alone travels by word of mouth only as far and as accurately as the *specific comparisons* people already have in their heads to talk about it with. Stowe, Vermont, already carried a strong informal reputation (an Advertising Age columnist described it as "the essential Eastern ski village, the way Aspen is out west or Val d'Isère is in France or Kitzbühel is in Austria") — but that same quote reveals the actual content skiers use when discussing ski destinations: **comparisons to other named, prestige ski resorts.** The positioning task was therefore to supply and formalize that exact comparison, rather than invent a new claim from scratch.

**The mechanism used: an outside expert supplying credibility for a comparative ranking claim.** Ski and travel writer Abby Rand, writing in Harper's Bazaar, published a list of the world's top ten ski resorts, and Stowe was one of the ten (alongside Aspen, Courchevel, Jackson Hole, Kitzbühel, Portillo, St. Christoph, St. Moritz, Sun Valley, and Vail). The resulting advertising ("Of the world's top 10 ski resorts, only one is in the East... You don't have to go to the Alps or the Andes or even to the Rockies... You need only head for the Ski Capital of the East: Stowe, Vermont") drove a measurable spike in brochure requests and set attendance records at the resort — despite Stowe's genuine geographic disadvantage against closer competitors to the major New York City market (Stratton, Sugarbush, Big Bromley, Mt. Snow all require less driving time), which the advertising had to implicitly justify by promising a proportionally larger reward for the longer drive.

**Why the "top 10" list format specifically works, and why it's durable:** it exploits the same mental habit already noted in Section 2 (the mind copes with complexity by converting it into ranked, bounded lists — "the Seven Wonders of the World" is cited as an ancient precedent for exactly this cognitive shortcut). A "top 10" position, once established through a credible outside source, requires no further defending or updating — there is no obviously better claim than being named among the world's best, so the position can be run indefinitely without becoming stale.

**Underlying psychological principle: deference to authority.** Citing a recognized outside authority (here, a named expert travel writer in a national magazine) works because it lets the prospect avoid trusting their own uncertain judgment. The text explicitly flags the darker version of this same mechanism via Allen Funt (creator of Candid Camera): people can be led by even the most minimal signals of authority — Funt's own field example being a fake "Delaware Closed Today" road sign, to which confused motorists' actual response was not disbelief but "Is Jersey open?"

## 20. Positioning an Institution: The Catholic Church Case Study

The book explicitly generalizes its framework beyond commerce: "This book could have been written about religion just as well as about advertising," because **the essence of any religion is communication** — from divinity, to clergy, to congregation — and, per the text, the recurring point of failure is specifically at the clergy layer, the communication intermediary, not at either end.

**Diagnosed problem: an unmanaged "identity crisis" following Vatican II.** Prior to Vatican II, the institutional Church held a clear, consistent position in the minds of the faithful: teacher of the law, with heavy emphasis on rules, rewards, and punishment, applied consistently across generations. Vatican II shifted the Church away from that posture (loosening many rules, changing liturgy and style, favoring flexibility over rigidity) — but, crucially, **no equivalent "corporate communication" function existed to explain the new direction in simple terms**, an omission the text likens explicitly to a company the size of General Motors running no overall corporate advertising program and letting all communication default to individual local dealers, "some of it good, much of it bad."

**Consequences cited as evidence of the resulting confusion:** regular Mass attendance fell below 50% of the Catholic population for the first time (a 20% drop, while Protestant attendance stayed stable over the same period); priests, nuns, and brothers fell 20% versus a decade earlier, with vocations down 60%; and — despite the Catholic Church being independently characterized by Protestant theologian Peter Berger as "the largest community of moral authority in American society" — a U.S. News & World Report survey of 24,000 influential executives ranked the Church and organized religion generally dead last in institutional influence.

**Diagnostic method applied, explicitly modeled on corporate identity work:** just as positioning a company requires retracing its history to rediscover its "basic business" (old plans, old programs, what worked and didn't), positioning the Church required retracing two millennia via Scripture rather than annual reports. Two specific Gospel passages were identified as containing the Church's actual, definable role: God's instruction (Matthew 17:23) to listen to the words of Christ, and Christ's own instruction (Matthew 28:19) to his followers to go and teach all nations what they had heard from him.

**Resulting proposed position: "teacher of the word"** (rather than the pre-Vatican-II "teacher of the law") — the Church's role framed as keeping Christ's message alive and relevant for each new generation, in each generation's own idiom, rather than a fixed rulebook. The text is explicit that Vatican II, viewed through this lens, "seemed to point the Church backward rather than forward" — i.e., toward an older, textually grounded role rather than a genuinely novel one.

**Stated pattern: positioning work is "a search for the obvious," and the obvious concept is simultaneously the easiest to communicate and the hardest to get accepted**, precisely because the human mind tends to admire complexity and dismiss simplicity as simplistic (the contrasting case cited: theologian Avery Dulles's academically well-regarded framework proposing the Church has not one but *six* distinct roles).

**Proposed implementation elements, once the position was set:** pulpit training to sharpen clergy as public speakers (with the pointed observation that, at the time, the most effective religious speakers were found on Sunday morning television, not in most churches), and an introductory film (proposed title: *Return to the Beginning*) to supply the emotional "drama" needed to launch any major communication effort — paralleling the text's general claim elsewhere that film/television is disproportionately effective for new-position introductions.

**Outcome: the recommendation was not adopted.** The text attributes this explicitly to institutional resistance (bishops resisting guidance from laity) and to the same obviousness-aversion named above — "simplicity is not as attractive as complexity" — and closes the case with open skepticism about whether a subsequent Vatican synod convened to address the same confusion would actually resolve it.

## 21. Positioning Yourself and Your Career

The book explicitly extends the same mechanics to individual careers: **"If positioning strategies can be used to promote a product, why can't they be used to promote yourself? No reason at all."**

**Define yourself around one concept.** People "suffer from the same disease as products" by trying to be all things to all people — but the mind of the prospect (here, the professional world) can barely hold one clear concept per person, and almost never two or three. The stated task is to identify a single sharp, ownable concept ("I'm the best lawyer in Dallas") and then spend a career establishing and defending that specific position — acknowledged as requiring talent, luck, and sustained strategy, but explicitly not something most people do, because "they vacillate. They expect others to do it for them."

**Make mistakes — deliberately accept a higher failure rate in exchange for more attempts.** Framed as: "anything worthwhile doing is worthwhile doing lousy," because waiting for the ability to do something perfectly risks never doing it at all. Supporting examples: Ty Cobb (96 stolen-base attempts out of 134, a 70% success rate, still remembered) versus Max Carey (51 of 53, a 96% success rate, largely forgotten); jockey Eddie Arcaro reportedly losing 250 consecutive races before his first win.

**Get your own name right, using the same principles as Sections 9–10.** Illustrative renamings: Leonard Slye → Roy Rogers; Marion Morrison → John Wayne; Issur Danielovitch → Isadore Demsky → Kirk Douglas. The **no-name trap applies personally**: young executives who adopt middle initials on memos (mimicking senior figures they've observed using initials) are borrowing a privilege that, as with corporate initials, only becomes usable *after* fame is already established, not before — and if you genuinely need a middle initial to differentiate yourself from other people who share your name, the actual fix is a different name altogether, because nobody will bother making that distinction on your behalf.

**The line-extension trap applies personally too.** Naming children "Mary 1, Mary 2, Mary 3" (or even Mary/Marian/Marilyn) is treated as creating exactly the same lifetime confusion a line-extended product name creates; a "Junior" suffix does a son no favor by denying him a separate identity. In entertainment specifically, even a famous parent's surname can be a liability rather than an asset for a rising performer: Liza Minnelli, performing under her own distinct identity, became a bigger star than her mother Judy Garland ever was, whereas Frank Sinatra Jr. is offered as the harder case — carrying an inherited name sets up an automatic unfavorable comparison ("he's not going to be able to sing as well as his father"), and, consistent with the "you hear what you expect to hear" principle from Section 2, the audience's expectation becomes self-confirming.

**"Find a horse to ride" — success is framed as being driven primarily by what others do for you, not by individual effort alone**, explicitly inverting Kennedy's line: "ask not what you can do for your company. Ask what your company can do for you." Six specific "horses" are named:

- **Your company.** Assess honestly whether it's actually going anywhere; a losing company sinks everyone aboard regardless of individual talent ("even the best officer on the Titanic wound up in the same lifeboat as the worst"). Favor genuine growth industries (at the time of writing: computers, electronics, optics, communications) and note that services broadly were growing faster than hard products (banking, leasing, insurance, medical, financial, consulting). When changing jobs into a growth field, evaluate expected future pay, not just the immediate offer.
- **Your boss.** Apply the identical "is this person actually going anywhere" test to whoever you report to, and deliberately seek out the smartest, most competent boss available rather than one who makes you look comparatively better by contrast ("a fresh flower stands out better if it's surrounded by wilted ones" is named explicitly as the wrong instinct, since underperforming teams tend to get replaced wholesale). Positioning yourself as someone eager to work with and learn from the best in your specialty is framed as more hireable than positioning yourself as an indispensable specialist the organization supposedly needs.
- **A friend.** Personal friends and business friends are treated as distinct categories with different utility; most career breaks are attributed to a business-friend referral, not to personal friendship, and this network has to be actively maintained *before* it's needed (the specific warning example: an old contact resurfacing after ten years to "have lunch" reliably means they need a job, and by then it is usually too late to build the relationship from scratch).
- **An idea.** Attributed loosely to Victor Hugo ("nothing... can stop an idea whose time has come"), with the caveat that a genuinely new idea will, by definition, provoke resistance and controversy rather than immediate universal agreement — a psychologist's observation (Charles Osgood) is cited that the vigor of opposition to a principle can itself be read as one indicator of the principle's validity, because obviously-wrong ideas get ignored rather than actively fought. An idea or position with no element of conflict in it is dismissed as "motherhood, apple pie, and the flag, revisited" — not really a position at all.
- **Faith (in others' ideas).** Illustrated by Ray Kroc, described as an older, largely unsuccessful man until he backed two brothers' idea (and name) that he himself hadn't originated — the McDonald brothers sold both to him for comparatively little, and Kroc, not the brothers, became one of the wealthiest people in America off the resulting chain.
- **Yourself.** Named as the sixth and final horse, but explicitly framed as "mean, difficult, and unpredictable," with self-reliance alone rarely sufficient — business, like life, is inherently a cooperative activity as much as a competitive one (you cannot complete a sale without someone else choosing to buy), and the closing metaphor makes the same point directly: **"the winningest jockeys are not necessarily the lightest, the smartest, or the strongest... the jockey that wins the race is usually the one with the best horse."**

## 22. Six Questions to Position Your Business

A structured self-diagnostic sequence, explicitly framed as simple to *ask* but difficult to *answer* honestly, intended to organize strategic thinking before jumping to a solution.

**1. What position do you already own?** This is a question to be answered from the marketplace, not from the marketing manager — corporate ego must be kept out of the answer, and formal research spending is explicitly endorsed if that's what it takes to get an accurate read, because it's better to know the truth now than to discover it later when nothing can be done. The instruction to "look at the big picture, not the details" is illustrated by the earlier cases: Sabena's real problem was Belgium's position, not Sabena's; 7-Up's real problem was the cola category's overwhelming share of mind ("get me a soda" defaulting to Coke or Pepsi), not any specific attitude toward lemon-lime drinks specifically. Most products, the text notes, are in 7-Up's pre-"uncola" situation: holding weak or nonexistent positions in most prospects' minds, and needing to hook onto something that's already there rather than starting from a blank slate.

**2. What position do you want to own?** "Own" is emphasized as the operative word — many programs set out to communicate a position that's already taken (Ford's Edsel found no room in the buyer's mind for another heavily chromed, medium-priced car) or too broad to be ownable or defensible (Rheingold beer's attempt to court every ethnic group in New York City individually — "Italians drinking Rheingold, Blacks drinking Rheingold, Irish drinking Rheingold, Jews drinking Rheingold" — ended up appealing to nobody, since one group's endorsement carried no weight, and often active dislike, with another). By contrast, Richardson-Merrill deliberately avoided head-on conflict with Contac and Dristan by preempting the narrower, unclaimed "nighttime cold remedy" position with Nyquil, which became the company's most successful new product launch in years. The everybody trap (Section 7) is explicitly reinvoked here as the personal-and-corporate failure mode of wanting too much position at once.

**3. Whom must you outgun?** If the intended position requires a head-to-head fight against an established leader, "forget it" — go around the obstacle, not over it, and pick a position nobody else has a firm grip on. The instruction is to spend as much strategic thought on the competitive landscape as on your own offering, because **prospects don't buy, they choose** among available brands — your product's actual merit matters less than its position among the alternatives on offer. Repositioning a category leader or an entire category (as Tylenol did to aspirin) is one route to a genuinely viable position; failing to do this is shown concretely in the Nuprin ($35 million launch, Bristol-Myers) and Advil ($40 million launch, American Home Products) cases, both of which introduced a genuinely new analgesic ingredient (ibuprofen) but neither of which bothered to reposition the dominant incumbent, Tylenol — and neither captured more than a marginal share as a result.

**4. Do you have enough money?** Positioning costs real money at every stage: building a share of mind, establishing a position, and holding a position once established, all require sustained spend, especially given the sheer volume of competing noise (the average mind is said to absorb roughly 200,000 advertising messages a year, against which even a $500,000 Super Bowl commercial buys only one of those 200,000 impressions). A named tactical response to limited budgets: **narrow the geographic scope of the launch** rather than spreading thin nationally — better to overspend in one city and prove the concept than underspend everywhere, then roll out from a proven, appropriately chosen base market (the specific example: establishing the No. 1 scotch position in New York, the country's largest scotch-drinking market, before a national rollout).

**5. Can you stick it out?** Positioning is explicitly framed as a cumulative, long-range asset, not a campaign that gets refreshed with each new trend. Winning companies "rarely change a winning formula" over decades (Marlboro's cowboy imagery; Crest's cavity-fighting claim, both cited as running long enough to reach a second generation of the same customer base). The prescribed discipline: a company should almost never change its *basic* positioning strategy — only its short-term *tactics*, which exist purely to execute that unchanging long-term strategy in fresh ways. Losing a mentally-owned position is compared to losing valuable real estate — once given up, it may be permanently unrecoverable (Levi's line-extending into casual wear, and subsequently finding its own core jeans position undercut by "designer label" competitors, is the specific cautionary case referenced back to Section 12–13).

**6. Do you match your position?** A direct challenge to the common complaint that positioning constrains creativity: **it does, and that's by design.** The described failure mode is a company completing rigorous strategic planning (charts, research, a clear position) and then handing the resulting strategy to "creatives" for execution, who apply their craft so heavily that the underlying strategy "disappears in a cloud of technique, never to be recognized again" — to the point that the text argues such an organization would be better off literally running the strategy flip-chart itself as the ad, rather than paying for creative execution that obscures it. "Avis is only No. 2 in rent-a-cars, so why go with us? We try harder" is cited again here specifically because it reads as *both* an ad and a bare strategy statement simultaneously — offered as the standard every execution should be measured against. The same "does your presentation match your position" test is explicitly extended to individuals (does your clothing signal banker, lawyer, or artist consistently with your stated position, or actively undermine it?).

**On the value and limits of outside help (agencies).** The genuinely useful thing an outside agency supplies is not creativity but **objectivity born of ignorance** — not knowing the inner workings of a company is precisely what lets an outsider see it the way the actual prospect does, from outside-in rather than inside-out. What an agency explicitly cannot supply is "magic" — the popular belief that creativity is some transferable substance an agency can apply like a coating to make any product sell is dismissed outright, with the flat statement: **"Today, creativity is dead. The name of the game on Madison Avenue is positioning."**

## 23. Playing the Positioning Game: Required Mental Attitudes

**You must understand that words themselves carry no fixed meaning.** As general semanticists argue, meanings live in the people using words, not in the words themselves — like a sugar bowl that's empty until someone fills it. A word that has "leaked" its intended meaning (the text's example: "Volkswagen" no longer holding the concept of a mid-sized luxury car) should simply be discarded in favor of a better container (Audi, which held that concept far more successfully and came to outsell BMW while closing on Mercedes-Benz, even as Volkswagen itself faltered in the U.S. market). Mental rigidity — insisting a product coming out of a Volkswagen factory must therefore be marketed as a Volkswagen — is named directly as "a barrier to successful positioning." This requires disdain for both dictionary and history-book meanings in favor of whatever meaning will actually trigger the desired response in the prospect's mind. Every product or service is, in this sense, "packaged goods" — if it isn't sold in a physical box, the name itself becomes the box.

**You must understand how words function as psychological triggers, not neutral labels.** Most people are, in the terminology borrowed from semanticist Alfred Korzybski, "unsane" — not fully rational, not irrational, but somewhere in between: rather than constantly updating their internal picture of reality to match new facts (the theoretically "sane" approach, which is exhausting), most people find it easier to bend the facts to fit their existing opinions, or simply defer to whichever "expert" opinion is nearest. This is precisely why a psychologically well-chosen name (Mustang, evoking sportiness and speed) works even though it has no literal engineering relationship to the product, and it operates within limits: a name that strays too far from observable reality gets rejected outright by the mind regardless of what the label says (an obviously small toothpaste tube printed with "large" doesn't fool anyone; "the People's Republic of China" was widely replaced with "Red China" precisely because too few people accepted the premise embedded in the official name).

**You must be careful about chasing change for its own sake.** Despite the accelerating pace of product life cycles (once measured in decades, increasingly measured in months at the time of writing) and a general cultural sense that "the world seems to be turning faster," the book argues the correct response to a faster-changing environment is *not* faster internal change, but a firmer long-range anchor. Companies that chased passing booms (Singer into home appliances, RCA into computers, General Foods into fast food, and the broader wave of firms that renamed themselves into meaningless initials chasing a naming fad) are contrasted unfavorably against companies that "kept at what they did best and held their ground" (Maytag's reliable appliances, Disney's fantasy-and-fun positioning, Avon's door-to-door model) and were rewarded for the discipline.

**You need vision — the ability to set a long-range direction and hold it through short-term turbulence.** Change is likened to ocean waves: agitating and confusing at the surface in the short term, but far less significant than the deeper, slower-moving currents underneath. Changing a large company's direction is compared to turning an aircraft carrier — it takes real distance before anything visible happens, and a wrong turn takes even longer to correct, which is why decisions have to be made with a 5–10 year horizon rather than a reactive, wave-by-wave posture. A further discipline embedded here: the ability to distinguish genuine strategic success from mere favorable economic tailwinds ("when the tide is rising, everything seems to be working; when the tide is falling, nothing seems to be working") — mistaking luck for skill is named as a specific risk ("today's hula-hoop marketing genius could be tomorrow's welfare recipient").

**You need courage — specifically, the willingness to spend aggressively while a position is still open and fluid.** Tracing leadership positions back through their histories (Hershey in chocolate, Hertz in rent-a-cars), the common thread identified is not superior marketing skill or product innovation but simply *seizing the initiative* — pouring in marketing investment while the category was still undecided. Hershey's own case is used as the negative example: convinced its position was strong enough not to need advertising, it waited too long to start, and by the time of writing was no longer even among the top five best-selling chocolate bars.

**You need objectivity, actively maintained through other people, not through self-discipline alone.** The instruction to "eliminate all ego from the decision-making process" is paired with an explicit structural mechanism rather than a purely personal virtue: "you can't play basketball without a backboard" — you need another person to bounce ideas off, precisely because the moment you believe you've found the simple, obviously-correct answer is the moment you've lost the objectivity needed to actually evaluate it. The book notes its own authorship (two people) as a direct, self-referential illustration of this principle, comparing the practice to table tennis — a game inherently requiring two participants to refine and perfect an idea through genuine back-and-forth.

**You need simplicity — and the obvious is harder to recognize than it sounds.** "This problem when solved will be simple" (a sign "Boss" Kettering reportedly kept on the wall of GM's Research Building) is offered as the underlying discipline. Effective lines cited as models of this simplicity in action: "Raisins from California. Nature's candy"; "Moist and meaty Gainesburgers. The canned dog food without the can"; "Bubble Yum. Number yum in bubble gum." A related structural discipline recounted: one agency head required account executives to write the marketing strategy on the *back* of every layout, so that when a client asked what an ad was supposed to accomplish, the strategy could literally be flipped to and read off. The book's explicit correction to this practice: a genuinely well-made ad shouldn't need that flip side at all, **because the ad itself should simply *be* the strategy** — "the agency made a mistake. It ran the wrong side of the layout."

**You need subtlety — finding an opening that is open *and* still commercially effective, not just open.** It's described as trivially easy to claim an extreme, unclaimed position (the far political right or far left, in the given example) — and equally certain that such a position, while genuinely ownable, will not produce broad commercial success. Genuine skill lies in finding an opening nearer the *center* of the spectrum (slightly conservative among liberals, or vice versa), which the text frames as requiring real restraint. The named failure mode for getting this balance wrong in the "safe but narrow" direction is dubbed **"Rolls-Royce thinking"** — a positioning success that is nonetheless a sales failure, illustrated by Rolls-Royce's roughly 1,000 U.S. unit sales a year (about 0.01% of the market) against Cadillac's 300,000+, despite both nominally competing as "luxury cars." The stated goal is to hold two things in balance simultaneously: a genuinely unique position, paired with an appeal that isn't so narrow it forecloses meaningful volume.

**You must be willing to sacrifice.** Positioning's essence, per the text, is giving something up in order to secure a specific, ownable position — Nyquil explicitly sacrificed the daytime cold-remedy market in order to own the nighttime one outright. This runs directly against the default marketing instinct to *broaden* reach (line extension, size/flavor proliferation, expanded distribution — all of which the book has already shown tend to produce short-term sales gains paired with long-term positioning erosion, per Sections 12–13). The resulting maxim: in positioning, smaller is often genuinely better — a smaller target owned outright beats a bigger market that has to be shared with three or four other brands, because you cannot be all things to all people and still hold a powerful, defensible position.

**You need patience, executed through deliberate, staged roll-outs rather than simultaneous national launches** (which few companies can genuinely afford in an overcommunicated market). Four named roll-out patterns: **geographic** (build a market, then expand to the next, moving systematically e.g. east to west); **demographic** (Marlboro became the top campus cigarette brand well before it became the top brand nationally, and only later rolled that position outward); **chronologic** (build a position with a specific age cohort and let the brand ride that cohort forward as it ages — Pepsi's "Pepsi Generation" being the named example); and **distribution-based** (Wella built credibility through beauty salons before expanding into drugstores and supermarkets).

**You need a global outlook, because positions established in one country can now be leveraged into others.** The book warns against a narrow domestic focus ("a company that keeps its eye on Tom, Dick, and Harry is going to miss Pierre, Hans, and Yoshio"), citing IBM's roughly 60% share of the German computer market and the fact that over half of IBM's total profits at the time came from outside the U.S. as evidence that positioning is already, in practice, a worldwide game. Operating internationally surfaces its own naming problems (U.S. Rubber's rename to Uniroyal is cited again here specifically as a move to create one workable global identity, rather than a name tied to one product and one country).

**What you explicitly don't need: a reputation for marketing genius.** This is named as a potential fatal flaw rather than an asset, because product leaders routinely (and wrongly) attribute their category success to transferable marketing skill, then try to apply that supposed skill elsewhere — with poor results (Xerox's computer record; IBM's own plain-paper copier line failing to dent Xerox's business, despite IBM's own vaunted marketing reputation). Bristol-Myers's sequential, expensive, and unsuccessful head-on attacks on established leaders (Fact against Crest, killed after $5 million; Resolve against Alka-Seltzer, killed after $11 million; Dissolve against Bayer) are used as a closing cautionary sequence, restating the book's first rule of positioning in its final, most compressed form: **to win the battle for the mind, you cannot compete head-on against a company holding a strong, established position — you can go around, under, or over, but never head-to-head.** The leader owns the high ground; only players who follow the actual rules of positioning move up the ladder, and, per the book's closing line, "only the better players are going to survive."
