# This Is Marketing — Framework Reference

*Pure framework reference distilled from the book. No commentary, no application notes — just the frameworks, models, and mental tools as the author presents them.*

*Source: This Is Marketing by Seth Godin*

---

## 1. The Marketer's Compass — From Mass to Trust

The book's organizing claim: marketing's "true north" has flipped. Every three hundred thousand years or so the Earth's magnetic poles switch places; in culture, this kind of flip happens far more often, and it just happened in marketing. Instead of selfish mass marketing, effective marketing now relies on empathy and service.

**Marketing is not a battle, a war, or a contest.** It is the generous act of helping someone solve a problem — their problem. It's a chance to change the culture for the better, and it involves very little shouting, hustling, or coercion.

**The internet inverted the medium.** Television was invented to hold TV ads; radio was invented to give radio ads a place to live. The internet wasn't built around interruption and mass — it feels like a vast, free playground where your ideas deserve to be seen by everyone, but in fact it's a billion tiny whispers, an endless series of selfish conversations that rarely include you or your work.

**The trap of ads.** For most of the author's lifetime, advertising simply *was* marketing: buy ads, ads work, ads pay for themselves. That stopped being true. "Which means you'll need to become a marketer instead" — seeing what others see, building tension, aligning with tribes, creating ideas that spread.

**"How do I get the word out?" is precisely the wrong question.** Getting discovered is the *last* part of the work, not the first. SEO experts, Facebook consultants, PR professionals, and ad agencies all answer a question that shouldn't be asked first.

**Shameless marketers brought shame to the rest of us.** A short-term, profit-maximizing hustler spams, tricks, and coerces — no other profession does this proudly (no civil engineers cold-call seniors to sell fake coins). This has driven ethical, generous marketers to hide their best work and feel shame about being market-driven. The alternative: marketing that seeks volunteers, not victims, and is focused on being missed when you're gone.

**The lock and the key.** It doesn't make sense to make a key and then run around looking for a lock to open. Find a lock (the customer you seek to serve), then fashion a key. It's easier to make products for the customers you seek to serve than to find customers for products you've already made.

**Case Study: Penguin Magic.** Grew large not by being the Amazon of magic tricks but by deeply understanding a narrow audience: every trick sold with a demonstration video (tension — you must buy to learn the secret); the site's product cycle is fast because professional magicians barely buy tricks (they only need ten or twenty) while amateurs constantly need new ones for the same repeat audience (friends and family); every trick is reviewed in detail by other magicians, not casual reviewers; the company invests in connection through email lists, live lectures ("the TED Talks of magic"), and conventions.

## 2. Marketing in Five Steps and What Marketers Know

**Marketing in five steps:**
1. Invent a thing worth making, with a story worth telling and a contribution worth talking about.
2. Design and build it so a few people will particularly benefit from and care about it.
3. Tell a story that matches the built-in narrative and dreams of that tiny group — the smallest viable market.
4. Spread the word.
5. Show up — regularly, consistently, and generously, for years — to organize, lead, and build confidence in the change you seek to make; to earn permission to follow up and enrollment to teach.

**Executive summary of the book, in the author's own words:**
- Ideas that spread, win.
- Marketers make change happen for the smallest viable market, by delivering anticipated, personal, and relevant messages people actually want.
- Marketers don't use consumers to solve their company's problem; they use marketing to solve other people's problems.
- Status — our self-perceived role in any interaction — sits at the heart of culture; affiliation and dominion decisions determine where we go and how.
- Persistent, consistent, frequent stories delivered to an aligned audience earn attention, trust, and action.
- "People like us do things like this" is how each of us understands culture.
- Ideas move through a slope: early adopters, then a chasm, then (sometimes) the masses.
- Attention is a precious, cluttered resource; smart marketers make their offering easy to position, resonate, and remember.
- Marketing begins (and often ends) with what you do and how you do it, not what comes after the thing ships.
- Culture beats strategy — so much that culture *is* strategy.

**Six things marketers know:**
1. Committed, creative people can change the world — they're the only ones who do.
2. You cannot change everyone; "Who's it for?" focuses your action.
3. Change is best made with intent — "What's it for?" is the posture of work that matters.
4. Human beings tell themselves stories that are, to them, completely and totally true; it's foolish to try to persuade them (or us) otherwise.
5. People can be grouped into stereotyped groups that tell themselves similar stories and make similar decisions based on perceived status and other needs.
6. What you say isn't nearly as important as what others say about you.

## 3. Stories, Connections, and Experience — Market-Driven vs. Marketing-Driven

**Case Study: VisionSpring.** Selling $3 reading glasses (manufactured for ~$2) to villagers in India who needed them, could see the demonstrated benefit instantly, and had the cash — yet only a third bought. The fix: instead of framing the sale as "here's an opportunity to shop," the team gave villagers the glasses first and said, "If they work and you like them, pay us three dollars. If not, give them back." Sales doubled. The lesson: **desire for gain versus avoidance of loss.** People in poverty may see shopping as a threat, not a pleasure, because being wrong can be costly in a way it isn't for the affluent. "My narrative about how to buy glasses isn't better or worse than the next villager's — it's simply my narrative."

**The quarter-inch drill bit, extended.** Theodore Levitt: "People don't want to buy a quarter-inch drill bit. They want a quarter-inch hole." The book pushes further: no one wants a hole either — they want the shelf, the feeling of an uncluttered room, the satisfaction of doing it themselves, the status of a spouse's admiration, the peace of mind of a safe, clean space. Ultimately: **"People don't want to buy a quarter-inch drill bit. They want to feel safe and respected."**

**People don't want what you make — they want what it will do for them, and how it will make them feel.** There aren't many feelings to choose from (belonging, connection, peace of mind, status, etc.); most marketers deliver the same feelings via different products, services, and stories, for different people in different moments. **"Who's it for" and "what's it for" are the two questions that guide all decisions.**

**Three tools, more powerful than any digital shortcut:**
- **We tell stories** — that resonate and hold up over time, made true by our actions.
- **We make connections** — humans are lonely and want to be seen, known, and part of something.
- **We create experiences** — using a product, engaging a service, donating, attending a rally, calling customer service; each is part of the story and builds connection.

**Market-driven vs. marketing-driven.** Every organization is driven by some primary force: chef-driven restaurants, tech-driven Silicon Valley, money-driven investment firms. Being **marketing-driven** — slick, focused on the offer, the surface shine, squeezing one more dollar — is a dead end. Being **market-driven** means listening to the market, hearing its hopes, dreams, and frustrations, and working to influence and bend it for the better. Market-driven lasts.

**The myth of rational choice.** Microeconomics assumes a "rational agent" who weighs information, probabilities, and costs/benefits consistently. This is false. The better bet: assume people act on current irrational urges, ignore counter-evidence, trade long-term for short-term benefit, and are influenced by the culture they identify with. Two mistakes to avoid: (1) assuming people are well-informed, rational, independent, long-term choice-makers; (2) assuming everyone is like you, knows what you know, wants what you want.

## 4. The Smallest Viable Market

**"What change are you trying to make?"** Every marketer is an agent of change, working to change other human beings — trivial or profound. Denying this is a form of hiding.

**Two common stumbles:**
- **Stumble 1:** Picking a grandiose, nearly impossible change (e.g., "change the face of music education nationally"). This is both a heavy burden and a convenient excuse for despair. Better to begin with a hurdle you can leap, then replicate the process on bigger challenges.
- **Stumble 2:** Reverse-engineering a "change" to match what you're already selling, loaded with meaningless buzzwords — contrasted with By the Way Bakery's real change: "We want to make sure no one is left out," turning hosts from exclusive to inclusive and guests from outsiders to insiders.

**Every promise takes the form: "If you do X, you will get Y."** Not a guarantee — more like "If this works for you, you're going to discover…" Promises are about status ("They laughed when I sat down at the piano…"), dominance ("Roll Tide!"), status and respect ("Choosy mothers choose Jif"), belonging ("I pledge allegiance…"), or affiliation and justice ("The Earth needs a good lawyer").

**"Who are you seeking to change?"** You cannot change everyone. You need to change *someone* — a group of someones you can identify by shared belief, geography, demographic, or (more usefully) psychographic. **"Who's it for?"** is a magic question that shifts the product you make, the story you tell, and where you tell it. Example: Starbucks and Dunkin' Donuts both sold coffee for decades without competing for each other's customer — Starbucks obsessed over a group with a precise set of beliefs about coffee, time, money, community, opportunity, and luxury.

**Worldviews and personas.** Use psychographics, not demographics: group people by the stories they tell themselves (a concept the book borrows from cognitive linguist George Lakoff's "worldviews" — the shortcut lens through which each of us sees the world, our assumptions, biases, and stereotypes). Case in point: Ron Johnson brought his own luxury-retail worldview to JCPenney, ending discounts and coupons, and sales dropped over 50 percent — he abandoned the bargain-hunting "sport" his actual customers played. A convenient shortcut here is inventing personas (e.g., "Bargain Bill," "Hurried Henry," "Careful Karla") — willfully exaggerating attitudes and beliefs to serve people better.

**Forcing a focus — the smallest viable market.** The relentless pursuit of mass leads to boring, average, inoffensive work. Begin instead with the **smallest viable market**: the minimum number of people you'd need to influence to make the effort worthwhile. Case in point: Danny Meyer's Union Square Cafe could only serve six hundred people a day, so the founder's job was to choose *which* six hundred — the magic was in curating the customers, not the real estate or a famous chef.

**Specific is a kind of bravery.** Specific means accountable — it worked or it didn't. Organize your project, life, and organization around the minimum viable audience, then find a corner of the market that can't wait for your attention and overwhelm their wants and dreams with your focus. This combines with the **lean-startup idea of the minimum viable product** — but the key word is *viable*: no fair shipping junk. Steve Blank's concept of **customer development** (gaining traction with customers, finding fit between what you make and what they want) is what separates successful projects from unsuccessful ones — more than fancy technology or expensive marketing.

**Shun the nonbelievers.** Marketing to the masses invites a deafening chorus of "no," tempting you to sand off your edges to fit in. Resist — it's not for them, it's for the smallest viable audience you set out to serve.

**"Winner take all" rarely is.** Example: in a district of 724,000 residents, only 20,000 voted in the last contested primary — meaning 5,000 votes (under 1 percent of residents) is the difference between winning and losing. Five thousand of the right people can be more than enough.

**A one-word transformation:** call your prospects and customers **"students"** instead — not the student-teacher relationship of testing and compliance, but the student-mentor relationship of enrollment, choice, and care.

**Coloring the ocean purple.** A teaspoon of bright thief-detector dye turns a swimming pool permanently purple but does nothing to the ocean. Sharing your best work only makes a difference if you find a "swimming pool" — a market small enough to visibly change — not the ocean of everyone.

**"It's not for you."** Saying this shows respect: it means not wasting someone's time, pandering, or insisting they change their beliefs. Every bestselling book on Amazon has one-star reviews — it's impossible to create work that both matters and pleases everyone. **The comedian's dilemma**: a great comic bombs in front of an audience that turns out to be a non-English-speaking tour group — it wasn't a bad set, it wasn't for them.

**The simple marketing promise (template):**
- My product is for people who believe _________.
- I will focus on people who want _________.
- I promise that engaging with what I make will help you get _________.

**Case Study: The Open Heart Project (Susan Piver).** Grew to 20,000+ members via eight repeatable steps: (1) start with empathy to see a real need; (2) focus on the smallest viable market ("How few people could find this indispensable and still make it worth doing?"); (3) match the worldview of the people served; (4) make it easy to spread; (5) earn and keep attention and trust; (6) offer ways to go deeper; (7) create and relieve tension at every step of the customer's journey; (8) show up often, with humility, focusing on what works.

## 5. Worldviews, Personas, and Empathy

**Empathy is at the heart of marketing.** People don't believe what you believe, don't know what you know, don't want what you want. **Sonder**: the moment you realize everyone around you has an internal life as rich and conflicted as your own — everyone has noise in their heads, feels wronged, is afraid, feels lucky, wants to connect and contribute, wants something they can't have, is lonely, insecure, and a bit of a fraud, and cares about something. The marketer's job isn't to insist others get with your program — it's more productive to "dance with them instead."

**The million-dollar bargain.** A nonprofit fundraiser projects her own frugality onto a donor and undersells the ask. Empathy reframes it: the donation isn't for her, it's for the donor, who tells himself "this million-dollar donation is a bargain — I'm going to get at least two million dollars' worth of joy, status, and satisfaction out of it." **Everything anyone purchases is a bargain — otherwise they wouldn't buy it.** Withholding a valuable option from someone because you lack empathy for their narrative is a form of stealing.

**"Better" is not transitive.** A ruler is longer than a thumb, a thumb longer than a peppercorn — this transitivity works for length but not for human value judgments. An Hermès bag costing more than a Louis Vuitton bag doesn't make it "better," only more expensive. **Better isn't up to you — it's "best for us,"** and different people's "us" values different things (sustainability and price vs. status and luxury, for the same product category). The marketer's job is to find "a spot on the map with edges that (some) people want to find" — a generous beacon, not a selfish unique selling proposition. **"We're this, not that."**

**The marketing of dog food.** Dog food is marketed as increasingly nutritious and delicious, yet no dog has ever bought dog food — the innovation is for the *owner's* feelings (satisfaction of caretaking, status of a luxury purchase, generosity of sharing), not the dog's palate. The lesson: there's almost always a disconnect between engineered performance and market appeal.

**Early adopters are not adapters.** Adapters figure out how to get along when the world changes, without enthusiasm. Early adopters (neophiliacs) are addicted to the new — they crave discovery, enjoy the tension of "this might not work," and love bragging about discoveries. They're very forgiving of early missteps and very unforgiving once the thrill of discovery wears off. You can't be perfect in their eyes; the best you can be is interesting. Marketers are torn between creating for easily-bored neophiliacs and building things that last for the broader market — "Who's it for?" resolves the tension.

**The conspiracy-theorist aside (Roland Imhoff's research).** People drawn to conspiracy theories are often searching for a feeling — uniqueness — not a logical truth; when told a theory was believed by 81 percent of a population versus 19 percent, subjects were far more enthusiastic about the *less popular* version. This is a milder version of the same identity-seeking drive behind early-adopter micro-tribes.

**Humility and curiosity.** A marketer is curious about others' struggles, dreams, and beliefs, and has the humility to accept people's scarce attention. People don't owe you attention just because you bought an ad — they might *trade* attention for something they need, want, or trust. This is the lock and the key again: find the people (the lock) and fashion a key just for them.

**Case Study: Be More Chill.** A poorly reviewed musical that flopped in its 2015 New Jersey debut became a hit soundtrack and cult phenomenon two years later — entirely through fan-made animation, streaming, and word of mouth, without a Broadway debut or good reviews, because it was built for a specific new generation, not for critics.

**"What's a car for?"** A teenager's first car isn't about transport; it changes the teen's status (dependent child to independent adult) and the parent's role (dominion to offering freedom and responsibility) — designers, marketers, and salespeople who see these underlying changes provide more value.

**Too many choices.** Old-fashioned industrial marketing used pressure and coercion because customers had no alternative (three TV channels, one store in town). Today's empowered consumer, faced with infinite alternatives (a million books published yearly, 500+ battery chargers on Amazon), often makes the obvious choice: walk away.

**Positioning as a service — the XY grid.** Draw a simple two-axis grid using attributes with two extremes on each axis (sustainability, obviousness, maintenance cost, safety, edginess, distribution, network effect, imminence, visibility, trendiness, privacy, professionalism, difficulty, elitism, danger, experimental, limited, incomplete, etc.). Plot every alternative on the grid to create a map a busy person can use to choose. Two kinds of claims work over time: **claims that are true**, doubled down on in all your actions, and **claims that are generous**, existing as a service to the customer. Example: one music teacher stakes out "rigor and competition wins" as his axes; another stakes out "whole-student experience, not competition" — they don't compete, they occupy different quadrants.

**Choose your own quadrant, not the popular one.** Picking popular axes crowds you into the "lumpenproletariat" of average brands; building your own quadrant — even with less obvious appeal — lets underserved customers find, connect with, and spread word about you.

**One last note on sonder:** we assume people on the "other side" of politics or taste don't really mean what they say. They do. Accepting that people have genuinely embraced who they've become makes it possible to dance with them — not transform them, not win an argument, but connect and add your story and beliefs to what they already see and hear.

## 6. Beyond Commodities

**Problem first.** Effective marketers begin with a group they seek to serve and a problem/change that group seeks, not with a clever solution. The opportunity is emotional change, not a tactical fix — not a quarter-inch hole, but a difference in how someone feels.

**Quality is required but no longer sufficient.** A century of institutional progress (FDA-type regulation, reliable delivery, safe cosmetics, dependable cars) means baseline quality is now assumed. **The commodity suckout**: if you make something others also make (found on Upwork, Amazon, Alibaba), raising your price to earn a decent return just sends customers elsewhere, because customers now know more than you do about your competitors.

**"You can choose anyone, and we're anyone" — the shoeshine problem.** A generic shoeshine stand competes purely on convenience and price, and shoeshines aren't a need anyway. The alternative is to identify the actual emotional story on offer (status, feeling cared for, being the kind of person who does this) and organize the whole experience around it.

**Bernadette Jiwa's ten things good stories do** (from *Story Driven* — used as a checklist for whether your business story is true and effective enough):
1. Connect us to our purpose and vision for our career or business.
2. Allow us to celebrate our strengths by remembering how we got from there to here.
3. Deepen our understanding of our unique value and what differentiates us in the marketplace.
4. Reinforce our core values.
5. Help us act in alignment and make value-based decisions.
6. Encourage us to respond to customers instead of react to the marketplace.
7. Attract customers who want to support businesses that reflect or represent their values.
8. Build brand loyalty and give customers a story to tell.
9. Attract the kind of like-minded employees we want.
10. Help us stay motivated and continue to do work we're proud of.

The alternative to a chosen story is a "rearview-mirror," scarcity-driven cycle of merely filling a hole in the market, always wary of competition. A chosen story is generative, based on possibility.

**Your story is a hook.** Once you claim a story and commit to changing people, you're on the hook to deliver — which is why it's tempting to make average stuff for average people (a low-risk "take it or leave it" alternative). Great marketing is the generous, audacious act of saying "I see a better alternative; come with me."

**Case Study: Stack Overflow.** Joel Spolsky and Jeff Atwood built a better alternative to Experts Exchange (which hid answers behind a paywall, creating "profit via frustration," while gaming Google's crawler with visible answers that were hidden from actual visitors). Stack Overflow made both questions and answers free, monetized through job ads, and — crucially — **treated different constituencies differently**: fast, ranked answers for time-pressed askers; a reputation/ranking system for answerers seeking community status; a fast, self-service tool for job-board posters.

**"Better is up to the users, not up to you."** Google was "better" than Yahoo! not through faster or more accurate search but through not making users feel stupid (two links on the homepage vs. Yahoo's 183). DuckDuckGo is "better" for people who value not being tracked and not being part of a big company. Better is contextual to who's judging.

**"And we serve coffee."** Trident Booksellers and Café survived Amazon's scale by offering something Amazon structurally can't: a physical "third place" to meet, connect, and dream. Books become "a souvenir of the personal connections we made today."

**Authenticity vs. emotional labor.** The "authentic, vulnerable hero" archetype is a dangerous myth for professionals. James Brown's collapse on stage was brilliant stagecraft, not an authentic outburst — it happened every night. A therapist listening patiently is doing his job, not necessarily feeling patient in the moment; a Starbucks barista wishing you a great day is presenting, not revealing. **Revealing is for family and close friends, not the marketplace** — protecting yourself professionally means you'll be needed tomorrow. Marketing acts are the generous actions of people who care; the best version of your work is brought to the person you serve, not indulged for yourself (a three-star chef doesn't cook herself a twelve-course meal).

**Emotional labor is the work of doing what we don't feel like doing** — showing up with a smile when wincing inside, choosing empathy over a make-believe muse. If your "authentic self" in the moment is a selfish jerk, leave him at home. Professionals exert emotional labor in service of imagining what someone else would want; that's the work that provides service.

**"Who's talking?"** A faceless corporate email in the second person signals hiding — it's slick but not real. A human being extending emotional labor to take responsibility ("Here, I made this") opens the door to connection. The goal isn't to personalize the work; it's to make it personal.

## 7. The Canvas of Dreams and Desires

**A job description is not a dream.** A U.S. government sewing-machine-operator job description is specific but describes tasks, not desire — money itself is meaningless except for what it buys. Likewise, your product or service is not "a widget" — when marketing change, you're offering a new emotional state, a step closer to a customer's dreams and desires, not a task or stuff. **We sell feelings, status, and connection.**

**Three common confusions about "what people want":**
1. People confuse *wants* and *needs*. True needs are air, water, health, shelter — nearly everything else is a want that privileged people relabel as a need.
2. People are aware of their wants but terrible at inventing new ways to address them — they prefer familiar solutions even when those solutions work poorly.
3. People mistakenly believe everyone wants the same thing — early adopters want new, laggards want unchanging, some want chocolate, others vanilla.

**The shared basket of dreams and desires** — a foundational, limited vocabulary marketers draw from (the book lists this explicitly): Adventure, Affection, Avoiding new things, Belonging, Community, Control, Creativity, Delight, Freedom of expression, Freedom of movement, Friendship, Good looks, Health, Learning new things, Luxury, Nostalgia, Obedience, Participation, Peace of mind, Physical activity, Power, Reassurance, Reliability, Respect, Revenge, Romance, Safety, Security, Sex, Strength, Sympathy, Tension. Marketing begins with assertions about which of these wants and desires drive the audience you seek to serve — not with your machines, inventory, or tactics.

**Nobody needs your product.** "People need a white leather wallet" is a category error: people don't need a wallet at all, and if they want a white leather one, it's for the feeling it produces, not its whiteness or its leather. Identify the feeling before you make the thing.

**"No one is happy to call a real estate broker."** More than 80 percent of people who hire a broker choose the first person to return their call — the interaction is laden with fear, anxiety, relief, and status concerns, not joy. Just as no one needs a drill bit, no one needs a broker (or a waiter, or a limo driver) — what they need is the feeling that comes from what the professional delivers.

**"Where's the angry bear?"** When someone doesn't act as expected, look for their fear — it's difficult to dream of anything while feeling threatened, even imaginarily.

**"What do you want?"** The specifics people insist on (a certain car color, a package width) rarely matter as much as the underlying emotional shift they seek (from fear to belonging, etc.) — which leaves marketers a huge amount of room to design within.

**Always be testing.** Boring products for everyone are beyond criticism but attract no one, because the market of people happy with boring is static and not looking for you. Two shifts make constant testing viable now: (1) it's cheaper and faster than ever to create a prototype or limited run; (2) it's cheaper and faster than ever to find early adopters willing to engage. "Always be wrong" is the honest corollary — most tests fail, and that's fine.

**Scrapbooking.** Radical originality from scratch is exhausting and has low ROI. Instead, identify the elements (typefaces, pricing structures, images, interfaces) that your audience already trusts, break them into indivisible pieces, and rebuild something new on top of them.

**If you had to charge ten times as much** — "more of the same" is the wrong answer. Charging ten times more requires a different extreme, a different story, a different kind of scarcity, not more volume of the same feature.

**Irresistible is rarely easy or rational.** Fiona's ice cream stand at the Opinicon resort could rationally charge far more (an MBA analysis might peg the "correct" price near $8), but the cones function as a symbol and a beacon for engagement, not a profit center in themselves. **Stew Leonard's**, once profiled for the highest sales per square foot in its category by Tom Peters, thrived on magic and experience; a later generation of owners optimized for short-term profit, and the magic (and eventually the traffic) drained away. The heart of a thriving enterprise is the irrational pursuit of becoming irresistible.

## 8. The Virtuous Cycle, Network Effects, and a Thousand True Fans

**The virtuous cycle.** Every very good customer gets you another one; dead-end, silent, or secretive customers aren't worth chasing — "you can't grow your work on a cul-de-sac." Your best customers become your new salespeople.

**Design for remarkability, illustrated by the fax machine and Ethernet.** The fax machine spread because it works better when your colleagues have one too. Bob Metcalfe's original 3Com Ethernet offering (three PCs sharing a printer) offered only marginal benefit; once users could share data, being off the network became painful, and the network effect took over.

**Questions that reveal real traction (not vanity metrics):** How many people outside HQ use it every day? How often do they send suggestions to improve it? How many are insisting their friends and colleagues use it *right now*? Who would miss it if it were gone? **"If you can't succeed in the small, why do you believe you will succeed in the large?"**

**A Thousand True Fans (Kevin Kelly, 2008).** A true fan "will buy anything you produce" — will drive two hundred miles to see you, buy every format of your work, purchase sight unseen. Roughly a thousand such fans can sustain a decent (not lavish) living for an independent creator. The challenge for most people isn't the mass market, it's the micro market — winning fifty or a hundred people who would miss you if you were gone, before worrying about anyone else.

**Even *Hamilton* proves the smallest-viable-market rule**: for over a year it was seen by only a few hundred people a night, and even at full Broadway capacity it reaches under 1 percent of the U.S. population. Hits today are "meaningful for a few and invisible to the rest."

**Case Study: The Grateful Dead — a template for the smallest viable market.** Key elements: appealed to a relatively tiny audience and focused all energy there; skipped radio in favor of fans sharing the word hand to hand (encouraged taping shows); relied on a small number of true fans who supported them *a lot* rather than many fans supporting a little; picked and owned both extremes on the XY axis (live improv jams vs. polished radio hits); gave fans plenty to talk about (insiders vs. outsiders). Required: extraordinary talent (146 concerts a year), significant patience (only ~5,000 people at a typical 1972 show; it took over a decade to become an "overnight" success), and the guts to be quirky. Contrast: Scott Borchetta/Taylor Swift represent the "hit machine" path — valid, but "someone is going to make hits, and it's probably not going to be you." For everyone else, there's the path of connection, empathy, and change.

**All critics are right (all critics are wrong).** A critic who dislikes your work is correct about disliking it; a critic who claims *no one else* will like it is wrong, because you like it, so someone else might too. Every bestselling book has both five-star and one-star reviews (12 percent of *Harry Potter*'s reviews were one or two stars) — there's always a desired audience and an "accidental audience" that gets satisfaction from disliking and sharing that dislike; neither reaction is very useful. **Seek advice, not feedback:** "I made something I thought you'd like — how'd I do? What advice do you have for making it fit your worldview more closely?" This surfaces useful direction instead of raw, often-irrelevant emotional reaction.

**"Why don't people choose you?" — and why are they right?** People who don't choose you are usually making a perfectly rational decision given what they believe, want, and know. Fill in: "For people who want what you want (___) and believe what you believe (___), your choice of ___ is exactly correct." Finding the empathy to say "I'm sorry, this isn't for you, here's my competitor's number" is what frees you to do work that matters.

## 9. People Like Us Do Things Like This

**The core mechanism of behavior change.** Everyone always acts in accordance with their internal narratives — you can't make someone do what they don't want to do. Most behavior is driven by two forces: the desire to fit in ("people like us do things like this") and the perception of status (affiliation and dominance). Since both forces usually push people to stay as they are, **tension** is required to change them.

**"People like us (do things like this)."** Whether crickets or beef makes you squirm or hungry isn't genetic or rational — it's cultural conditioning. From childhood to death, most action is driven by the question "Do people like me do things like this?" Even adopting outlier behavior is still alignment with the behavior of outliers — no one is fully unaware of the crowd. You can't change *the* culture, but you can change *a* culture — your pocket of the world. The smallest viable market maximizes your chance of doing exactly that, because the enriched, connected core organically shares the word outward.

**Case Study: The Blue Ribbons.** After a school budget vote failed once and faced draconian mandated cuts on a second failure, activists skipped rallies and flyers and instead tied blue ribbons on a tree in town. Within days the idea spread to dozens more trees. The message — "people like us, in this Blue Ribbon district, support our schools" — passed the budget two to one.

**Defining "us."** In the mass-media era, "us" tried (imperfectly) to mean "all of us" — but today popular culture reaches far smaller slices (*Mad Men*, heavily covered by the *New York Times*, was watched by only 1 percent of the U.S. population). The long tail of culture doesn't need everyone; it's happy with *enough*. The tighter and more specific the "us," the better. The right approach is not "the people who give are people like us" (backwards) but proactively defining and then changing what a chosen "us" believes and shows each other.

**"It shouldn't be called 'the culture.'"** There is no universal culture — only "a culture" or "this culture." The two-step discipline: (1) map and understand the worldview of the specific culture you seek to change; (2) focus all energy there and ignore everyone else.

**Just enough art.** Cultural innovation is hierarchical across time: JetBlue only had to be hipper than American and Delta; six years later Virgin America had to be hipper than JetBlue — the bar keeps rising, and you can't leap the whole sequence at once (leap too far and the tribe won't follow).

**Case Study: Gay marriage referendum in Ireland.** Rather than argue fairness and civil rights abstractly, an elderly couple (Brighid and Paddy White) made a video about supporting their gay son — enabling viewers to see themselves ("people like us") as parents and traditionalists. Political change is cultural change, and culture changes horizontally, person to person.

## 10. Elite vs. Exclusive, and Roots and Shoots

**Elite vs. exclusive (Malcolm Gladwell's distinction).** *Elite* is an external measure — does the wider world respect this badge? (The Rhodes Scholarship is elite but not exclusive — it isn't a tribe with its own culture.) *Exclusive* is an internal measure — us vs. them, insiders vs. outsiders (the Hell's Angels are exclusive but not elite). Harvard Business School and the Navy SEALs are both. **It's exclusive institutions that change things** — elite status can be revoked overnight by outsiders, but exclusive organizations thrive as long as members wish to belong, which is something you can actually build. At the heart of an exclusive organization: every member is "people like us" — sign up and gain status, walk away and lose it. To change a culture, begin with an exclusive cohort, where you can create the most tension and the most useful connections.

**Case Study: Robin Hood Foundation gala.** Raised $101 million in one night (2015) — not because the gala format is magic, but because of "the extraordinary peer pressure of people like us do things like this," built over a generation among Wall Street donors, trading cash for status in a hyper-competitive environment. The intentional nature of this normalization is easily overlooked; it rarely happens by accident.

**The standing ovation test.** At TED, three respected people standing is enough to trigger a wave. At a Broadway show of strangers, ~15 scattered people can start one, because "tourists like me respond in ways like this" (venue bias). Among hardcore jazz fans at a club, it doesn't happen, because the venue's own bias (jazz fans don't give standing ovations) is hard to override. The mechanism: our desire to fit in interacts differently depending on whether we trust and recognize the people around us.

**Roots and shoots.** Your work is a tree: roots live in the soil of the dreams and desires of those you specifically seek to serve, not everyone. A commodity has shallow roots and gets crowded out by similar trees. As the tree grows, it becomes a beacon that early adopters climb, use for shade, and eventually eat from — and as it grows taller (blocking less sun for itself because there are few competing trees nearby), it attracts more people while blocking out competitors, because "the market likes a winner." It's a mistake to show up with an acorn and expect a crowd.

## 11. Trust and Tension Create Forward Motion

**Pattern match vs. pattern interrupt.** A **pattern match** fits an offering into an existing routine (a new cereal brand added to a family's usual rotation) — an easy "sure, why not" choice. A **pattern interrupt** requires a jolt of tension, because it asks someone to break an established, low-risk pattern; most people, most of the time, will say no, because patterns are established, time is precious, and risk is feared. Life transitions (new parents, newly engaged people, people who just moved) lack an established pattern, so marketing to them is "all interrupt" rather than requiring pattern-breaking persuasion.

**Case Study: Slack's two-stage growth.** Stage one was a **pattern match** — new software for people who like new software (neophiliacs). Stage two was engineered tension: Slack is better when your coworkers use it, so existing users have a selfish incentive to recruit others, and every day a colleague isn't on Slack is painful for them ("You're missing out"). The pattern-interrupt tool was built directly into the software.

**Tension is the force on a stretched rubber band** — pull one end and it creates tension everywhere. Social tension (a teacher calling on a student) can overcome inertia that voluntary action (raising a hand) could not.

**What are you breaking?** Launching anything new breaks something else's status: a second hotel ends the first hotel's uniqueness; the telephone ended the telegraph's speed advantage; an exclusive party creates outsiders; a new network gaining traction causes people in the old network to reconsider their allegiance — this reconsideration *is* what tension feels like.

**Tension is not the same as fear.** If you feel like you're coercing or scaring people, you're likely doing it wrong. Fear is a dream killer — it paralyzes. Tension, by contrast, is something marketers can create precisely *because* they care: it's the promise that we can get through a threshold to the other side (the tension of not-yet-knowing that characterizes all great education, jazz concerts, or thrilling movies). Fear paralyzes without a visible way out; tension, once a way out is seen, becomes the tool that moves people forward.

**Marketers create tension; forward motion relieves it.** Examples given: the going-out-of-business sale (tension of "what bargains did I miss?"); getting in early on a new social app (tension of falling behind peers); scarcity marketing (limited sneaker runs, "the last three houses on our block sold for less than expected"). None of these feelings existed before a marketer manufactured the gap — and the underlying driver of nearly all of them is **status**: where do we stand, what does the tribe think, who's up and who's down.

**How the status quo got that way.** Dominant narratives and market leaders persist because "they're good at resisting efforts by insurgents like you" — not because they're objectively correct. The status quo doesn't shift because you're right; it shifts because the culture changes, and the engine of culture is status.

## 12. Status, Dominance, and Affiliation

**Status roles are everywhere more than one human (or animal) is present** — from lion packs and Galápagos penguins to dating (who picks up the check) and boardrooms (who speaks, who decides). Keith Johnstone's book *Impro* is credited as the source for seeing "status roles" as the hidden driver of culture.

**Case example: the opening scene of *The Godfather*.** Low-status undertaker Bonasera visits high-status Don Corleone on his daughter's wedding day (a tradition requires the Godfather to grant any favor asked). The scene is a controlled disruption and restoration of the status hierarchy — it ends with Bonasera kissing the Don's ring, relieving the tension by returning to his place in the hierarchy.

**What status does.** Status is our position in a hierarchy and our perception of that position. It protects us, helps us get what we want, gives leverage to make change, can be a hiding place, a gift, or a burden — and the desire to change or protect it drives almost everything we do.

**Case Study: Lions and Maasai warriors (Leela Hazzah / Lion Guardians).** Killing a lion was traditionally a Maasai adolescent rite of passage, driving lion populations from ~200,000 to an estimated 30,000. Rational conservation arguments don't override deeply held cultural status needs. The fix worked *with* the culture: the actual underlying goals (community bonding, empowerment, bravery, patience, a status-raising rite of passage) were never really about killing per se — so the program built a new rite of passage around *saving* (finding, naming, tracking) lions instead, and status shifted to attach to protection.

**Six things about status:**
1. **Status is always relative** — there's no highest number, only position relative to others in the group.
2. **Status is in the eyes of the beholder** — you can be low status externally and high status in your own narrative simultaneously, and both are "true."
3. **Status attended to is the status that matters** — it's only relevant to people who care about it in that interaction.
4. **Status has inertia** — people are more likely to work to maintain their status (high or low) than to change it.
5. **Status is learned** — beliefs about status start early, though a new cohort can shift perceived status quickly.
6. **Shame is the status killer** — shame works as a lever precisely because accepting it undermines someone's entire relative-status narrative.

**Status is not the same as wealth.** A Pulitzer-winning columnist, a hospital chief, or a penniless yogi can all outrank a wealthy person in status, depending on the community doing the judging. Modern culture has gotten "lazier," conflating status with money or follower counts, but status is genuinely multi-form.

**Frank Sinatra had more than a cold (Gay Talese).** Sinatra lived two conflicting lives: an outwardly suave, high-status power broker, and an inwardly low-status, disrespected "skinny kid" — illustrating that you can't assume whether someone believes their apparent status or is desperately trying to escape/prove it. Every big decision is made based on perceptions of status, whether or not the marketer can read them accurately.

**Two XY grids for reading status (as described in text):**
- **High/low perceived status × high/low self-status.** Quadrant (a) high perceived + high self-status is rare (example given: Oprah Winfrey) — the person who chooses rather than waits to be chosen. Quadrant (d), high perceived + low self-status, is more common and produces "divas" and impostor syndrome (Sinatra again). Quadrant (b), low perceived + high self-status, includes people determined to tear down everyone around them (O. J. Simpson, Martin Shkreli cited). A fourth quadrant covers the hard-charging, selfish person who wants to win every engagement through a mix of value creation and tearing down competitors.
- **Seeking to stay high/move up vs. stay low/move down, crossed with enabling self/demeaning others vs. enabling others** — used to characterize different status-management strategies.

**Affiliation vs. dominion — two different ways to measure status.** Tom Hanks (searched as "nicest guy in Hollywood") represents affiliation; the fictional Don Corleone represents dominion.

*Affiliation questions:* Who knows you? Who trusts you? Have you made things better? What is your circle like? Where do you stand within the tribe? Can't we all get along?

*Dominion statements:* This is mine, not yours. Who has more power? I did this myself. My family needs more of what we already have. My side dominating your side means I don't have to be in charge, as long as my leader is winning.

A twelve-year-old who cares only about winning the ball game (dominion) may care intensely about who sits next to him on the bus (affiliation) and not at all about class rank — the same person measures different things in different contexts.

**Learning from pro wrestling:** it's a battle for status not just among wrestlers but among fans — when your hero moves up, so do you.

**The alternative to dominion is affiliation.** You can gain status by letting someone merge into traffic just as much as by cutting them off. This is community-based status: respect earned through contribution and care, especially toward people with no ability to repay you. Modern, urban, internet, arts, and innovation-driven society runs primarily on affiliation, not dominion — "I'm connected, I'm family" rather than "I'm better."

**Fashion is usually about affiliation** — the question isn't "who wins" but "is everyone in sync," with the leader providing a signal others can follow.

**Sending dominance signals:** Uber's early rollout (contentious relationships with regulators, competitors, drivers) built a brand around winning/losing that resonated with some investors, employees, and users.

**Sending affiliation signals:** marketers spend enormous time/money signaling how busy a trade-show booth is, who's at the launch party, who blurbed the book — shorthand for "are people like us doing something like this?" Affiliation, unlike dominion, welcomes abundance because it benefits from the network effect. Tactics: tombstone ads listing "the right firms," referral generation for B2B sellers, a craftsperson building a single-neighborhood reputation. **Dominion is vertical (above/below); affiliation is horizontal (who's standing next to me).**

Whether your specific audience measures dominance or affiliation is **up to the customer, not you** — the marketer's own worldview matters less than the worldview of those being served.

## 13. A Better Business Plan

**A five-section alternative business-plan structure**, proposed to replace the typical obfuscating document:

- **Truth** — describes the world as it is: the market, existing needs, competitors, technology standards, and how others have succeeded/failed historically. Non-partisan; it just states how things are, with as much specificity and ground knowledge as possible.
- **Assertions** — how you will change things: "We will do X, and then Y will happen." This is where tension gets created via storytelling for a specific market, with an expected response to your arrival.
- **Alternatives** — since the assertions section will inevitably be somewhat wrong (budgets and deadlines get missed), this section states what you'll do when that happens, and how much flexibility the product or team has.
- **People** — who's on your team (and who will join) — not resumes, but attitudes, abilities, and shipping track record. Extends to who you're serving, who the champions are, and what they believe about status and worldview.
- **Money** — how much you need, how you'll spend it, cash flow, P&L, balance sheets, margins, exit strategy.

**On mission statements.** A glib, reverse-engineered mission statement ("I'd like to sell more of what I've already decided to sell") isn't useful. Most marketers actually share the same underlying "why" (be successful, engage mutually beneficially, be respected, profit enough to do it again) — that's not distinctive. A better business plan takes that universal need and makes it *specific*: who and what it's for, the tension you'll create, the status roles you're engaging, and the story that will make change happen. If a specific approach fails, it doesn't invalidate your purpose — it just rules out one route, freeing you to find a new one.

## 14. Semiotics, Symbols, and Vernacular

**We communicate with symbols, not just meanings.** Letters like "C-A-R" are a stand-in, not a picture. Nike spent billions teaching people the swoosh means human possibility, achievement, status, and performance; comic sans, to a designer, signals bad taste and low status.

**"What does this remind you of?"** Busy people scan instead of study, constantly asking what a signal reminds them of — your logo, your stories, your room setup for an offsite, the flashing lights at a concert, the feel of a newspaper vs. a tablet, a medical office's décor. This is semiotics: flags, symbols, shortcuts, and shorthand, all workable with intent. A design that "reminds me of a scam" (a GeoCities-style flashing site) takes a long time to undo.

**Hiring a professional** means designing something that reminds *other people* of the right story, not just something you personally like. There's no single "professional look" — a summer blockbuster and a teen-makeup YouTube video each give themselves away in seconds through different vernaculars.

**Compounding signal value: Don LaFontaine**, who recorded more than five thousand movie/TV voice-overs — not because of raw talent advantage but because his voice, once established, reminded audiences of prior big-budget work, compounding over time. Semiotics doesn't care who made the symbol — the meaning lives in the mind of the person looking at it, and there's no one right answer across audiences (a hoodie signals different things in Silicon Valley than in East London).

**Why Nigerian scam emails are sloppy on purpose.** The misspellings function as a filter, signaling "go away" to the skeptical and well-informed while still reaching the greedy and gullible — sending the signal is more efficient than wasting the scammer's time on people who won't fall for it.

**The flags on SUVs are called flares.** Purely decorative wheel-arch flares on expensive cars are pure status signifiers with no function — as are holes in the back of driving gloves (a vestige of an era when a watch needed to poke through). Once a status symbol becomes recognizable (like details on an Hermès bag) it gets copied and diluted until it just signals changing taste rather than genuine scarcity.

**A flag is not for everyone** — the smallest viable market gives you freedom to pick a symbol most resonant to that specific audience, even if it wouldn't work for a broader one. There's a paradox: making change requires going first with an innovation, but innovation often reminds (some) people of a past failure — so you begin with an audience willing to tolerate that.

**The same and the different — vernacular fit.** Car ads mostly look alike because sameness signals safety for a huge purchase; fashion ads in *Vogue* look nothing like ads in *Field & Stream* because the vernacular (typefaces, photo styles, copy) has to match what the specific "us" expects.

**Case Study: Apple's "1984" Super Bowl ad (Lee Clow).** Most viewers didn't understand the Orwell reference, but Apple didn't need them to — it needed roughly a million media-savvy and nerd viewers to get it, talk about it, and go first. It took thirty years for the idea to spread to everyone, building hundreds of billions in market cap through the deliberate use of semiotics, not technology.

**Case Study: "Where's Keith?" (Witchsy.com).** Founders Penelope Gazin and Kate Dwyer invented a fictional male co-founder, "Keith," whose emails got answered far more readily and helpfully than the women's own — exposing gender bias baked into how vendors judged senders. Symbols can work for or against you; ignoring biased cultural judgments doesn't make them go away.

**Flags are chosen with intent — not flying one is itself a choice.** The lazy move is insisting you don't need a flag or badge because your features speak for themselves; something else always matters.

**Are brands for cattle? A brand is not a logo — it's shorthand for the customer's expectation of a promise.** If people care whether they get you or a substitute, you have a brand; a true commodity (wheat, coffee by the pound, bandwidth) has no brand because switching providers wouldn't matter to the buyer (this is offered as the test for why Verizon and AT&T brands are functionally weak). Building a brand requires investing in connection and non-transferable properties. **A logo without a brand is meaningless** — it's the Post-it reminder of the promise a brand already makes, not a substitute for having one.

## 15. Treat Different People Differently

**The standard-deviation model of an audience.** In any group of 100, roughly 68 cluster near average on any measured trait, 27 sit further out, and 4 are extreme outliers (Everett Rogers's diffusion research). Most people like what they already have and aren't seeking novelty; the small minority of **neophiliacs** on the innovation curve's edge want the better, the clever, the new — waiting in line for openings, upgrading immediately, reading *Vogue* for the ads.

**Enrollment, not interruption.** The alternative to buying attention is voluntary education: asking people to eagerly lend attention in exchange for insight or forward motion. Enrollment is mutual and consensual (hands raised, eyes on the board) and is the prerequisite for teaching and learning together. Lazy marketers try to *buy* enrollment with flashy ads; the best marketers *earn* it by finding people who already want the change on offer and connecting them to others who want it too.

**Different segments want fundamentally different things**, and asking "what do people want" as a single question is the wrong frame:
- Neophiliacs want to go first, want hope/possibility/magic, the thrill (and risk) of newness, and the pleasure of showing off their discovery and being rewarded for productivity.
- The typical corporate risk-averse employee wants to avoid trouble with the boss, and if trouble happens, wants an airtight alibi.
- The social crusader wants a glimmer of hope and a chance to make things right.
- The dominance-measuring person wants to win — or failing that, to watch the opponent lose.
- The affiliation-seeking tribe member wants to fit in and be in sync, without the risk of having to lead.
- Some want responsibility, others want recognition; some want a bargain, a few want to overpay to prove they can; almost no one wants to feel stupid; many now prize convenience (not having to exercise judgment) while others feel empty without contributing effort.

**The superuser.** Restaurants that spot the local critic and elevate that one table's experience illustrate a real principle — but today "everyone" technically has Yelp/social-media power, which doesn't mean you should (or even could) treat literally everyone better; the math doesn't hold. The real opportunity is to notice who is *already* acting like a neophiliac, sneezer, or power user, and deliberately "adopt them back" — giving talkers something to talk about and would-be leaders the resources to lead.

**"What's the purpose of this interaction?"** A customer service call from a high-value, long-tenure customer (who, hypothetically, generates eight times average revenue) is a different situation from a call from an anonymous, low-margin caller — yet frontline staff (often the least-paid, least-respected people in the org) usually can't distinguish them. Scripted, liability-minimizing responses ("as stated," "our policy") are one option; the alternative — going out of your way, having a CEO personally call, using FedEx's last pickup — is easily justified by the lifetime value of an extraordinary customer, even though it can't scale to every caller.

## 16. Goals, Strategy, Tactics, and the Two Kinds of Advertising

**Goals, strategy, and tactics — a hierarchy.** Tactics are the countless discrete, replaceable steps you take (list-able, easy to imitate if leaked). Strategy is the durable, harder-to-copy umbrella the tactics support (safe to share, because competitors "don't have the guts or persistence to turn your strategy into their strategy"). Your goal is the change you're betting the strategy will produce. Examples: Coca-Cola's goal (more people drinking more Coke) was served by a strategy of mass advertising to normalize Coke as part of a happy culture, executed via ever-changing ad *tactics*. Patagonia's goal (get a small group of outdoor enthusiasts to care about the environment) is served by a strategy of redefining environmental and clothing-quality perception among that group and giving them tools to evangelize, executed via tactics (recycling programs, store locations, material choices) that can change without abandoning the underlying 30-year strategy.

**Advertising is an optional engine for growth, not a requirement.** Media, telecom, and delivery companies profit by selling the attention of the people they ostensibly serve. Buying an ad means you don't have to earn attention — you're the customer of the intermediary, not an outsider anymore. Good news: a working ad approach scales precisely; bad news: it's hard to find one that works. **An ad, unnoticed, doesn't exist.** A noticed ad creates tension (of not knowing, of being left behind, of things possibly getting better or worse) only for the subset of people it's actually noticed by.

**Three elements of the magic of online advertising:** (1) more precise targeting (psychographic, not just demographic) than any prior medium; (2) instant reach (an ad can run within a minute of deciding to run it); (3) full measurability. Yet online advertising is also the most-ignored advertising ever created — it's unearned, bought media, and the audience knows it, is inundated, and is suspicious.

**Brand marketing vs. direct marketing (Lester Wunderman's distinction).** **Direct marketing** is action-oriented and measured — if you run an ad and count clicks/conversions, that's direct marketing. **Brand marketing** is culturally oriented and can't be measured — a billboard hoping to be remembered when someone eventually needs a funeral parlor is brand marketing. Each can produce side effects of the other type, but confusing the two is dangerous: Google's and Facebook's revenue explosion rests entirely on ads that pay for themselves in direct-response terms (a hundred dollars of ad spend generating $125 profit, so the advertiser buys more until the math stops working) — while legacy brand advertisers (Ford, Absolut, Palmolive) built entire brands on unmeasured brand marketing and now struggle when forced into a measured, direct-response paradigm.

**Rule for direct marketing: measure everything, or don't run the ads.** Compute cost per attention, per click, per order.

**Rule for brand marketing: be patient, refuse to measure, engage with the culture, and be consistent.** If you can't be consistent and patient, don't pay for brand marketing ads.

**A simple guide to online direct marketing** — the whole chain reduces to: the ad exists to get a click; the click exists to make a sale or earn permission; the sale exists to lead to another sale or word of mouth; permission exists to lead to education and a sale. Assign a value to every step; if you can't, don't run direct-response ads yet.

**A simple guide to brand marketing.** Everything is a brand signal (how you answer the phone, packaging, hold music, executive behavior, even the packing peanuts you use) whether or not it's measured. You definitely don't have enough time and money to build a brand for everyone — so be very specific, and then overdo the brand marketing for that specific audience so every interaction lets people make a smart guess about the whole.

**Frequency and the trust of repetition.** People remember what they rehearse, not what they merely read, hear, or see once — the mechanism behind trusting the familiar (we remember Uncle Fred, who came to Christmas twenty years running, not his one-time date). Jay Levinson's maxim: "Don't change your ads when you're tired of them. Don't change them when your employees are tired of them. Don't even change them when your friends are tired of them. Change them when your accountant is tired of them." There is a real dip — a gap between when the marketer gets bored and when the market actually gets the message — and quitting projects, talks, or businesses right before crossing that gap forfeits the trust that frequency would otherwise have built.

**SEO's math problem.** The premise that ranking for generic terms ("tire store," "restaurant") will get millions of businesses found doesn't scale — there are a thousand pages of results, and almost none of them can be first. The real path is building something specific enough that people search *for you by name*, not for a generic category — "step one is to make a product or service that people care enough to search for specifically... step two is to be the one they want to find."

## 17. Price Is a Story

**Pricing is a marketing tool, not just a way to collect money.** Two linked truths: marketing changes your pricing, and pricing changes your marketing — because people form assumptions based on price, and price shapes what people believe about your offering. Your price should align with the extremes of your positioning. Notably, "cheapest wine on the menu" vs. "most expensive wine" questions say nothing about the wine's actual taste or value — pure story.

**The baker's margin example.** Bread costing $1.95 to make: at $2.00/loaf the profit is $0.05; at $2.50 it's $0.55 (an eleven-fold increase); at $3.00 it's over $1.00 (twenty times the first margin). Selling at the low price requires ~21 times the volume of the premium price to match profit — "the difference between a few customers an hour or a line out the door." A higher price also funds a better experience (staff, signage, sponsorships, packaging, samples) that reinforces the story customers tell about the purchase. **"Better to apologize for the price once than to have to excuse a hundred small slights again and again."**

**Different prices for different people.** The price tag was invented by Quakers who considered haggling immoral; it was adopted at scale by Macy's and Wanamaker's for operational efficiency, not moral reasons. The internet reintroduces differential pricing, but the hard part for most organizations isn't the mechanics of variable pricing — it's the storytelling around it. Tesla told luxury buyers a fixed-price story that reassured them; Uber's demand-based surge pricing cost the brand billions in trust by breaking that story.

**"Cheap" is another way to say "scared."** Racing to be the cheapest usually means you haven't invested enough in generating real change — you're promising "the same, but cheaper," which requires no new calculation or emotional engagement from the buyer. **"Low price is the last refuge of a marketer who has run out of generous ideas."**

**Why not make everything free? Two reasons:**
1. A transaction is fundamentally different from encountering something freely shared — the scarcity, tension, and enrollment involved in choosing to *pay* disappear when a purchase is truly free.
2. Without cash flow, you can't invest in the product, team, or marketing.

**But free scales differently than "cheap."** Free is a different category of transaction entirely (it "scales to infinity," unlike a merely-low price) and spreads faster than anything tethered to money — hypothetically, a $3/month Facebook would have attracted far fewer than a million users.

**The resolution: pair two offerings.**
1. Free ideas that spread (recipes, podcasts, seminars).
2. Expensive expressions of those same ideas that are worth paying for (the plated pasta at the restaurant, the $84 concert ticket for a song that's free on the radio). **"The china and the ticket are souvenirs of ideas, and souvenirs are supposed to be expensive."**

**Trust and risk — the counterintuitive relationship.** It seems rational that people demand more trust before large, risky transactions — but often the opposite happens: high cost or risk itself produces cognitive dissonance that manufactures a feeling of trust ("I'm a smart person, so the smart thing to do would be to trust before spending this much, therefore I must trust this"). This is why boot camps (high cost of blood/sweat/tears) build strong group alignment, why Outward Bound changes people, and why expensive hotels/restaurants can survive bad reviews. **"Lowering your price doesn't make you more trusted. It does the opposite."**

**Case Study: No tipping at Union Square Hospitality Group (2016).** USHG eliminated tipping and raised menu prices ~20 percent, redirecting the revenue to parental leave, fair wages, and treating staff as professionals (kitchen staff paid better; front-of-house incentivized to collaborate rather than compete for tips). The change surfaced explicitly non-universal appeal: diners who enjoy the status of a flourished large tip lost that thrill ("it's not for you, sorry"), while diners who prioritize fairness and dignity found it easier to become regulars — a values-aligned story to tell themselves and others (a form of virtue-signaling status). The relevant marketing questions: who's it for, what's it for, how does it change status, and what will I tell the others?

## 18. Permission Marketing, the Purple Cow, and Designing for Evangelism

**Permission is anticipated, personal, and relevant.** Defined originally in the author's earlier book *Permission Marketing*: the privilege of delivering messages people actually want to receive, as opposed to stealing scarce attention through spam. Real permission is demonstrated behaviorally: **"If you stop showing up, people are concerned. They ask where you went."** It's earned incrementally, like dating — not claimed via fine print, a purchased list, or the absence of a complaint.

**Case example: Yoyodyne.** The author's own 1990s direct-marketing company achieved open rates over 70 percent and response rates around 33 percent on permission-based email — roughly a thousand times the rate of a typical 2018 commercial email — by only ever contacting people who had actively opted in.

**Own your permission asset; don't rent it.** Contacting people through a social platform makes you a "sharecropper" — the platform, not you, owns the relationship and can monetize or restrict your access to it at will. **"Every publisher, every media company, every author of ideas needs to own a permission asset — the privilege of contacting people without a middleman."** The promise made to earn permission must be honored exactly as stated (e.g., a newsletter cadence) until both sides explicitly agree to change it — launching a new product, running for office, or hitting quarter-end doesn't grant the right to unilaterally alter the deal. A useful metric: how many people would actively notice or complain if you stopped showing up?

**Case examples of owned permission assets:** DailyCandy (Dany Levy's city-focused email newsletter, eventually sold for $100M+); Tuma Basa's RapCaviar Spotify playlist (grew from a defensive hire in 2015 to 9 million subscribers within three years, effectively becoming more influential than any single radio station or magazine in shaping hit music).

**Earning permission through generosity, not a pitch.** Marvel launches new franchises at Comic-Con — not with a sales pitch (the movie isn't out for a year) but with generosity: exclusive footage, director interviews, real news — signaling to the tribe's neophiliac core that "this is the sort of thing people like us will be talking about," and trusting the word to spread from there.

**The Purple Cow — remarkability as the transmission mechanism.** It's nearly impossible to spread word directly (too slow, too expensive to interrupt and enroll one by one); the alternative is intentionally building a product or service that people *decide* is worth talking about. Critically, **whether something is remarkable is decided by the user, not the creator** — "if they remark on it, then it's remarkable... if they remark on it, the word spreads" — but it must be built with intent, deep in the product or service itself, not bolted on as a stunt.

**Offensive/juvenile/urgent/selfish stunts are not the same as purple.** People spread word about you because doing so is a form of talking about themselves ("look at my taste," "look at how good I am at spotting important ideas") — but they'll also spread word to *shun* you, sending a signal that you're to be avoided, if you cross a line. Word-of-mouth serves the sharer's taste, standing, and desire for novelty — not your intentions.

**Suspending "Fight Club rules."** In Chuck Palahniuk's novel, "the first rule of Fight Club is you don't talk about Fight Club" — which, for the right character/worldview, is itself an invitation to talk about it (a version of Metcalfe's Law). Alcoholics Anonymous, despite "anonymous" branding, is built on the practice of actively talking about AA as a generous, shame-killing act of fellowship. Ideas now travel horizontally, person to person — begin with the smallest possible core and give them something worth talking about.

**Designing for evangelism.** Evangelism (a member proactively bringing tension to a non-member — e.g., an AA member reaching out to someone with a drinking problem) is emotionally difficult and easy to avoid. The design task is to build the incentive to evangelize into the fabric of the offering itself — people spread word because it serves *their* goals and self-story, not because it matters to you.

## 19. Trust Is as Scarce as Attention

**The trust vacuum.** Affiliation-based internet culture threatens dominion-oriented actors, who've generated waves of distrust toward institutions we used to trust culturally (media, politicians, spiritual institutions, social networks, even neighbors) — compounded by real, exposed misbehavior among those same institutions. The result: "more people are connected and fewer are trusted."

**Three available paths for a marketer in a low-trust environment:** ignored, sneaking around, or trusted. Being ignored forfeits both trust and attention. Sneaking around (pretending to be one thing while acting differently) can steal some attention and manufacture faux trust, but it doesn't last. Trust is the only path that repays the investment required — and it's also the easiest to live with. A trusted marketer earns enrollment, keeps promises (building more trust), tells stories that lead to more enrollment and more promises, and eventually earns peer-to-peer word of mouth. **Benefit of the doubt is real, but scarce** — it's granted only once people's own identity and status are already invested in your direction.

**The trust of action, not talk.** In a scanning, gossiping culture, action earns trust better than words do — "we remember what you did long after we forget what you said." The relevant moments are what you did after a refund request, a data breach, a plant closure — the daily, one-person-at-a-time behavior nobody's watching, not the press conference.

**Famous to the tribe.** "Everyone is famous to fifteen hundred people" (some to three thousand). This kind of narrow fame, where people you trust have also heard of someone, functions differently than mass fame — and is enough: the goal is being known to the smallest viable audience, not maximizing social-media follower counts.

**Public relations vs. publicity.** Publicity chases clips, write-ups, and features ("as long as they spell my name right"). Public relations is the more valuable discipline: telling your story to the right people in the right way, in service of building a durable idea engine rather than chasing ink at all costs.

## 20. The Funnel and the Long Tail

**The funnel as leaky trust pipe.** Attention pours in the top; committed, loyal customers come out the bottom; most people leak out along the way through mismatch, distraction, fear, or simply not being the right fit. As people move through the funnel (stranger → friend → customer → loyal customer), their trust status shifts — sometimes upward through experience and cognitive dissonance, more often downward as saying "yes" gets more stressful the further along the path they go.

**Six ways to fix your funnel:**
1. Make sure the right people are attracted to it in the first place.
2. Make sure the promise that brought them in aligns with where you hope to take them.
3. Remove steps so fewer decisions are required.
4. Support people through the funnel, reinforcing their dreams and easing their fears.
5. Use tension to create forward motion.
6. Hand people who successfully complete the funnel a megaphone — a tool to tell others ("people like us do things like this").

**Funnel math, illustrated (Casey Neistat example).** 1,000,000 video views → 18,000 click through to a livestream → ~1,000 leave a comment → ~5 take further action. **"Your mileage may vary"** — but the key insight is that Casey's advantage isn't a perfectly optimized funnel, it's that the *top* of his funnel is effortlessly and continuously filled by an already-enrolled audience.

**The sustainable direct-marketing funnel — full mechanics.** Example: $1,000 spent reaching a million people yields 20 clicks ($50/click); 1 in 10 clicks converts to an order ($500/order). If lifetime customer value exceeds $500, you can profitably keep buying ads at the same rate — "the funnel ratchets forward." Google/Facebook profit by skimming a large share of that margin (e.g., Google nets $100/sale in profit vs. the advertiser's $10) — but advertisers keep buying because their own marginal math is still positive. **The essential rule: figure out lifetime customer value first**, then work backward to what you can afford to spend per click/order (illustrated via an old-fashioned direct-mail stamp analogy: if a 50-cent stamped letter needs 1,000 sends to generate one $700-lifetime-value order, buy all the stamps you can afford; if lifetime value is only $400, you have no business buying stamps — fix the offer or the business, not the mailing volume). **"If you can't see the funnel, don't buy the ads."**

**The long tail (with the short head).** In any ranked market (Amazon books, streaming music, websites), a small number of hits (the short head) vastly outsell everything else, but the aggregate long tail — specialized, low-volume individual items — can rival or exceed the short head in total volume (half of Amazon's book sales are titles outside the top 5,000; half the *volume* of music streamed isn't available in stores). Big platforms profit by aggregating the long tail; any single creator stuck on the long tail struggles to make a living from it alone.

**The strategy implied by the long tail: divide the market into many curves, not one.** Every specific market (literary fiction for teens, chip-carving books, GH5 camera tutorials, improv performance, even ear-splitting drone music) has its own short head once its audience is connected enough to recognize each other and see what "the hit" is — because it's the hit that connects them and signals belonging. **Living successfully on (what looks like) the long tail requires two things:** (1) create the definitive, most essential contribution to the specific field; (2) connect the market you designed it for so they recognize you as their short head — the glue holding the tribe together.

## 21. Crossing the Chasm

**Geoffrey Moore's chasm.** Early adopters buy things specifically because they're new, interesting, and a little risky — but there aren't enough of them to sustain mass organizations or profits, which depend on the middle of the market. The chasm exists because the mass market wants the opposite of what early adopters want: something safe, proven, a pattern match rather than a pattern interrupt. Satisfying early adopters may require the very disruption the mass market actively avoids — few innovations glide smoothly from one part of the market to the other.

**The bridge across the chasm is the network effect.** Early adopters have a direct, selfish incentive to bring an idea to the masses when the idea *works better the more people use it* — there's no reason to talk about a chocolate you like (others eating it doesn't improve your life), but every reason to recruit friends onto Snapchat, Instagram, or Twitter, because your own experience improves as your network grows. **The bridge is built on two questions: What will I tell my friends? Why will I tell them?** People never spread word simply because the marketer wants them to or asked them to — they need a "why," which usually means changing the offer itself to include a genuine network effect.

**The Gartner Hype Cycle** is referenced as a meta-model of how a "technology trigger" leads through a Peak of Inflated Expectations, a Trough of Disillusionment, a Slope of Enlightenment, and finally a Plateau of Productivity.

**Case Study: Facebook's chasm-crossing.** Facebook deliberately launched inside a single highly status-anxious, highly connected local tribe (Harvard), where a neophiliac went first (because early adopters like going first) and the relentless status ratchet (more friends = more status; other Ivy League students already had admired status) did the rest. It then crossed one campus chasm at a time before crossing to the general public — "the last chasm leap is the billion-dollar one," and status again did the heavy lifting (join, or face social isolation). This scale of leap is explicitly called unlikely for most marketers to replicate — "the market is just too big, and the typical network effect isn't that strong."

**Crossing the local chasm is realistic and sufficient.** Illustrated by a schoolyard yo-yo fad: an ordinary kid bringing a yo-yo on the wrong day fails; a charismatic, well-liked (but not intimidating) kid who starts an inclusive "Yo-yo Union" and brings extra yo-yos for friends succeeds — a small, connected, low-cost-of-entry local network effect spreads through the whole playground within weeks (though fads without built-in identity/persistence cool off just as fast as they spread).

**Case Study: Water Health International (clean water adoption).** Early adopters buy WHI's purified water first — some out of genuine understanding of the health value, more often simply because they like buying new things — and are eager to talk about it, aided by a distinctive, brightly colored Jerry can that functions as both anti-contamination measure and conversation-starting badge. WHI accelerates the local chasm crossing by working through schoolchildren (microscope demonstrations of parasites in home water samples, which kids then report to parents), which activates status pressure among adults (feeling embarrassed not to have clean water once neighbors and respected village figures visibly do) — a network effect based on physical proximity rather than software, crossing the local chasm within months.

**A note on B2B marketing.** B2B (business-to-business) marketing looks procedural (RFPs, meeting spec, price wars, long cycles) but reduces to the same underlying mechanism: every business buyer asks herself, **"What will I tell my boss?"** — and you're marketing the answer to that question. A marketer out of ideas finishes the sentence with "…bought the cheapest one"; a better marketer finishes it with a narrative about status, fear, affiliation, belonging, dominion, safety, commitment, or insight. (Cited example: LEED certification adoption for buildings grew from just two submissions a day at launch to tens of thousands over roughly a decade, following the same status/network-effect logic as consumer adoption curves.)

## 22. Organizing and Leading a Tribe

**"It's not your tribe."** A leader doesn't own the tribe or get to direct members toward personal aims. At best, a tribe listens to you and interprets your words in ways it believes will move its own mission forward — earning you the chance to do it again. The tribe would likely survive without you; the goal is for it to *miss* you if you left.

**The story of self, us, and now (Marshall Ganz, who worked with both Cesar Chavez and Barack Obama).**
- **Story of self** gives you standing — a platform to speak from. It's not catastrophizing or performative vulnerability; it's demonstrating "you are people like us, you did things like this," and your resulting change is legible and credible.
- **Story of us** is the kernel of the tribe — why are we alike, why should we care, can I imagine being in your shoes. It's about togetherness, explaining why the story of self is relevant to *us* and how we all benefit from belonging.
- **Story of now** is the pivot that enlists the tribe in action — the peer opportunity/peer pressure that supplies tension for everyone to move together, urgently, without leaving anyone behind.

Illustrative example structure given in the text (a weight-loss/figure-skating narrative): paragraph one is the story of self (a personal transformation); paragraph two is the story of us (how the change extended to relationships, including people like the listener); paragraph three is the story of now (an explicit, immediate call to action — "I called ahead and reserved rental skates for you").

**Manipulation is the tribe killer — Saul Alinsky's thirteen *Rules for Radicals* principles**, cited as tools historically used for zero-sum political conflict:
1. "Power is not only what you have but what the enemy thinks you have."
2. "Never go outside the expertise of your people."
3. "Whenever possible, go outside the expertise of the enemy."
4. "Make the enemy live up to its own book of rules."
5. "Ridicule is man's most potent weapon."
6. "A good tactic is one your people enjoy."
7. "A tactic that drags on too long becomes a drag."
8. "Keep the pressure on."
9. "The threat is usually more terrifying than the thing itself."
10. "The major premise for tactics is the development of operations that will maintain a constant pressure upon the opposition."
11. "If you push a negative hard and deep enough, it will break through into its counterside."
12. "The price of a successful attack is a constructive alternative."
13. "Pick the target, freeze it, personalize it, and polarize it."

**The book's proposed reversal of these same thirteen principles**, reframed for building rather than defeating:
1. "Put people to work. It's even more effective than money."
2. "Challenge your people to explore, to learn, and to get comfortable with uncertainty."
3. "Find ways to help others on the path find firm footing."
4. "Help others write rules that allow them to achieve their goals."
5. "Treat the others the way you'd want to be treated."
6. "Don't criticize for fun. Do it when it helps educate, even if it's not entertaining."
7. "Stick with your tactics long after everyone else is bored with them. Only stop when they stop working."
8. "It's okay to let the pressure cease now and then. People will pay attention to you and the change you seek when they are unable to consistently ignore it."
9. "Don't make threats. Do or don't do."
10. "Build a team with the capacity and the patience to do the work that needs doing."
11. "If you bring your positive ideas to the fore, again and again, you'll raise the bar for everyone else."
12. "Solve your own problems before you spend a lot of time finding problems for the others."
13. "Celebrate your people, free them to do even more, make it about the cohort, and invite everyone along. Disagree with institutions, not with people."

**Shared interests, goals, and language build a tribe** even without a formal leader. The marketer's real opportunity is connecting lonely, disconnected tribe members to each other — through intentional cultural artifacts (costumes, code words, secret handshakes), some of it deliberately left unstated (Easter eggs, unannounced features) so that commitment and longevity earn an extra edge. Example: Nike's multimillion-dollar "Breaking2" moonshot (attempting a sub-two-hour marathon) engaged and challenged the tribe regardless of literal success.

**Tribal engagement decays without maintenance — "it will fade if you let it."** Early adopters are perpetually drawn to the next new thing and will be first to leave; people who preferred the old status quo may also drift back once tension dissipates, because the abandoned status quo never disappeared, it just went quiet. There's a **half-life** to unmaintained tribal behavior — some fraction of engagement disappears in any given period without continued, energetic reinvestment. **"The best marketers are farmers, not hunters. Plant, tend, plow, fertilize, weed, repeat."**

**Case Study: Zig Ziglar's "take a room in town."** Most 1960s door-to-door salespeople hit the easy early-adopter sales in a town, then moved to the next town. Ziglar instead took a room for weeks, ran repeated demonstration dinners, and built five-to-seven touch relationships with the "middle of the curve" who needed multiple exposures before deciding — deliberately building a human bridge across the local chasm rather than fleeing when he hit it, on the theory that the volume gained after crossing more than compensates for the days with no sales at all.

## 23. Case Studies in the Method

**"How do I get an agent?" (Brian Koppelman's reframe).** Screenwriters, directors, and actors ask this constantly, but an agent doesn't function as a tireless full-time promoter. **The actual method is to do work so impossibly magical that agents and producers come looking for you** — work that creates an imbalance in the viewer that can only be relieved by sharing it with someone else, raising the status of whoever asks "Have you seen…?" This works precisely because it's not always a fully polished, complete creation — imbalance and tension drive the spread.

**Case Study: Tesla — breaking the other car first.** The Model S's primary function, for early buyers, wasn't transportation (none of the first 50,000 buyers actually needed a car) — it was to break the story that luxury-car neophiliacs told themselves about the car already in their garage, undoing their status as smart, wealthy, tech-savvy, environmentally conscious, or audacious. Legacy manufacturers (Ford, GM, Toyota) had the resources and technical know-how to build something similar but couldn't, because "car companies like us don't take risks like this" — and their existing customers held the same self-image. Musk deliberately chose difficult, simultaneous extremes (fastest, safest, and most efficient of its size, all three at once) to position the car on behalf of the audience he sought.

**Case Study: The NRA as a (values-neutral) marketing role model.** With only ~5 million members (under 2 percent of the U.S. population), the NRA has changed the attitudes and focus of thousands of lawmakers by focusing relentlessly on its minimal viable audience, comfortably saying "it's not for you" to everyone else, and activating members to contact officials (a Pew study cited: gun owners are more than twice as likely to contact government officials about their issues than non-owners). The NRA embraces existing worldviews rather than trying to change them, and creates a clear "people like us" in-group with sharp, sometimes deliberately divisive public statements — offered explicitly as an example of *method*, not of the author's own political endorsement ("The NRA isn't my version of 'better,' but it clearly resonates with those they seek to serve").

**Case Study: Getting the boss to say yes.** Framed as identical in mechanism to any other marketing challenge. A boss is not eager to change her worldview; she wants what she's always wanted, sees things through her own experience, is attentive to her own "people like us," and wants status, safety, and respect. Leading with price, features, or false urgency rarely works, and asking for authority without offering responsibility rarely works either. The alternative: identify her status roles, read whether she leans dominion or affiliation, and use trust-building to earn enrollment — turning her, in effect, from "boss to be pitched" into a student being taught, step by step, drip by drip.

## 24. Marketing Ethics and Closing Principles

**Is marketing evil?** The book's direct answer: not marketing itself, but "some" marketers are. Named as genuinely evil: persuading kids to start smoking, cynically manipulating the electoral process, lying in ways that cause disastrous harm, selling an ineffective potion when an effective medicine exists, inventing new ways to make smoking acceptable for profit. Named as beautiful, powerful, or magic: marketing that persuades people to get a polio vaccine, wash hands before surgery, buy something that genuinely brings more joy or productivity, or elect someone who improves the community. **The test proposed: marketing works for society when the marketer and the consumer are both aware of what's happening and both satisfied with the outcome.** Swindling someone out of their house to earn a commission is wrong regardless of whether "you're just doing your job," precisely *because* marketing works — impact makes the marketer responsible, not exempt.

**The tyranny of perfect vs. the possibility of better vs. the magic of good enough.**
- **Perfect** closes the door — it asserts you're done and forbids further attempts; seeking perfection and failing to reach it is treated as a failure.
- **Better** opens the door — it invites improvement on behalf of those you serve.
- **Good enough** is not a shortcut but a starting point for a specific causal chain: good enough leads to engagement, engagement leads to trust, trust lets you see (if you choose to look), seeing allows learning, learning allows a promise, a promise might earn enrollment, and enrollment is exactly what's needed to achieve *better*. **"Ship your work. It's good enough. Then make it better."**

**Marketing yourself to yourself.** The most persistent obstacle to marketing well is often the private story you tell yourself — that you're struggling, unknown (and deserve to be), a fake or a fraud, or unjustly ignored. **"They're as true as we want them to be. And if you tell yourself a story enough times, you will make it true."** If your work fails to land, the honest diagnosis usually isn't "I lack a purpose" but "my marketing craft needs work" — the wrong axes chosen, empathy missing, the wrong story told to the wrong person on the wrong day. That's a fixable craft problem, not an identity verdict.

**Failing to market your work, when it's genuinely valuable, is a form of stealing** — you're withholding value from someone who would benefit more than they'd pay, and hesitation isn't modesty, it's a disservice to the person who needed to learn from, engage with, or buy from you.

**Closing executive definition — "This is marketing":**
- Marketing seeks *more* — more market share, more customers, more work.
- Marketing is driven by *better* — better service, better community, better outcomes.
- Marketing creates culture — status, affiliation, and "people like us." Most of all, marketing is change.
- Change the culture, change your world.
- Marketers make change happen.
- Each of us is a marketer with the ability to make more change than we imagine; the obligation is to do marketing we're proud of.

**"How tall is your sunflower?"** Most marketers fixate on visible height — brand size, market share, follower counts — but tall sunflowers have deep, complex root systems; without roots (anchoring your work in the actual dreams, desires, and communities of the people you serve) height is never reached. The book frames itself explicitly as "a book about roots."

**How to know if you have a marketing problem** (a diagnostic checklist offered near the close): you aren't busy enough; your ideas aren't spreading; the community around you isn't what it could be; the people you care about aren't achieving what they hoped; your candidate needs more votes; your work isn't fulfilling; your customers are frustrated. **If you can see a way to make any of this better, you now have a marketing problem** — meaning: a solvable one.

**The Simple Marketing Worksheet (verbatim structure from the book's appendix):**
- Who's it for? What's it for?
- What is the worldview of the audience you're seeking to reach? What are they afraid of?
- What story will you tell? Is it true? What change are you seeking to make? How will it change their status?
- How will you reach the early adopters and neophiliacs? Why will they tell their friends? What will they tell their friends?
- Where's the network effect that will propel this forward? What asset are you building? Are you proud of it?
