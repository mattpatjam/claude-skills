# Breakthrough Advertising — Framework Reference

*Pure framework reference distilled from the book. No commentary, no application notes — just the frameworks, models, and mental tools as the author presents them.*

*Source: Breakthrough Advertising by Eugene M. Schwartz*

---

## 1. Mass Desire — The Force That Makes Advertising Work

The central thesis of the entire book: copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already-existing desires onto a particular product. This is the copywriter's task — not to create mass desire, but to channel and direct it. No single advertiser can spend enough money to actually create mass desire; he can only exploit it, and he "dies" when he tries to run against it.

Proof by case history — the automotive field:
- 1948: the public wanted a longer, lower, wider car. Chrysler bucked the trend with a shorter, more functional car. Catastrophic results despite a brilliant campaign.
- 1954: the public wanted horsepower. Ford tried to sell safety instead. Results were non-existent; the next year Ford advertised the highest horsepower in its history.
- The Edsel: a good car, backed by fine advertising, that died fighting the overwhelming switch in demand toward cheap, simple, compact cars.

**Definition:** Mass Desire is the public spread of a private want. Advertising is a business of statistics — it depends on many individuals sharing the same response. A market is born the moment a private desire is shared by a statistically significant number of people large enough to profitably repay the cost of advertising, manufacturing, and selling, plus a profit.

**The Amplification Effect:** because mass desires are created by forces (social, economic, technological) far greater than advertising itself can command, advertising that exploits already-existing desire commands an economic force far more powerful than the dollars spent on it — $1 spent can create $50 or even $100 in sales. When advertising instead tries to *create* desire, it is no longer advertising but education, and can produce at best $1 in sales for every $1 spent.

**The two categories of nationwide forces that create mass desire:**
- **Permanent Forces**
  - *Mass Instinct* — desires that never fade (women wanting to be attractive, men wanting to be virile, everyone wanting health). The copywriter's job is not to spot the trend (it's already visible) but to distinguish his product from others already competing for it.
  - *A mass technological problem* — an unsolved problem (bad TV reception, corroding mufflers, slow-acting aspirin) that keeps customers buying and trying until it's solved.
- **The Forces of Change**
  - *Style* — the beginning, fulfillment, and reversal of a trend (swimming pools over bigger cars; horsepower appeal subordinated to gas economy). Requires sensitivity, foresight, and intuition to catch the rising tide early.
  - *Mass Education* — the slow trickle-down of tastes and behaviors through society (school primers, movie screens, celebrity example, group pressure, backyard gossip, and the cumulative unconscious effect of all advertising raising the standard of living). The key problem here is timing: when does the shift become statistically significant?

**The copywriter's three-stage process for channeling mass desire onto a product:**
1. **Choose the most powerful desire that can possibly be applied to your product.** Every mass desire has three vital dimensions:
   - *Urgency/intensity* — degree of demand to be satisfied (constant arthritic pain vs. a minor headache).
   - *Staying power* — degree of repetition, inability to become satiated (raw hunger vs. a craving for gourmet food).
   - *Scope* — the number of people who share the desire.
   Every product appeals to two, three, or four such desires, but only one can predominate in the headline.
2. **Acknowledge that desire — reinforce it and/or offer the means to satisfy it — in a single statement in the headline.** The headline is the bridge between prospect and product, meeting the prospect exactly at his current point of awareness:
   - If he's aware of the product and that it satisfies his desire, the headline starts with the product.
   - If he's aware only of the desire, the headline starts with the desire.
   - If he's aware only of a general problem, the headline starts with the problem and crystallizes it into a specific need.
3. **Show, through the product's built-in performances, how it inevitably satisfies that desire.**

**The Analysis of the Product — Physical vs. Functional:** every product is actually two products. The *physical product* (the steel, glass, paper) does not sell by itself — people do not buy the steel in a car. The *functional product* (what it does — the series of benefits performed for the consumer) is what actually sells. The physical product's only role is to reinforce and document the functional promise, in four ways:
- By justifying the price (more steel, more stitches per inch = higher price justified).
- By documenting the quality of performance (weight of steel in a car door → belief the car protects you in a crash).
- By assuring the performance continues over time (ceramic mufflers mean no repair bills for the car's life).
- By sharpening the reader's mental picture of the performance (Rolls-Royce's rubber-shielded chassis parts → belief in perfect riding silence).
- Above all, by giving the performance claim a fresh basis for believability when a new entrant is invading an established Mass Instinct field — a new mechanism, new quality, or new freedom from limitations (the "point of difference").

Every product offers several distinct sets of performances (illustrated with the automobile: transportation, dependability, economy, power, recognition, value, novelty), but an ad can effectively tap only one dominant performance at a time — because the headline has only one glance of the reader's attention to work with. The copywriter's job is to find the one dominant performance that fits the lock, and squeeze every drop of power out of it.

## 2. Your Prospect's State of Awareness

Before writing a headline, three questions must be answered: (1) what is the mass desire that creates this market, (2) how much does the market already know about how the product satisfies that desire (their **State of Awareness**), and (3) how many similar products have they already been told about (their **State of Sophistication**, covered in the next section).

**The headline's real job:** a headline does not need to sell, does not need to mention the product, and does not even need to mention the main appeal. Its only job is to stop the prospect and compel him to read the second sentence — and the second sentence's only job is to force him to read the third, and so on down the page. Only a prospect actively seeking a specific brand-name product, offered a special price reduction, can be sold entirely through the headline; attempting a full sell in the headline for any other case is "shooting craps with your client's money."

**The Five Stages of Awareness** (from easiest to hardest copy problem):

1. **The Most Aware.** The customer knows the product, knows what it does, knows he wants it — he just hasn't bought yet. The headline needs only the product name and a bargain price (example: *"Revere Zomar Lens, Electric Eye Camera — Formerly $149.50 — Now Only $119.95."*). This is typical department-store/discount/catalog advertising; there is nothing creative about the job.

2. **The customer knows of the product but doesn't yet want it.** The great bulk of all advertising. Here the headline faces one of seven tasks:
   - Reinforce desire for the product, via *Association* (*"Steinway — The Instrument of the Immortals"*), *Sensory Sharpening* (*"Tastes like you just picked it — Dole"*), or *Illustration*.
   - Sharpen the prospect's image of how the product satisfies desire (*"At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise in a Rolls Royce is the electric clock."*).
   - Extend the image of where/when the product satisfies desire (*"Thirst knows no season" — Coca-Cola, run in winter*).
   - Introduce new proof/documentation (*"9 out of 10 screen stars use Lux Toilet Soap..."*).
   - Announce a new mechanism that satisfies the desire even better (*"Hoover's new invention washes floors and vacuums up the scrub water."*).
   - Announce a new mechanism that eliminates former limitations (*"You breathe no dusty odors when you do it with Lewyt."*).
   - Completely change the image or mechanism to remove the product from competing for the same claim (this is a State-of-Sophistication problem, covered in Section 3).

3. **How to introduce new products** — the prospect recognizes he wants what the product does, but doesn't know a product exists that does it. Three steps: name the desire/solution in the headline, prove the solution can be accomplished, show the mechanism is contained in your product. Requires analysis, intuition (sensing a trend at its start), and verbal creativity. Examples: *"Light a Lucky, and you'll never miss sweets that make you fat."*; *"Who else wants a whiter wash — with no hard work?"*; *"How to win friends and influence people."*; *"Now! Run your car without spark plugs."*

4. **How to introduce products that solve needs** — the prospect has a need, recognizes it immediately, but doesn't yet see the connection to the product. Start by naming the need/solution, dramatize the need vividly, present the product as the inevitable solution. Ranges from the barest statement (*"Corns?"*) to problem+solution (*"Stops maddening itch."*) to removal-of-limitation promises (*"Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery."*) to preventive framing (*"Look, Mom! No cavities!"*) to sharpened, previously-vague problems (*"Do YOU make these mistakes in English?"*).

5. **How to open up a completely unaware market** — the hardest case: the prospect isn't aware of his desire/need, won't admit it, or it resists verbalization. Each stage of awareness is separated from the others by a psychological wall; a headline effective in one stage fails completely in another. Rules for this stage, arrived at by elimination:
   - Eliminate all mention of price — it means nothing to someone who doesn't want the product yet.
   - Keep the product name out of the headline — it means nothing (or may actively hurt).
   - Do not state directly what the product does or what desire/problem it solves — this is not a sophistication problem but one of complete indifference; a direct claim simply will not work.
   - What's left: the market itself. Write an **identification headline** — one that sells nothing, promises nothing, satisfies nothing, but instead echoes an emotion or attitude that picks people out from the crowd and binds them together, telling them what they are. Its only function is to get the prospect to read the next paragraph, which pulls him into the third, and so on — building a progression toward the eventual product.
   - Products that begin to slip in sales despite increased advertising budgets, where direct statements of the product name, function, or desire no longer sell, need to be "reborn" via this fifth-stage approach.

Several fully-analyzed period examples of the identification headline are given, including: *"THE UNIVERSITY OF THE NIGHT"* (correspondence courses, giving words to a hidden dream); *"WHY MEN CRACK…"* (Postum, exploiting a hidden fear); *"WITHIN THE CURVE OF A WOMAN'S ARM"* (Odorono, leading into an unacceptable problem via a universally accepted image); the Marlboro tattoo ad (projecting a hidden desire that can't be put into words); *"WHY HAVEN'T TV OWNERS BEEN TOLD THESE FACTS"* (exploiting a common resentment to broaden the market beyond handymen); *"THEY LAUGHED WHEN I SAT DOWN AT THE PIANO. BUT WHEN I STARTED TO PLAY!—"* (projecting an ultimate triumph the prospect identifies with); *"OFTEN A BRIDESMAID BUT NEVER A BRIDE"* (Listerine, projecting the result of a problem too insulting to state directly); *"HERE'S AN EXTRA $50, GRACE—"* (projecting the reward while avoiding the effort implied to get there).

A final note on style: advertising styles (verse, first-person confession, narrative) go stale and become laughable over time, but the underlying strategies they express remain timeless — the same desires, differently dressed, could still sell today.

## 3. The Sophistication of Your Market

The third question determining headline content: how many similar products has this market already been told about? Research is straightforward — a few hours reviewing competing ads reveals it.

**If you are first in your market:** prospects have no prior sophistication, so they believe more readily and buy more readily. This happens via technological breakthrough (hair sprays), a radically better product (long-playing records), a familiar product at an explosively low price (the Model T), or an ad man's insight applying an existing product to a new market (Ovaltine repositioned from insomnia aid to body-builder for children; Revlon's sponsorship of "The $64,000 Question"; Lifebuoy's discovery that its medicinal odor could be sold as a cure for "B.O."). Strategy: **be simple, be direct.** Name either the need or the claim in the headline, nothing more (*"NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!"*).

**The Five Stages of Sophistication:**

1. **First Stage.** The raw, direct claim, stated simply (*"NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!"*).

2. **Second Stage.** Once others have entered with the same claim, mere repetition stops working. Strategy: copy the successful claim but enlarge it, drive it to the limit, outbid the competition (*"LOSE UP TO 47 POUNDS IN 4 WEEKS — OR RECEIVE $40 BACK!"*; *"I AM 61 POUNDS LIGHTER…NEVER A HUNGRY MINUTE."*; the rose-plant headline escalation from *"PICK 25-50-100 ROSES"* to *"SIX HUNDRED MUMS FROM A SINGLE BUSH!"* to *"WHO EVER HEARD OF 17,000 BLOOMS FROM A SINGLE PLANT?"*). Eventually claims become unbelievable, the government investigates, and sales turn down.

3. **Third Stage.** The market has now heard every claim taken to its extreme and stopped believing them, yet the underlying desire never fades — it's a matter of restorative power (recurring mass instinct, unsolved technological problem, or periodic renewal need). Strategy: shift emphasis from *what* the product does to *how* it works — bring a **new mechanism** into the headline itself, restoring freshness and believability to an old claim (*"FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!"*; *"FIRST WONDER DRUG FOR REDUCING!"*). The claim, once dominant in the headline, is now compressed into a subordinate restatement while the mechanism leads.

4. **Fourth Stage.** Competitors adopt the new mechanism and elaborate on it exactly as Second Stage elaborated on the original claim (*"FIRST NO-DIET REDUCING WONDER DRUG!"*). Same limitations eventually apply: believability collapses again.

5. **Fifth Stage.** The field is "exhausted" — competitors drop out en masse, the market no longer wants to be aware of the product category. This corresponds directly to the Fifth Stage of Awareness (Section 2): shift entirely from promise/mechanism to identification with the prospect himself.

**Full industry case study — cigarettes**, tracing all five stages: taste/pleasure claims (*"I'D WALK A MILE FOR A CAMEL!"*) → broader comparison claims (*"LIGHT UP A LUCKY, AND YOU WON'T MISS THE SWEETS THAT MAKE YOU FAT!"*) → mechanism claims (*"LUCKIES — THEY'RE TOASTED!"*; *"PALL MALL'S GREATER LENGTH FILTERS THE SMOKE FURTHER!"*) → competitor mechanism-elaboration (*"NINE OUT OF TEN DOCTORS PREFER LUCKIES!"*) → Fifth Stage solved via Motivation Research and pure visual identification (Marlboro's "Virile Men" ads, no headline or claim needed). Filter cigarettes then retraced the exact same five stages a generation later (Kent's "Micronite Filter" → Viceroy's "20,000 Filter Traps" → Parliament's "Most Important ¼ Inch" → Tareyton's "Dual Filter" → Winston's identification-flavor return, *"TASTES GOOD LIKE A CIGARETTE SHOULD"*).

Two named **side problems** encountered by the cigarette industry, illustrating cross-cutting strategy: (1) doubling the market by making smoking acceptable for women — solved entirely through an identification image linking a man and woman in a romantic scene (*"BLOW SOME MY WAY."*), since a direct statement would have been rejected; (2) retaining the market after a health scare — solved by channeling smokers into a different kind of cigarette (filters) rather than fighting the scare directly.

A closing "personal note" from Schwartz: strategies like copying a competitor's fresh mechanism overnight are listed here purely as objective, effective business strategy in a competitive industry — not as ethical recommendation.

## 4. 38 Ways to Strengthen Your Headline (Verbalization)

Once the content of the headline is determined (via mass desire, awareness, and sophistication analysis), **Verbalization** is the separate technique of increasing headline impact through *how* it is stated, not what it says. It can strengthen a claim, make it fresh again, or pull the prospect into the body copy. The most direct statement of a claim is best when you're first in the field; verbalization becomes necessary when competitive or complicated. The 38 named devices, each with example headlines from the text:

1. Measure the size of the claim (*"20,000 FILTER TRAPS IN VICEROY!"*)
2. Measure the speed of the claim (*"IN TWO SECONDS, BAYER ASPIRIN BEGINS TO DISSOLVE…"*)
3. Compare the claim (*"SIX TIMES WHITER WASHES!"*)
4. Metaphorize the claim (*"MELTS AWAY UGLY FAT!"*)
5. Sensitize the claim — feel/smell/touch/see/hear (*"TASTES LIKE YOU JUST PICKED IT!"*)
6. Demonstrate the claim with a prime example (*"JAKE LaMOTTA…FAILS TO FLATTEN MONO PAPER CUP!"*)
7. Dramatize the claim or its result (*"THEY LAUGHED WHEN I SAT DOWN AT THE PIANO…"*)
8. State the claim as a paradox (*"HOW A BALD-HEADED BARBER SAVED MY HAIR!"*)
9. Remove limitations from the claim (*"SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY!"*)
10. Associate the claim with people/values the prospect identifies with (*"MICKEY MANTLE SAYS: CAMELS NEVER BOTHER MY THROAT!"*)
11. Show how much work the claim does, in detail (*"RELIEF FROM ALL 5 ACID-CAUSED STOMACH TROUBLES!"*)
12. State the claim as a question (*"WHO ELSE WANTS A WHITER WASH — WITH NO HARD WORK?"*)
13. Offer information on how to accomplish the claim (*"HOW TO WIN FRIENDS AND INFLUENCE PEOPLE!"*)
14. Tie authority into the claim (*"BOSS MECHANIC SHOWS HOW TO AVOID ENGINE REPAIR BILLS!"*)
15. Before-and-after the claim (*"BEFORE COLDENE…AFTER 5 DAYS OF ACHING…"* vs. *"WITH COLDENE…IN FIVE DAYS!"*)
16. Stress the newness of the claim (*"ANNOUNCING! GUIDED MISSILE SPARK PLUGS!"*)
17. Stress the exclusivity of the claim (*"OURS ALONE! PERSIAN LAMB ORIGINALS!"*)
18. Turn the claim into a challenge for the reader (*"WHICH TWIN HAS THE TONI?"*)
19. State the claim as a case-history quotation (*"LOOK, MOM — NO CAVITIES!"*)
20. Condense the claim — interchange product and the thing it replaces (*"NOW! A RING AND PISTON JOB IN A TUBE!"*)
21. Symbolize the claim with a parallel reality (*"STARTING NEXT TUESDAY, THE ATLANTIC OCEAN BECOMES ONLY ONE-FIFTH AS LONG!"*)
22. Connect the mechanism to the claim in the headline (*"FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!"*)
23. Startle the reader by contradicting how he thinks the mechanism works (*"HIT HELL OUT OF THE BALL WITH YOUR RIGHT HAND!"*)
24. Connect the need and the claim in the headline (*"THERE IS ONLY ONE SOLUTION…FIND THE MAN!"*)
25. Offer information in the ad itself (*"WHY MEN CRACK…"*)
26. Turn the claim/need into a case history (*"AUNT MEG, WHO NEVER MARRIED…"*)
27. Give a name to the problem or need (*"DAYTIME FATIGUE"*)
28. Warn the reader about pitfalls of not using the product (*"DON'T INVEST ONE CENT…UNTIL YOU CHECK THIS GUIDE!"*)
29. Emphasize the claim through phraseology — breaking into two sentences or repeating part of it (*"NOBODY BUT NOBODY UNDERSELLS GIMBEL'S!"*)
30. Show ease of the claim by imposing a universally-overcome limitation (*"IF YOU CAN COUNT TO ELEVEN, YOU CAN INCREASE YOUR SPEED…"*)
31. State the difference directly in the headline (*"THE DIFFERENCE IN PREMIUM GASOLINES IS RIGHT IN THE ADDITIVES!"*)
32. Surprise the reader that former limitations are now overcome (*"SEE WHAT HAPPENS WHEN YOU CRUSH A HARTMAN DC-8? NOTHING!"*)
33. Address the people who *can't* buy the product (*"IF YOU'VE ALREADY TAKEN YOUR VACATION, DON'T READ THIS…"*)
34. Address the prospect directly (*"TO THE MAN WHO WILL SETTLE FOR NOTHING LESS THAN THE PRESIDENCY OF HIS FIRM."*)
35. Dramatize how hard it was to produce the claim (*"When Jens finished designing this candleholder we had to invent a whole new kind of candle."*)
36. Accuse the claim of being too good (*"IS IT IMMORAL TO MAKE MONEY THIS EASILY?"*)
37. Challenge the prospect's present limiting beliefs (*"YOU ARE TWICE AS SMART AS YOU THINK."*)
38. Turn the claim into a question and answer format.

Schwartz notes this list is not exhaustive — "an infinite number of variations" exist, and every good copywriter invents new ones.

## 5. The Art of Creative Planning

**The Three Levels of Creativity** used to find a headline:
1. **The Word-Substitute Technique** (shallowest, most widespread, most ineffective). Take a proven successful headline and swap in your own product name or performance (*"I'D WALK A MILE FOR A CUP OF BLANK COFFEE!"*). Works only if product, market, and timing all match the original — otherwise these become **Echo Ads**, ads that remind people of some other product, losing the unique power of the actual product-market-timing relationship. "This is the kind of ad that clients write, rather than copywriters."
2. **Formulas.** The copywriter memorizes rules or principles and pours the headline into them, like the 38 verbalization devices in Section 4 — valid for strengthening expression, but cannot generate the underlying idea itself.
3. **The Analytical Approach.** No answers, only guideposts and questions. The only real path to a true breakthrough, because "the problem defies a formula" — a solution that took weeks to develop can be used only once. Copywriters who stop searching for the unique solution "begin to copy instead of create," and eventually "begin to copy themselves" out of their own old files — which reduces talent to "file-cabinet mediocrity."

**On Motivation Research (MR):** two legitimate uses for the copywriter. First, as a source of information about needs and desires that may be hidden, verbally unacceptable, or unknown — but a motivation-research finding is never itself a headline or a theme; "like any other fact, it is a direction," telling you where not to waste time and indicating the general area of the solution. Second, as a tool for testing the copywriter's own hunches over the life of an ongoing campaign, surfacing research questions the copywriter can hand to MR specialists to phrase, test, and answer, from which a new headline or campaign theme can be born.

**On Product Personality:** a product (or store, or category) has a complete personality made of many traits, but one trait will always be the most effective in summarizing that personality (Cadillac = quality, always). This dominant trait is featured, blended with one or two others for variety, but never overtaking the primary trait — building "an ever-enlarging pyramid of persuasion" that terminates in the product.

**On Prevention Headlines:** a prevention headline can work, but only when the prospect visualizes the future problem happening to people he loves, not to himself directly — decay-prevention toothpaste works by focusing on the children, not the parent; life insurance sells by picturing the horror inflicted on a man's wife and children, not his own death.

**On Splinter Markets:** a market is rarely one uniform block (not all women who want to lose weight want it for the same reason — appearance vs. health are separate sub-categories requiring different mechanisms: speed/ease for appearance, safety/permanence for health). A smaller company can deliberately avoid competing for the core of a market and instead concentrate on a splinter segment — done regionally, or (more effectively) sociologically — altering headline, identification copy, mechanism, substantiation, media selection, and distribution channel accordingly. Success in the splinter segment can later become the point of difference used to invade the majority market.

**Summary of the entire creative-planning process:** analyze the market (breadth, depth, the emotional forces creating it) → analyze the product (what it is and what it does, physical vs. functional) → combine into a single theme (the desire/satisfaction, the need/solution, the identification/expression) → determine the point of entry via the market's State of Awareness and State of Sophistication. What results is not a formula but "compasses" — analytical procedures to break a problem into parts and find the most important one, then techniques to build up the solution's power by removing limiting factors and adding strengthening material (conception, then execution).

## 6. Inside Your Prospect's Mind — The Three Dimensions

After the headline stops the prospect, body copy does the selling by expanding or altering three dimensions of the prospect's existing mental world. The length, structure, development, style, and pace of an ad are all determined by how much space is needed to work on each:

1. **Desires.** Wants, needs, cravings, thirsts, hungers, lusts — physical, material, sensual. They already exist; they cannot be created, diminished, or fought — only expanded, sharpened, channeled, and given a goal. The mechanism for this is **Intensification** (Section 7).

2. **Identifications.** The roles the prospect wants to play in life and the personality traits he wants the product to help him build or project — not material/physical/sensual, but symbolic and status-driven, adding a second dimension of value to every purchase. The mechanism for this is **Identification** (Section 8).

3. **Beliefs.** The opinions, attitudes, prejudices, and conceptions of reality the prospect already lives by — "the world of emotionalized reason." Advertising cannot argue with these beliefs or change them; it must accept them as they exist and build up from them using the prospect's own kind of logic. The mechanisms for building belief are **Gradualization, Redefinition, Mechanization, and Concentration** (Sections 9–12), plus **Verification** (Section 14).

These three dimensions are studied separately for analytical clarity, but in an actual ad they are woven together into a single fluid path of thought — the technique of **Interweaving and Blending** (Section 14).

## 7. The First Technique of Breakthrough Copy: Intensification

Desire, as it exists in the prospect's mind, is vague, hazy, and only a fraction of its true potential power. The copywriter's job is to fill out vague desire with concrete images of fulfillment — to multiply its strength by the number of satisfactions suggested to achieve it. "You are literally the script writer for your prospect's dreams."

How much space to give Intensification depends on the medium (small-space media demand compression + campaign repetition; large-space/direct-mail media allow a full unfolding) and on how many *fresh, non-repetitive* perspectives on the same core image can be generated — the prospect will leave with only one dominant image, but every new angle on it makes that image sharper and heavier with emotional weight.

**Thirteen ways to intensify desire**, illustrated throughout with real mail-order copy blocks:
1. **First presentation of your claims** — a thorough, blunt, fully detailed description of the product's appearance or results (the rose-bush ad: *"MORE ROSES THAN YOU EVER SAW ON ANY ROSE BUSH…"*; the Bible flashlight-projector ad; the model-jet-airplane ad).
2. **Put the claims in action** — show not just what the product does but exactly how it does it (the model jet flying; the self-propelled fish lure buzzing and diving).
3. **Bring in the reader** — put the reader inside the product-in-action scene, showing what happens to him personally the first day he owns it (the car power-booster ad racing another car at a stoplight).
4. **Show him how to test your claims** — turn the demonstration into a specific, dramatic test the reader performs himself (the spark-plug idle-speed test; the memory-course 20-fact overnight test).
5. **Stretch out your benefits in time** — extend the vision across weeks and months of continuous benefit (the English plant-food ad's three-stage growth sequence).
6. **Bring in an audience** — celebrities (testimonial) or ordinary people (case history/narration) who provide a fresh perspective through their own reactions (the reducing-pill case-history passage).
7. **Show experts approving** — professionals, the sophisticated, the discriminating registering astonishment, adding surprise, competition, and discovery to the image (the rose-ad horticulturists' test-field astonishment).
8. **Compare, contrast, prove superiority** — lay the old product/service side by side with the new to throw advantages into relief (the Dollar Book Club price-comparison radio spot; the Fuel Reclaimer "polluted vs. free extra gas tank" contrast).
9. **Picture the black side, too (Heaven-or-Hell)** — paint the negative in full color, then apply the salve, generating two currents of motivation: repulsion from the old, attraction to the new (the spark-plug carbon-filth-vs.-Power-Flash-plug passage).
10. **Show how easy it is to get these benefits** — every point where the product touches the prospect's life (price, ease of use, unwrapping the carton) is a fresh angle to reiterate benefit (the "30 seconds work…eight different ways" motor-oil passage).
11. **Use metaphor, analogy, imagination** — go beyond raw fact into dramatized imaginative form (the Alexander Hamilton "Glory of the Upward Path" ad; Big Ben's "First he whispers, then he shouts"; the Sherwin Cody course personified as an ever-present, patient mentor).
12. **Before you're done, summarize** — using one of two conventional devices:
    - The **catalog** — a brief condensed listing of performances/benefits/applications with no elaboration, of two types: those that expand desire *horizontally* across more applications/prospects (the blow-torch kit's "Does 101 jobs around the house" list) — a deliberate "shotgun" hedge after the "high-velocity rifle" of a single dominant appeal — and those that expand desire *vertically* by piling desire upon desire in summary of everything that came before (the fish-lure ad's cumulative "Here's What This Amazing…Lure Does For You!" passage).
13. **Put your guarantee to work** — turn the guarantee itself into the ad's climax, a final summary of performance reinforced by positive reassertion (the spark-plug two-year guarantee; the plant-food money-back guarantee).

**Applying Intensification to the campaign** (as opposed to a single mail-order ad): national advertising runs far more frequently than mail order and therefore cannot rely on one all-inclusive ad — it must find a theme for a *series*. This creates the **Central Campaign Idea, restated in a series of fresh disguises**, illustrated along a spectrum:
- Fully repeated verbal + visual formula (Colgate's *"Gardol — the Invisible Shield…"*, with ritualistic wording and only the opening demonstration varying).
- **"Rule and Example"** — an exactly repeated headline/verbal image, paired with continuously changing illustrations that keep it fresh (Toni's *"WHICH TWIN HAS THE TONI…"*; Clairol's *"DOES SHE OR DOESN'T SHE?"*).
- A rigidly maintained **focal point** with no repeated slogan at all — either a dominant product appeal symbolized consistently across every execution (Cadillac's "quality," expressed through photography, typography, styling, and even the models' jewelry), or the product itself as the sole constant, with entirely new copy every time (the Volkswagen campaign — *"THINK SMALL."*, *"Lemon."*, *"Our new model."*, *"Why you should open the window before you close the door of a Volkswagen."*, *"Our number one salesman."* — each a **"Creative Gamble"**: omitting the product/trademark/performance from the headline entirely, betting that the body copy alone can close the sale).

## 8. The Second Technique of Breakthrough Copy: Identification

A second, more subtle and partly unconscious form of desire beyond physical want: the **Longing for Identification** — the desire of the prospect to act out certain roles in life and to define himself to the world. Every product should offer the prospect two separate reasons to buy: fulfillment of a physical want, and a particular method of fulfilling it that defines him to the world as a particular kind of person (the "role" the product offers — its non-functional, super-functional value, built in by merchandising rather than engineering).

**Two kinds of roles:**
- **Character Roles** — expressed by adjectives ("progressive," "chic," "well-read"). Part of the prospect's own personality; his task is to develop them and get others' attention on them. A product can help achieve mastery of a character role, simplify/speed that mastery, or serve as a symbol of the mastery to invoke others' admiration. Because character roles live largely in the subconscious and are rarely tested against reality, the prospect is *more* ready to believe a flattering character-role claim than either a performance claim or an achievement-role claim — "acceptance is easy, painless, non-demanding."
- **Achievement Roles** — status/class/position roles created by society and earned (*"Executive," "Home Owner," "$20,000-a-year Man"* for men; *"Wife," "Fashion Setter," "Career Woman"* for women), usually expressed by nouns/titles. Because these achievements are immaterial and invisible, they must be translated into physical symbols for display — and "the easiest and most universally acknowledged symbols of success in America today are the products we can buy."

**The most general, compelling roles Americans buy:** the wish to be virile or feminine; to be exciting, unique, fun-filled, adventurous; to be friendly and well-liked; to be important, influential, correct; to be modern and up-to-date; and above all, to be successful.

**The Primary Image of the product:** every product arrives with its own existing personality/characteristics, which may contradict, ignore, or only partially include what the prospect wants (a cigarette isn't inherently a symbol of success; a piston ring isn't inherently virile). You cannot contradict an accepted image — "they form the walls of a prison" — but you *can* build on it. Two techniques for building identification into a product:
1. **Change the intensity of the primary image** — dramatize it if favorable, tone it down if negative/neutral (Marlboro intensified inherent cigarette-virility three ways at once: virile men, in virile situations, plus the "Creative Gamble" of the tattoo — repetition reinforced through variation).
2. **Use the primary image as a bridge/base to graft on additional favorable images**, which must always be logically consistent with that primary image's broadest definition — never substituted outright, because "people just won't believe that a product is what it isn't." Illustrated via the 1926 Chesterfield *"Blow Some My Way"* ad: the male smoker (the accepted, unavoidable primary image) is deliberately subdued and darkened in the visual, redirecting attention to the woman and the romantic beach scene — leading the (1920s) female viewer step by step from an acceptable image into an unacceptable one she would otherwise never have identified with.

**Multi-image symbols** (a fine painting suggesting both success *and* cultural breeding simultaneously) broaden a market by grafting new emotional appeals onto its fringes, while also deepening emotional attraction among the existing core. Illustrated with a fully worked hypothetical: reinforcing piston rings (a neutral/mechanical product) by placing the replacement scene in a Mercedes SL-300 sports-car garage, with a mature, precise mechanic and a young, casual, wealthy-but-modest owner — building excitement and precision entirely through visual/situational choices rather than verbal claims, because "verbal terms...can be used to imply images of identification; but these are different types of images, and must be conveyed in a different way."

**Limits — the believability bridge:** many campaigns collapse by asking the market to identify with an unbelievable image (a deodorant-soap campaign using an elite society image for a mass market, which killed even the performance claims by association). Two governing questions:
1. Do people already believe the product has the target character trait, and can they identify their own lives with it? If yes to both, use the image directly.
2. If either answer is no, find another primary image to use as a believability bridge — inserting a first, acceptable image the prospect can identify with immediately, then hanging the target image onto that hook (Marlboro's virility concept as the accessible hook that then let men accept a yachting costume or opera hat in later executions).

**Saleable identifications from the physical product itself:** beyond functional performance, the physical product can be mined for image potential across three areas — its appearance, its components/structure, and its technical background. Appearance images can be borrowed from a product's own extreme/glamour variants (family sedans borrowing styling cues from racing and sports cars) or, for products with arbitrary physical form (liquids, powders), borrowed entirely from the product's background or components. Example: Johnson & Johnson's Micrin mouthwash, packaged in glass shapes borrowed directly from doctors' offices and operating rooms, instantly communicating "this is medicine, this works" through the container alone.

## 9. The Third Technique of Breakthrough Copy: Gradualization

**What belief is:** the prospect's mental picture of the world — what facts make it up, how it works — plus the emotional security and comfort derived from living in a world that "has meaning" and can be depended on. Most core beliefs were laid down in childhood; asking someone to shatter one is asking them to become a child again, and — barring being "frightened beyond belief" — they simply won't do it.

**The basic rule of belief:** if you violate the prospect's established beliefs even slightly, nothing you promise can save the ad. Conversely, if you channel the tremendous force of already-existing belief behind even one small claim, that one fully-believed claim will outsell all the half-questioned promises your competitors write for the rest of their days — powerfully enough to support even an otherwise-absurd claim. Advertising's job is not to change facts but to *extend* them — building a bridge of belief from what the prospect accepts today to what he must believe to accept your claim.

**Gradualization defined:** the process of starting an ad with a statement that will be immediately and entirely accepted, then building a chain of subsequent acceptances upon it, leading the reader to a goal conclusion he would not have accepted as readily without the preliminary statements. Unlike proof, testimonials, or documentation (which add content-strength), Gradualization is purely structural — it determines the *architecture* of the ad, not its content.

**Architecture of belief:** every claim, image, or proof has two separate sources of strength — (1) the content of the statement itself, and (2) the preparation made for it (either already existing in the prospect's mind or deliberately laid down earlier in the copy). You can therefore strengthen a statement either by increasing its content-intensity, or by repositioning it in the sequence so the groundwork already laid increases the intensity of belief given to it on arrival.

**A new definition of Awareness:** State of Awareness can be redefined as **readiness to accept**. This explains why the single most powerful claim does not always make the strongest headline — without supporting evidence already in the prospect's mind, he simply won't believe it, and will turn away rather than read on.

**Fully worked example — a 1951 Television Repair Manual ad.** The direct power-claim headline (*"Save up to $100 a Year on Your TV Repairs!"*) failed because the market first had to believe two unbelieved facts: that repairs *could* save money, and that the *average owner* was capable of making them. The revised ad built acceptance step by step:
- Headline *"WHY HAVEN'T TV OWNERS BEEN TOLD THESE FACTS?"* — no claim, just an implication of foul play that echoed existing suspicion; first acceptance.
- Inclusion question *"Was your set purchased after the spring of 1947?"* — a question whose form limits the market but whose content assures a Yes from ~95% of readers; second acceptance, building a Habit of Agreement.
- First definite promise, reinforced structurally by the grammatical bridge "Then…" (implying exclusion, hence credibility) and by describing near-universal symptoms before the save-money claim, while deliberately leaving the *mechanism* ambiguous (naming it too early would make the reader turn the page).
- A repeated "How many times…?" inclusion-question sequence, coaxing out a private stream of "Yes…Yes…Yes" agreement.
- Manufacturer "Breakdown Test" proof, laying the groundwork for **Goal Conclusion #1**: TV sets are not fragile.
- A transitional paragraph ("And, most important, these experts have discovered that you do not have to be a handyman…") introducing the entirely new idea — that the *owner* can fix minor issues — disguised via paragraph parallelism, tie-in phrases ("And," "most important"), and phrase-echo ("these experts have discovered") so it reads as a continuation rather than a new claim; this builds toward **Goal Conclusion #2**: the owner can correct minor breakdowns himself.
- The final **syllogism**: sets are dependable (1) + owners can fix minor adjustments (2) → therefore the owner should get this knowledge and save the money (3). Closing line: *"You pay a repairman — not for his work — but for his knowledge. If you had that knowledge yourself — then you would not have to pay him at all."*

**Devices that build believability structurally:**
1. **The Inclusion Question** — questions at the ad's start that let the prospect identify himself as part of the Yes-answer group before recommendations are made (*"ASK YOURSELF THESE NINE QUESTIONS…"*).
2. **Detailed Identification** — cataloguing specific symptoms/problems at the start so the reader knows "you have been there yourself," which requires accurate prior research or the ad's believability collapses.
3. **Contradiction of Present (False) Beliefs** — bluntly telling the reader his current belief is false, best paired with strong authority to earn the right to contradict (a plastic surgeon's cosmetics ad: *"From this moment on, forget everything you have ever heard or read about what age 'must do' to your appearance."*).
4. **The Language of Logic** — weaving in the vocabulary of reasoning ("the reason for," "simply because," "this has been proved by thousands," "and, most important of all, is the fact that") which, after centuries of conditioning in court, science, and fiction, carries an aura of conviction regardless of the content it's attached to.
5. **Syllogistic Thinking** — building formal, mechanical logical sequences (the spark-plug ad: bigger spark → more powerful explosion → more power from gasoline; poor explosion → wasted gas; good explosion → more miles per gallon) that create a feeling of inevitability — "the reader feels the product must work... he has been shown proof that it works."
6. **Other Belief Forms** — Contingency Structures ("If…then…"); Repetition of Proof/Echoing ("These experts found…these experts found…"); the Promise-Belief-Promise Variation (every promise sentence followed by a proof sentence, so the reader never has breathing space to question); Paragraph Parallelism (reusing an already-accepted sentence structure to borrow acceptance for a fresh claim).

## 10. The Fourth Technique of Breakthrough Copy: Redefinition

Every product carries some built-in resistance beyond the basic reluctance to pay money, falling into three categories of drawback: **too complicated**, **not important enough** (too narrow a market), and **costs too much**. Redefinition is the process of giving the product a new definition — saying it is *this* rather than *that* — to remove the roadblock, ideally before the prospect even consciously registers it exists.

The classic case: **Lifebuoy soap**, whose medicinal odor was an unremovable drawback. Rather than fight it, the copy redefined it as an asset via the "B.O." campaign — a "flip-flop" complete reversal, a "concept-judo" turning a liability directly into a proof of the soap's odor-destroying power.

**Three uses of Redefinition:**

1. **Simplification** — for the overcomplicated product. Illustrated via the two Television Repair Manual ads: the first failed by confronting the fear of "repairs" head-on; the second rebuilt belief first (per Section 9), then executed "the turn" — joining the two established worlds through the product without a jar — by rebranding "repairs" as "adjustments," "breakdowns" as "warning signals" or "symptoms," and by comparing the TV set to the human body (something familiar, giving off warning signs before serious illness). Three specific mechanics of this simplification: analogy to something commonplace (body/cold symptoms), relabeling the vocabulary itself (adjustments vs. repairs), and explicitly segregating true "repairs" into the least-likely-to-occur category. Also demonstrated via the Sherwin Cody English course, which redefined "good English" itself into a short, closed list of manageable facts (69 words = half of speech; a dozen punctuation principles; 25 typical grammar errors) — "the complicated becomes simple, the hard becomes easy," requiring only 15 minutes a day. The two tools for this category, used together: Redefine (this chapter), then Mechanize the new simplicity (next chapter).

2. **Escalation** — for the product that works and is easy enough to use but lacks a broad enough appeal. The task is to widen the product's importance/role in the prospect's life by redefining the benefits it delivers. Example: a 1960s English-course ad rede­fined "good English" from an end in itself (respectability) into a means to a more compelling end (persuasion power) — *"REVOLUTIONARY NEW WORD POWER MACHINE MAKES YOU A MASTER OF ENGLISH OVERNIGHT."* Also used to escalate a product's stakes ("You pay $2,000…$3,000…$4,000 for your car. And a single 99¢ part robs you of the real power…") or its frequency of relevance ("Your Entire Life is Spent Trying to Get Others to Do What You Want — Without Friction").

3. **Price Reduction** — for the product that costs too much relative to its category. The technique is to switch the comparison standard psychologically to a more expensive reference point rather than defend the premium directly. Fully dissected example: an SA Fire Injector spark plug at $1.49 (1.5x category price) redefined via a cascading sequence of bargain-phrases — "practically made by hand," "would have to sell for as high as $5 each," the doubled reference to $30–$40 (per plug, then per set), "get the price down so low," the payback-multiple reward framing ("pay for themselves 12 times in one year"), "astonishing proposition," the reciprocity-condition ("if you will…tell your friends"), "prepared to do," "You can have" (not "buy" — framed as being *let* have it at a favor-price), landing on "only $1.49 each" as the tenth reinforcing bargain-phrase in two short paragraphs.

## 11. The Fifth Technique of Breakthrough Copy: Mechanization

As the prospect reads copy, he makes three kinds of demands on it: (1) "Tell me more" — more information/image/desire; (2) "Oh yeah? Who says so?" — demand for proof; (3) "How does it work?" — demand for a **mechanism**. Mechanization answers the third: the verbal, logical demonstration that the product actually delivers what's promised — historically called **"Reason Why" copy** (Claude Hopkins was its master). The real question when sitting down to write is never *whether* to use mechanism, but **how much**.

**Three stages of Mechanization, driven by market sophistication:**

1. **Name the Mechanism.** When the prospect is already familiar with, and accepts, the way a mechanism works (because other advertisers already educated the market), simply name it and move on to compete on price or other features (*"TAKE FOOLPROOF PHOTOS WITH THE XENOPHON 1750 — With Electronic Light Setter…Push-Button Zomar Lens…Magazine Load…"*). Most catalog and retail copy needs only this level.

2. **Describe the Mechanism.** When the mechanism isn't well known, use the classic **Promise → Reason Why** one-two punch: build a strong quick promise, intensified over several paragraphs, then — right when the reader's internal reaction becomes "How?" — shift direction and sell the mechanism (illustrated via the 1926 Rinso ad: the whiteness promise is intensified for three paragraphs, then the copy pivots to "Dirt floats off — stains go. The secret is simply Rinso…"). Rule: mechanism copy must never become dry scientific discourse — "every word in good copy, including mechanism copy, sells" — it is selling a secondary claim that proves the primary one.

3. **Feature the Mechanism.** When the market is highly sophisticated, promises sound alike, and price competition turns suicidal, the mechanism itself becomes powerful/novel enough to belong in the headline (this connects directly to Third-Stage-of-Sophistication headlines from Section 3: *"FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY,"* *"FIRST WONDER DRUG FOR REDUCING,"* *"RUN YOUR CAR WITHOUT SPARK PLUGS,"* *"SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY"*). The rule of thumb: if people assume they know how it works (or the claim is new enough that they don't care), a word or phrase suffices; if they're unsure, describe it in selling language until belief is earned; if the mechanism is exceptionally strong or dramatic, or you want definite superiority over competitors, sell it hard (leading directly into Concentration, Section 12).

**On mechanizing a price cut specifically:** price cuts and product advantages are "only as good as your words, and your strategy, makes them" — an unexplained price cut generates no lift because nobody believes it. Robert Collier (credited as the great master of this) demonstrated building an elaborate, dramatized *reason* for a bargain (an overstocked mill's forced fire-sale of madras cloth due to unseasonable weather) rather than simply announcing the markdown — "a mechanism within a mechanism," building belief upon belief, and reaping roughly ten times the believability for the eventual bargain claim as a result of taking this "Creative Gamble" on holding the reader's attention through the setup.

## 12. The Sixth Technique of Breakthrough Copy: Concentration

No copy ever sells a product — it sells a way of satisfying a particular desire. Because commercial life is almost universally competitive, the book reviews five weapons for beating competition: (1) superiority of product (the ultimate weapon — "if your copy is better than your product, send it to your client instead of your prospect"), (2) superiority of promise (the entire book to this point), (3) product-role (Identification, Section 8), (4) response and reaction (escalating claims, shifting mechanisms, invading new markets — Section 3), and (5) **direct attack — Concentration**, the subject of this chapter.

Unlike the other four (which ignore competitors and act as though no alternative satisfaction exists), Concentration is most necessary when the advertising budget is smaller than a dominant competitor's, or when most of the prospect pool already belongs to that competitor — the first task becomes cracking his image and shattering loyalty before desire can be re-channeled.

**The governing rule of Concentration:** never attack a competitor's weakness unless you can simultaneously provide the solution to that exact weakness. Attacking without offering the cure reads as self-serving bias and produces only skepticism; attacking *while* proving your product removes the weakness reads as being "for his own good," and can shatter even deeply ingrained loyalty.

**Structure #1 — direct point-by-point contrast ("Bad — Good — Bad — Good…").** Fully dissected via the fire-injector spark-plug ad's "MECHANICS AND ENGINEERS READ THIS CAREFULLY" sequence: each stated weakness of ordinary spark plugs (jumping a spark across an air gap; the gap widening as the electrode burns; carbon accumulation; a thin skimpy spark that blows out under pressure; the need for premium gas) is immediately, structurally counteracted by the parallel strength of the injector (firing on the surface of a conductor; no air gap or burning electrode; self-cleaning; a heavy powerful flame; more mileage on regular gas) — reinforced by contrastive word choice (weak "spark" vs. strong "fire"/"flame"), parallel sentence structure, and closing documentation (a U.S. Air Force reference).

**Structure #2 — time-sequence contrast** ("What happens to you now, with the product you're using currently?" followed by "What will happen when you switch?"), used when the comparison can't be broken into discrete side-by-side points. Illustrated via a reducing-pill ad: the negative sequence redefines ordinary reducing plans as "completely passive," dependent on the dieter's own willpower, doing nothing "actively" — described through a common, recognizable failure narrative building agreement via "So what happened?" — culminating in "And then your will power snapped!...And the fat flowed back." This sets up the hero product (a "tiny grey pill," backed by a Nobel-prize-winner-discovered ingredient) to re-enter fully-primed Intensification copy with far greater impact than a cold opening could achieve.

A closing note: the same effect scales down to a single phrase or headline (*"SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY"* — implied competitive weakness plus compensating promise, in four words). Length isn't what makes Concentration effective; it is the emotional reaction produced — causing the reader to question a habit, shift loyalty, or take a chance on your product.

## 13. The Seventh Technique of Breakthrough Copy: Camouflage

A final, distinct mechanism for building believability: **borrowing** it from the reservoirs of trust already stored up elsewhere in society, rather than building it from within the copy itself. People choose a publication (or broadcast) because they believe it tells the truth, and some of that trust carries over unconsciously to its advertising pages — and, further, readers become conditioned to receive truth in that publication's particular phraseology, so that the phraseology itself begins to carry an aura of truth independent of content.

**Three ways to borrow this stored believability:**

1. **Format.** Match the ad's visual structure as closely as possible to the host medium's editorial look, minimizing the mental gear-shift from "editorial" to "advertisement." Fully dissected via a side-by-side comparison of the same "handling people" book ad in a generic, house-set magazine format versus a version rebuilt feature-for-feature to mimic The Wall Street Journal's own editorial conventions (old-fashioned upper-and-lowercase headline type instead of bold/modern/caps; the Journal's unusual stacked-subhead convention; a line drawing instead of a photograph, matching the paper's own editorial illustration style; subhead placement flush left, matching column style). The Journal-adapted version ran successfully nineteen consecutive months with no drop-off in pull — roughly double the pulling power and staying power of the same exact copy in generic mass-produced format.

2. **Phraseology.** Adopt the stereotyped phrasing conventions specific to a media class. Newspaper-style news openings (issue date, city of origin, byline) lend a report-like, non-sales atmosphere: *"SKIN SPECIALIST DEMONSTRATES HOW TO RINSE AWAY YOUR BLACKHEADS. By Claire Hoffman. New York, N.Y. — A leading doctor today showed…"*. In direct mail, mimic the format of correspondence that's opened without exception (refund checks, government correspondence, dividend notices, confidential reports). In radio/TV, adopt news-format sound and cadence.

3. **Mood (two named idioms for escaping the "hard-sell" stereotype):**
   - **Understatement** — simplicity, few adjectives, short falling sentences, self-deprecating humor, a refusal to take itself too seriously (the Volkswagen "You never run out of air" ad, told entirely in short, plain, almost list-form sentences about the absence of a radiator).
   - **Deadly Sincerity** — leaning over backwards to point out flaws in your own offer so that the benefits that follow are believed more deeply, precisely because ordinary advertising never criticizes its own product (*"TO THE MAN WHO WILL SETTLE FOR NOTHING LESS THAN THE PRESIDENCY OF HIS FIRM…This is a private advertisement. It is not meant for ninety-nine men out of every hundred."*).

## 14. The Final Touches — Weaving the Ad Together

Having broken the ad down into its component mechanisms (Intensification, Identification, Gradualization, Redefinition, Mechanization, Concentration, Camouflage), the final task is reassembly into one cohesive, forward-pulling unit.

**Verification — placement of proof.** Documentation (statistics, tests, testimonials, authorities, seals, awards) is most effective not by its content alone but by *where* it appears — proof works best when the reader is already, unconsciously, demanding it, and is ready to accept it as necessary and logical. Three related but distinct concepts, clearly separated:
- **Documentation** — any outside proof (statistics, facts, tests) that the product works.
- **Mechanization** — the verbal/logical demonstration that the product works, which does not require outside documentation at all; it proves itself through logic and structure alone.
- **Verification** — the *arrangement* of documentation within the copy to gain maximum immediate acceptance and emotional effect.
Illustrated via the fire-injector ad, where the U.S. Air Force reference is placed *after* the full Concentration sequence rather than up front, so it reinforces already-built believability rather than trying to create belief cold — "1 + 1 can often equal 10" through correct positioning.

**Reinforcement — how two claims do the work of four.** Emotional writing doesn't add mathematically; skillfully combined images can produce ten times the impact of either alone. Named example: Avis's *"We try harder"* combined with its reason why, *"We're only second"* — the joint impact of the two ideas together far exceeds what either produces separately. Also demonstrated via the "presidency of his firm" ad's juxtaposition of blunt flaw-admission ("This is John Horn's first book...it has flaws") directly against the positive claim that follows, which the admission makes far more believable by contrast.

**Interweaving — blending promise, logic, emotion, and image within the same sentence.** Credited to Walter S. Campbell's *Writing Non-Fiction*: even a purely factual sentence needs emotion and evaluation to carry meaning to the reader. Every copy sentence should contain as much of promise, logic, emotion, *and* image simultaneously as possible, packed so tightly the individual threads become nearly impossible to separate. Fully dissected examples from the fire-injector ad show six stacked disadvantage-images in a single "bad" sentence, five stacked promise-images in the parallel "good" sentence, and a single sentence weaving mood-builder ("And, most important") + authority-reference ("these experts") + scientific-idiom believability ("have discovered") + core promise ("do not have to be a handyman") + restated promise ("or a mechanic") + ease-promise ("coax") + condensed-claim promise ("this performance") all at once — done unconsciously by an experienced copywriter who has internalized the principle, not deliberately assembled piece by piece.

**Sensitivity — the overall architecture of the ad.** Beyond the sentence level, an ad has an overall structure: when and how many times it shifts between copy types (promise → mechanism → documentation → promise again). Recognizing shift points — the moment the reader mentally says "I've read enough about this, give me this instead" — and switching direction to meet the new demand is what makes an ad cohere rather than fall apart. Demonstrated via four fully mapped mail-order book ads on similar subjects, each labeled block-by-block (Promise, Definition, Documentation, transition sentences numbered in sequence) to show how differently the same underlying elements (promise, redefinition, authority, documentation, catalog of benefits) can be blended and sequenced across otherwise-similar products. The diagnostic questions to ask when reviewing a finished ad: is it straight promise? Too much documentation? Too little definition of the new possibilities the product creates? Could another brand name be substituted without changing anything? "A new structure is a new ad."

**Momentum — drawing the reader deeper into the copy.** Two types of momentum-builder:
1. **Momentum phrases** — time-honored transitional phrases inserted mainly at shift points to keep interest from flagging and to signal what's coming (*"Here's how,"* *"Let me explain,"* *"For example—,"* *"…to start with…,"* *"And yet it's only the beginning."*).
2. **Incomplete statements / teasers** — deliberately creating interest in a point, raising a question about it, and implying (without yet giving) the answer later in the copy, so the reader continues in order to resolve the open loop (*"a modern miracle of engineering science comes to life underneath your hood!"*; *"the fantastic Power-Booster that…HAD TO BE BANNED from the great European raceways—because it gave so much added power…"*). Every good example of this device does double duty — it builds momentum *and* sells at the same time.

**Mood — packing copy with emotion through word choice and rhythm.** Words and rhythm are "to the copy writer what line and color are to the painter" — they carry emotion so subtly the reader never notices where it's coming from. Two functional classes of words within any given passage: pure **image-sharpeners** (visual/sensory precision) and **emotion-definers** (words that tell the reader what to *feel* about the image, invisible by design — the moment a reader consciously notices them, the copy becomes "corny" or "questionable" and is lost). The rhythm of a passage should be read aloud and matched to its intended mood: staccato verb-claim-verb-claim rhythm builds a machine-gun sense of overwhelming momentum; the near-total absence of adjectives (as in Volkswagen copy) builds sincerity and self-deprecating trust; long, deliberately clumsy, unscannable sentences (as in the fire-injector ad) build a feeling of ingenuous, unpolished honesty rather than slick "copywriter" prose. Mood-building, like every other mechanism in the book, is deliberate on the copywriter's part and meant to remain entirely unnoticed by the reader.
