# Building a StoryBrand — Framework Reference

*Pure framework reference distilled from the book. No commentary, no application notes — just the frameworks, models, and mental tools as the author presents them.*

*Source: Building a StoryBrand by Donald Miller*

---

## 1. Why Most Marketing Fails — Noise, Survival, and the Confusion Mantra

Miller's starting diagnosis: most marketing dollars are wasted not because the product is bad but because the message describing it is unclear. Pretty design does not sell; words sell, and if the underlying message hasn't been clarified first, expensive design just amplifies noise.

Working with "Science Mike" McHargue, Miller frames this around how the brain filters information. The brain's overriding job is to help a person survive and thrive (echoing Maslow's hierarchy: physical survival, safety, social belonging/relationships, then meaning). It is constantly scanning incoming information — including ads — for anything relevant to that job, and discarding the rest without conscious effort (the "chairs vs. exits" example: nobody counts chairs in a room, but everyone unconsciously locates the exits).

Two named mistakes brands make that get them filtered out as noise:

- **Mistake One** — failing to focus on the aspects of the offer that help people survive and thrive (physically, emotionally, relationally, or spiritually). Talking about backstory, internal goals, or company history that doesn't connect to the customer's survival is "positioning yourself as the chairs, not the exits."
- **Mistake Two** — forcing customers to burn too many mental calories to understand the offer. Processing information costs the brain energy, and a survival mechanism exists specifically to tune out sources that make people work too hard to extract meaning.

**Story** is presented as the antidote: a "sense-making device" that organizes information (an ambition, the challenges opposing it, and a plan to overcome them) into a shape the brain doesn't have to labor to parse. Miller's music/noise analogy: music is noise submitted to structure, which is why it's remembered, while unstructured noise is forgotten — the same distinction separates a brand message that sticks from one that doesn't.

The StoryBrand mantra, repeated throughout the book as the organizing discipline behind every editorial decision: **"If you confuse, you'll lose."** Anything in a piece of marketing collateral that doesn't serve the customer's story has to be cut, the same way a screenwriter cuts any scene that doesn't serve the plot.

## 2. The SB7 Framework — Story in a Nutshell

The book's central model, built from analyzing the common structure beneath most successful stories (screenplays, novels, plays) across genres. Reduced to seven elements:

A **CHARACTER** who wants something encounters a **PROBLEM** before they can get it. At the peak of their despair, a **GUIDE** steps into their life, gives them a **PLAN**, and **CALLS THEM TO ACTION**. That action helps them avoid **FAILURE** and ends in a **SUCCESS**.

Mapped onto brand messaging, the seven parts become:

1. A Character (the customer)
2. Has a Problem
3. And Meets a Guide (the brand)
4. Who Gives Them a Plan
5. And Calls Them to Action
6. That Helps Them Avoid Failure
7. And Ends in a Success

This is called the **SB7 Framework**. The output of working through all seven parts for a business is a single-page document called the **StoryBrand BrandScript** (see Section 12).

**StoryBrand Principle: customers aren't looking for another hero; they're looking for a guide.** The single most load-bearing reframe in the book is that the customer is the hero of the story and the brand is never the hero — it plays the guide role (the Yoda, the Haymitch, the Q). A brand that positions itself as the hero is competing with the customer for the hero's own spotlight, and loses, because every human being already walks through life experiencing themselves as the protagonist of their own story.

The book illustrates the framework with recurring story maps (e.g., Katniss/Haymitch in *The Hunger Games*, Luke/Obi-Wan in *Star Wars*), each broken into: character, external problem, internal problem, philosophical problem, guide, plan, call to action, and both the failure and success outcomes — the same seven-part skeleton later formalized as the BrandScript.

## 3. The Three Crucial Questions and the Grunt Test

Two paired diagnostic tools for checking whether a story (or a piece of marketing) is clear enough to hold attention.

**The Three Crucial Questions** — at any point in a well-told story, an audience should be able to answer:

1. What does the hero want?
2. Who or what is opposing the hero getting what she wants?
3. What will the hero's life look like if she does (or does not) get what she wants?

If these three questions aren't answerable within the first fifteen to twenty minutes of a film, the story has already lost its audience. The same standard applies to a brand's marketing materials.

**The Grunt Test** — the standard for whether a website or piece of marketing collateral is clear enough to convert. The mental image: a caveman looking at your website should be able to grunt out answers, within about five seconds, to:

1. What do you offer?
2. How will it make my life better?
3. What do I need to do to buy it?

If a website can't pass the grunt test, it is losing sales regardless of how good the underlying product is.

## 4. A Character: Defining What the Customer Wants

The first module of the BrandScript. StoryBrand Principle: **the customer is the hero, not your brand.**

The task is to identify a single, specific thing the customer wants, and state it simply. Examples the book cites from client work: a resort defining the want as "the luxury and rest you've been looking for"; a university calling its offer "a hassle-free MBA you can complete after work"; a landscaping company positioning itself around "a yard that looks better than your neighbor's."

**Opening a story gap.** Naming a specific desire for the customer creates a "story gap" — a tension between the character and what they want that the audience (or customer) wants to see resolved. This is presented as the same mechanism that makes a musical phrase, a rhyming couplet, or hunger/appetite compelling: an opening creates a pull toward closure. Failing to define a clear customer desire means no story gap opens, and without an open gap there's no reason for the customer to keep engaging.

**Pare the ambition down to a single focus.** A brand that tries to name everything it can do for a customer dilutes the story gap the same way an amateur screenwriter dilutes a hero's motivation by giving them too many competing goals. At the top-level BrandScript, pick one simple, primary desire; more specific desires can be assigned to individual divisions or products in their own, subordinate BrandScripts.

**What "survival" means in this framework.** Miller uses "survival and thrive" broadly, covering categories such as:

- Conserving financial resources (e.g., Walmart's "Save Money. Live Better")
- Conserving time
- Building social networks / tribal belonging
- Gaining status (why luxury brands like Rolex or Mercedes sell an identity, not just a product)
- Accumulating resources (productivity, revenue, waste reduction — especially relevant to B2B)
- The innate desire to be generous (aspirational identity of sacrifice and helping others)
- The desire for meaning (citing Viktor Frankl against Freud: humans are driven more by meaning than pleasure)

The practical test for this module: would a random customer, if asked, be able to say exactly where your brand is trying to take them? If not, the story hasn't opened, and the brand is paying the cost of confusion.

## 5. Has a Problem: The Villain and the Three Levels of Conflict

StoryBrand Principle: businesses tend to sell solutions to external problems, but people buy solutions to internal problems.

**Every story needs a villain.** The villain is the device that gives conflict a concrete focal point and generates sympathy for the hero. Four rules for a usable villain in a BrandScript:

1. The villain should be a **root source** (a cause, not a feeling — high taxes is a villain; frustration is what a villain makes you feel).
2. The villain should be **relatable** — instantly recognizable as something the audience already dislikes.
3. The villain should be **singular** — one villain, not several, or the story loses focus.
4. The villain should be **real** — not manufactured fear-mongering.

**The Three Levels of Problems.** In any story, a villain creates an external problem, which produces an internal frustration, which is in turn philosophically wrong at a larger level. The same three levels operate in a customer's real decision to buy:

- **External problem** — the tangible, physical problem a product or service literally solves (a leaky pipe, hunger, termites, the need for a car). This is what most companies talk about, and it's the easiest to identify.
- **Internal problem** — the frustration, self-doubt, or emotional cost the external problem is causing the customer. Companies sell to this level far less often than they should, even though it is the deeper motivator. Examples from the book: Apple selling relief from tech intimidation rather than computing power; CarMax selling freedom from the fear of a pushy salesman rather than "a used car"; Starbucks selling sophistication and belonging rather than "a cup of coffee."
- **Philosophical problem** — the larger, "ought"/"shouldn't" question the story raises: why does this matter in a bigger sense than the immediate transaction? ("Bad people shouldn't be allowed to win," "People ought to be treated fairly.")

**The Perfect Brand Promise.** The strongest brand messaging resolves all three levels at once, the way a climactic scene in a film (e.g., Luke destroying the Death Star) resolves the external threat, the hero's internal self-doubt, and the philosophical stakes in a single moment. The book illustrates this with worked examples (villain / external / internal / philosophical) for brands like Tesla, Nespresso, and Edward Jones.

The problem module of the BrandScript asks four brainstorming questions: What is the villain? What external problem does that villain cause? What internal feeling does that external problem produce? What philosophical wrong does the brand stand against?

## 6. And Meets a Guide: Empathy and Authority

StoryBrand Principle Three: customers aren't looking for another hero; they're looking for a guide.

A brand becomes the guide in the customer's story (rather than a rival hero) by demonstrating two characteristics simultaneously:

- **Empathy** — showing an understanding of the customer's pain and frustration. Miller cites Oprah Winfrey's observation that people fundamentally want to be seen, heard, and understood, and Bill Clinton's "I feel your pain" as a political example of empathy establishing trust. Empathetic language patterns: "We understand how it feels to...", "Nobody should have to experience...", "Like you, we are frustrated by...". A sub-tactic here is signaling **commonality** ("are you like me?") — customers extend trust faster to brands they perceive as sharing their values or tastes, because the brain fills unknown gaps with trust once it recognizes similarity.
- **Authority** — demonstrating competence, without lapsing into bragging (which risks repositioning the brand as the hero). Four low-key ways to demonstrate authority:
  1. **Testimonials** — ideally around three, kept brief; more than that risks looking like self-promotion.
  2. **Statistics** — concrete numbers (customers served, money saved, growth percentages).
  3. **Awards** — small, unobtrusive mentions.
  4. **Logos** — for B2B, showcasing known businesses already served, as social proof.

Miller ties this pairing to Harvard researcher Amy Cuddy's finding (from *Presence*) that people meeting someone new subconsciously ask two questions in sequence: "Can I trust this person?" (answered by empathy) and "Can I respect this person?" (answered by authority/competence). Trust has to be established before respect is even considered — so empathy has to come first in the messaging, with authority following to seal it.

## 7. Who Gives Them a Plan: Process Plans and Agreement Plans

Once a guide has been established, the hero still needs a bridge to cross before acting — described metaphorically as "stones in the creek" that reduce the perceived risk of the jump. In the BrandScript, this bridge is called **the plan**, and it exists to remove one of two obstacles: confusion, or fear.

**Process Plan** — a short, numbered list of steps that clarifies exactly what the customer needs to do to buy the product, or what happens after they buy it (or both). Rules of thumb:

- At least three steps, no more than six; more should be grouped into phases.
- Can be a pre-purchase plan (e.g., "Measure your space → Order the items that fit → Install it in minutes"), a post-purchase plan (e.g., "Download the software → Integrate your database → Revolutionize your customer interaction"), or a plan spanning both.
- The goal is always to remove confusion, not to document every actual step — even if the real process has twenty or thirty steps, customers should be shown a simplified version of three to six.

**Agreement Plan** — a list of promises made to the customer specifically to reduce fear of doing business with the brand (distinct from the process plan, which reduces confusion about steps). Example: CarMax's four-point agreement addressing fear of haggling and fear of buying a lemon. An agreement plan can also double as a values statement (e.g., Whole Foods) that signals shared values with the customer, and it often works in the background rather than being featured prominently.

**Naming the plan.** Giving either type of plan a title (e.g., "the easy installation plan," "our quality guarantee") increases its perceived value by framing it as a distinct, brand-owned asset rather than a generic list of steps.

## 8. And Calls Them to Action: Direct and Transitional Calls to Action

StoryBrand observation: heroes (and customers) rarely act on major decisions unless challenged to by an external force. Passive, soft calls to action get ignored amid roughly three thousand daily commercial messages; bold, repeated calls to action get noticed. Underselling, not overselling, is described as the far more common failure mode among the thousands of businesses StoryBrand has worked with.

Two kinds of calls to action are recommended, used together, operating like two phases of a relationship (explicitly framed by Miller as "will you go out with me" versus "will you marry me"):

- **Direct call to action** — a request that leads straight to a sale, or the first step toward one: "Buy now," "Schedule an appointment," "Call today," "Register today." Should appear as one visually obvious element (larger, bolder, a distinct color), repeated consistently — top right of a website, center of the page above the fold, and repeated again as the page is scrolled.
- **Transitional call to action** — a lower-risk, usually free offer that "on-ramps" a customer who isn't ready to buy yet: downloading a PDF, watching a webinar, attending a free class, sampling a product. A good transitional call to action does three things:
  1. Stakes a claim to territory/expertise before a competitor does.
  2. Creates reciprocity (the more value freely given, the more the relationship is likely to be reciprocated later).
  3. Positions the brand as the guide by solving a smaller problem for free first.

Both kinds of calls to action should always be present together in marketing materials; neither replaces the other.

## 9. That Helps Them Avoid Failure: Stakes and Loss Aversion

StoryBrand principle: if a story has no stakes, it has no story. A hero who faces no possibility of failure generates no audience interest, and a brand that never names what the customer stands to lose by not engaging fails to answer the "so what" question every customer is implicitly asking.

**Loss aversion as the underlying mechanism.** The book grounds this in Daniel Kahneman's Prospect Theory (1979): people are more motivated to avoid a loss than to pursue an equivalent gain — in some situations two to three times more motivated. This is presented as the behavioral-economics reason negative stakes belong in marketing messaging, not just as a storytelling device.

**The four-step fear appeal**, drawn from Infante, Rancer, and Womack's *Building Communication Theory*, as a structure for introducing negative stakes without becoming a fearmonger:

1. Establish that the reader/listener is vulnerable to a specific threat.
2. Tell them that, because they're vulnerable, they should act to reduce that vulnerability.
3. Offer a specific action that protects them from the risk.
4. Directly challenge them to take that action.

**Fear is salt in the recipe.** A moderate amount of negative-stakes messaging is most persuasive; too little leaves the story flat (no urgency), too much triggers a defensive shutdown (audiences "block out" high-fear messaging rather than acting on it). The prescribed dose in the BrandScript's failure module is deliberately small — just a few bullet points naming concrete negative consequences of not engaging (e.g., a financial advisor listing "confusion about how your money is being invested," "not being ready for retirement," "hidden fees").

## 10. And Ends in a Success: The Vision and the Three Story Resolutions

The final module of the SB7 Framework: defining, specifically and vividly, what the customer's life looks like after their problem is resolved. The instruction is to be as concrete as Kennedy's "we're going to put a man on the moon" rather than vague ("a highly competitive space program") — specificity is what makes a vision motivating.

**The Before/After tool** (credited to Ryan Deiss of DigitalMarketer): a grid used to imagine, in concrete terms, how the customer's life, feelings, average day, and status change from before to after engaging the brand — used as a brainstorming aid for populating the success module.

**Three dominant ways stories resolve**, identified as the recurring patterns storytellers use to give an audience a satisfying ending, and treated as three separate psychological needs a brand's success message can tap into:

1. **Winning power or position (status).** People want to be more esteemed, respected, and socially secure. Brand tactics that offer status: offering access (loyalty tiers), creating scarcity (limited editions), offering a premium tier (naming your best customers "Preferred" or "Diamond"), and offering identity association (luxury brands selling refinement and success alongside the physical product).
2. **Union that makes the hero whole (completeness via something external).** The classic "lovers unite" ending taps a deeper need to be completed by something outside oneself — not necessarily romantic. Brand tactics: reducing anxiety (positioning the product as the source of relief and closure), reducing workload (tools that make the customer "superhuman"), and offering more time (any product that expands a customer's functional time).
3. **Ultimate self-realization or self-acceptance.** The hero reaches their potential or simply accepts themselves as they are, resolving an inner journey rather than an external lack. Brand tactics: inspiration (association with athletic or intellectual achievement, e.g., Red Bull, Under Armour), acceptance (helping people accept themselves as they are, e.g., Dove's Real Beauty films, American Eagle's unretouched Aerie campaign), and transcendence (inviting customers into a larger movement, e.g., Tom's Shoes' "one for one" model, FUBU's "For Us By Us").

The overarching instruction: the success module should simply be a bulleted list of resolutions to the customer's external, internal, and philosophical problems — kept simple, with more space allotted to this module than to the failure module (positive stakes should outweigh negative stakes in volume).

## 11. The Aspirational Identity: Participating in Customer Transformation

A distinct concept from the "success" ending — this module answers not "where does the story end up" but "who does the hero become."

Heroes are, by story convention, flawed and doubtful at the outset and transformed by having gone through the conflict; the same arc (self-doubt → courage → competence) recurs across nearly every popular story because self-doubt is close to universal in real audiences.

**Defining an aspirational identity** means identifying who the customer wants to become through engaging the brand — illustrated with Gerber Knives (customer identity: tough, adventurous, capable — "Hello, Trouble") and Dave Ramsey's Financial Peace University (identity: someone who is debt-free, financially disciplined, and in control of their life, dramatized through the on-air "Debt-Free Scream").

**The diagnostic question**: how does the customer want to be described by their friends? The answer supplies the language for the "from" (the deficient starting identity) and "to" (the aspirational identity) lines of the BrandScript — e.g., a financial advisor's customer moving "from confused and ill-equipped" to "competent and smart"; a shampoo brand's customer moving "from anxious and glum" to "carefree and radiant."

**Affirming the transformation.** Stories consistently include a scene where the guide explicitly tells the hero they have changed (Obi-Wan's ghost affirming Luke, Lionel telling King George he will be a great king) because heroes, still full of residual self-doubt, often can't see their own transformation without someone else naming it. The implication for brands: don't just deliver the transformation, explicitly narrate it back to the customer.

## 12. The StoryBrand BrandScript

The BrandScript is the single-page tool that operationalizes the SB7 Framework: it is not the finished marketing copy itself, but the underlying grid of short, decided-upon statements — one per SB7 element — used afterward to generate all actual marketing material (websites, keynotes, elevator pitches, sales letters).

Working method recommended for filling it out, repeated at the end of each SB7 chapter:

1. Read/understand the given part of the framework.
2. Brainstorm several possible messages for that section, alone or with a team.
3. Make a decision — commit to a single specific message per module rather than keeping the field vague or multi-option.

The seven modules on the BrandScript correspond exactly to the seven SB7 elements: A Character (what they want) / Has a Problem (villain, external, internal, philosophical) / And Meets a Guide (empathy, authority) / Who Gives Them a Plan (process, agreement) / And Calls Them to Action (direct, transitional) / That Helps Them Avoid Failure / And Ends in a Success — plus the aspirational-identity "from/to" transformation lines.

**Diverse brands and multiple BrandScripts.** A single overarching BrandScript should carry one unifying customer desire for the whole brand; individual divisions or products can each have their own subordinate BrandScript addressing a more specific want, nested underneath the top-level one, rather than trying to cram every possible value proposition into a single script.

**Internal use of the BrandScript.** The same tool can be run a second time with the roles reassigned: the internal team as the hero and company leadership as the guide, to define the employee value proposition (compensation, development, and culture as "tools" leadership gives employees to help them win) — distinct from, but structurally identical to, the external customer-facing BrandScript.

## 13. Building a Better Website: The Five Things It Should Include

A website is treated as the highest-leverage single piece of collateral to run through the BrandScript filter — often the first real "date" a prospective customer has with the brand. The standard: it should function like an elevator pitch and pass the grunt test (Section 3).

Five required elements, based on patterns across thousands of reviewed client websites:

1. **An offer above the fold.** The very first thing visible (before any scrolling) must let the visitor know, in one bold, short statement, what's in it for them. It should meet at least one of these criteria: promise an aspirational identity, promise to solve a problem, or simply state exactly what the company does. Anything else (backstory, mission-statement language, inside jokes) burns the visitor's limited attention before the offer is even understood.
2. **Obvious calls to action.** A direct call to action ("Buy Now," etc.) belongs in two consistent locations: top right of the page, and center-page above the fold — repeated again as the visitor scrolls, following the natural Z-pattern eye movement across a page.
3. **Images of success.** Photography should favor smiling, satisfied people (ideally using the product) over sterile shots of buildings or facilities, because images of resolved story loops communicate an emotional destination the visitor wants to reach.
4. **A bite-sized breakdown of revenue streams**, for businesses with more than one product or division: find one umbrella message that unifies the divisions, feature it above the fold, then let visitors self-select into sub-pages (each potentially with its own BrandScript) rather than forcing every offer into one undifferentiated page.
5. **Very few words.** Visitors scan, they don't read; the recommended internal shorthand is to "write it in Morse code" — brief, punchy, relevant copy, with long explanations collapsed behind a "read more" link rather than displayed in full paragraphs. Some of the most effective reviewed sites used ten sentences or fewer on the entire page.

The overarching discipline: every word, image, or idea on the site should trace back to a category in the BrandScript — anything that doesn't is noise.

## 14. The One-Liner Formula

A one-liner is a short, memorable, repeatable answer to "what do you do?" — modeled explicitly on a Hollywood **logline** (the one-sentence pitch used to sell a screenplay), which works because it combines imagination (the listener can picture the story) with intrigue (they want to know more).

Built from a compressed, four-part version of the BrandScript:

1. **The Character** — who is the customer? (should trigger a "that's me" reaction)
2. **The Problem** — what specific struggle do they face?
3. **The Plan** — what, briefly, does the brand do about it?
4. **The Success** — what does life look like once the problem is solved?

Worked example given in the book: a Pilates studio targeting busy mothers —

- Character: busy moms
- Problem: no time to work out
- Plan: short, meaningful weekly workouts
- Success: health and renewed energy
- Resulting one-liner: "We provide busy moms with a short, meaningful workout they can use to stay healthy and have renewed energy."

Recommended uses once drafted: memorize it personally, have the entire team memorize it (turning every employee into a de facto sales force), feature it on the website, and repeat it across every piece of collateral (email signatures, business cards, packaging, social bios) — treated like a musician's "hit song," meant to be performed with fresh energy on every repetition rather than varied or retired.

## 15. Using StoryBrand to Transform Company Culture: The Narrative Void and the Thoughtmosphere

A structural problem named the **Narrative Void**: the vacuum that opens inside an organization when there is no unifying story connecting its parts. Left unaddressed, different divisions (leadership development, PR, career development, onboarding, local marketing, corporate communication/advertising) drift into disconnected, self-referential activity — symbolized in the book by a diagram of an organization with a mission statement at the center surrounded by disengaged, siloed functions (a "boring mission statement," "weak middle management," "dead-end jobs," "rogue messaging," and so on radiating outward).

The book argues a written mission **statement** is not the same thing as an operating mission, and cites Gallup research (2012) estimating employee disengagement was costing the U.S. economy $450–550 billion a year, tying that cost partly to an information-overload environment (thousands of daily marketing and non-marketing messages) that has made internal communication weaker even as external noise has grown.

**Thoughtmosphere** — a term (credited to StoryBrand's Ben Ortlip) for the invisible mixture of beliefs and ideas driving employee behavior and performance inside an organization. A thoughtmosphere improves once a StoryBrand-based narrative is created, translated into concrete talking points, and reinforced through a deliberate plan of execution (video curriculum, regional meetings, national conventions, CEO updates, and similar channels) so every stakeholder understands their specific role in the larger story.

**Being "on mission"** is distinguished from merely having a mission statement: it means every department strategy, operational detail, and customer experience is calibrated around the same shared narrative. The book's illustrative case (an unnamed billion-dollar restaurant chain) reports growth accelerating from roughly 5 percent to nearly 30 percent within three years of this kind of narrative realignment, using the same people already inside the organization.

**Dual BrandScripts inside a culture engagement**: the external, customer-facing BrandScript drives the customer value proposition; a parallel, internally-facing BrandScript (team as hero, leadership as guide) drives the employee value proposition — see Section 12.

## 16. The StoryBrand Marketing Roadmap: Lead Generators, Drip Campaigns, Transformation Stories, and Referral Systems

Presented as the practical follow-up sequence once a BrandScript exists and the website has been rebuilt around it (Sections 12–14). Framed as a "getting started" checklist of near-free tactics, in order:

**1. Create a lead generator and collect email addresses.** A vague "join our newsletter" offer fails because it promises no specific value; a **lead generator** (the same thing elsewhere called a transitional call to action) must (a) provide real, substantial value and (b) establish the brand's authority. Five recommended formats: a downloadable guide/PDF, an online course or webinar, a software demo or free trial, free samples, or a live event. Guidance on volume: give away generously (the book's own experience is that generosity has never cost it customers); a downloadable PDF should be short (around three pages) but dense with value; email-list thresholds for "getting started" scale with business size — as few as roughly 250 qualified addresses can be enough for a business under $5 million in revenue.

**2. Create an automated email drip campaign.** Once addresses are collected, a pre-written sequence ("nurturing campaign") should trigger automatically. A typical cadence: three nurturing emails followed by one sales email with a direct call to action, repeating on a cycle. Formula for a **nurturing email**: (1) talk about a problem, (2) explain a plan to solve it, (3) describe what life looks like once it's solved — plus a P.S., since the postscript is disproportionately read even by people who skim past the body. Formula for an **offer/call-to-action email**: (1) talk about a problem, (2) describe the product that solves it, (3) describe the resulting life, (4) issue a direct call to action. A roughly 20 percent open rate is treated as an acceptable industry baseline, and even unopened, undeleted emails are treated as having done useful branding work simply by keeping the logo in front of the recipient; unsubscribes are treated as a healthy filtering mechanism rather than a loss.

**3. Collect and tell stories of transformation.** Because transformation of the hero is the deepest structural driver of story engagement (Sections 10–11), the same principle should be used to generate testimonials that actually show transformation rather than generic praise. A five-question interview structure elicits a transformation-shaped testimonial:

- What was the problem you were having before you discovered our product?
- What did the frustration feel like as you tried to solve that problem?
- What was different about our product?
- Take us to the moment you realized our product was actually working.
- What does life look like now that the problem is solved (or being solved)?

**4. Create a system that generates referrals.** Referrals and peer recommendations are cited (via American Marketing Association research) as up to 2.5 times more responsive than other marketing channels, yet most businesses that say they grow "by word of mouth" have no actual system generating it. A three-part referral system:

- **Identify existing, ideal customers** and build a way to communicate with them differently than with new prospects (the book's example: Domino's building customer profiles it can prompt at high-likelihood moments).
- **Give customers something concrete to pass along** rather than simply asking them to hand over friends' contact information (which feels transactional) — e.g., a short educational video or PDF they can forward, paired with an email framing the ask around helping the friend, not extracting a lead.
- **Offer a reward or affiliate structure** (discounts, free product, or an affiliate commission, e.g., roughly 10 percent on referred orders) to incentivize the referral directly, automated through the same email system used for the drip campaign (Task 2), with a cutoff so repeat customers aren't pitched the referral offer indefinitely.

Illustrative real-world referral mechanics cited in the book: a test-prep academy's "100 percent refund for three referrals in a semester," a golf range's invite-a-friend coupons for free lesson balls, a contractor's post-project open-house party, and a wedding photographer's free anniversary portrait in exchange for referrals at the time of booking.

The five roadmap tasks (one-liner — Section 14 — plus these four) are explicitly framed as a repeatable checklist: execute all five following a rebuilt, BrandScript-driven website, and expect compounding rather than one-time results.
