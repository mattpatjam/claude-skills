# Ogilvy on Advertising — Framework Reference

*Pure framework reference distilled from the book. No commentary, no application notes — just the frameworks, models, and mental tools, explained in original language.*

*Source: Ogilvy on Advertising by David Ogilvy*

---

## 1. Advertising as Salesmanship, Not Art

Ogilvy's foundational claim: advertising is not an entertainment medium or an art form, it is a medium of information whose only job is to sell. He judges his own work by one test: did it make people buy the product, not whether anyone called it "creative." He traces this back to a formative distinction from ancient Greece — when Aeschines spoke, the crowd said "how well he speaks"; when Demosthenes spoke, they said "let us march." The second reaction, not the first, is the standard for advertising.

He rejects the idea that he imposes "rules." What he does is report on how consumers actually react to different stimuli, based on accumulated research. A finding that celebrity endorsements test below average, or that black type on white ground reads better than the reverse, is not dogma — it's an observed regularity, offered as a hint rather than a commandment.

A companion principle: not all advertising helps the client — some campaigns have been shown, on measurement, to actually *reduce* sales because the message worked against the product. There is a tacit industry-wide temptation to assume all advertising is beneficial; Ogilvy insists this is a myth, and that agencies and clients should acid-test their advertising against real sales results rather than assume goodwill.

He also draws a direct line between preparation and results: his most successful campaigns (Rolls-Royce, Mercedes-Benz, Good Luck margarine, Shell gasoline) all followed weeks of immersing himself in facts about the product before writing a word — the "electric clock" Rolls-Royce headline and the Shell Platformate story both came from this kind of homework, not from stylistic inspiration.

## 2. Repeat Your Winners, and Other Working Rules

A cluster of practical operating rules Ogilvy returns to across the book:

**Repeat your winners.** If an advertisement works, keep running it until research shows it has genuinely stopped selling — don't retire it just because internal audiences (clients, executives) are bored of seeing it. Readership of a repeated advertisement does not decline meaningfully across several insertions in the same publication. The reasoning: you are not advertising to a "standing army" of the same eyes each time, but to a "moving parade" of new prospects entering the market — a good advertisement functions like a radar sweep, constantly finding new buyers as they arrive, so it should keep running as long as the sweep still finds people.

**Down with committees.** Committees can criticize but cannot create. Most weak campaigns are the product of trying to reconcile too many stakeholders' divergent objectives into one execution, which produces something that tries to do everything and therefore sells nothing distinctly.

**Codify what works.** Ogilvy positions himself in a lineage (starting with George Gallup's research at Young & Rubicam in the 1930s) of advertising people who systematically accumulate "plus and minus factors" — techniques that reliably out- or under-perform — rather than relying on personal intuition or one-off inspiration. He treats the failure of most agencies to build and retain this kind of institutional knowledge as a major, avoidable weakness of the industry.

**Learn from direct response.** Because direct-response advertisers (mail order, coupon response) can measure exactly how many sales each advertisement produces, their observed practices are more trustworthy guides to what works than the habits of general advertisers, who rarely know if their advertising sold anything. Where the two traditions disagree — copy length, commercial length, time slots — Ogilvy consistently sides with the direct-response evidence.

**"Good" advertising vs. "liked" advertising.** These are not the same thing. Ogilvy warns readers not to judge advertisements by which ones they personally find charming, witty, or beautifully illustrated — that instinct systematically favors executions that fail to ask "would this make me buy the product?"

**The cult of "creativity."** Ogilvy is suspicious of the industry's obsession with the word "creative" and with "originality" for its own sake, quoting Rosser Reeves' warning that originality is "the most dangerous word in advertising" because it seduces copywriters into producing swamp-fire novelty rather than sales. An agency's own house rule he cites approvingly: "if it doesn't sell, it isn't creative."

## 3. The Big Idea

Ogilvy holds that homework alone never wins fame or fortune — success also requires inventing "big ideas" that cut through and make people notice and act. He estimates that only about one campaign in a hundred contains a genuine big idea, and that in his own long career he personally originated no more than twenty.

Big ideas, in his account, come from the unconscious rather than from conscious effort. His method: saturate the conscious mind with facts about the product, then deliberately "unhook" rational thought — through a long walk, a hot bath, a drink — and wait for the idea to surface. He illustrates this with his own experience developing the recurring horse-and-wagon imagery for Pepperidge Farm bread, which arrived to him fully formed in a dream.

He offers a five-question test for recognizing a big idea when you have one:
- Did it make me gasp when I first saw it?
- Do I wish I had thought of it myself?
- Is it unique?
- Does it fit the strategy perfectly?
- Could it be used for thirty years?

That last criterion is the hardest and most important: he points to campaigns that ran for decades (a shirt-brand eyepatch motif running over 30 years, a skin-cream product's soap-comparison claim running continuously since the mid-1950s, a financial firm's "do you know me?" celebrity-recognition format, a long-running cigarette-brand campaign) as proof that the rare truly big idea can be renewed indefinitely rather than wearing out on a normal advertising timetable.

## 4. Brand Image

Every advertisement should be understood as a contribution to a cumulative brand image, and that image should stay consistent year after year — a discipline made difficult in practice by staff turnover, new agencies, and incoming marketing directors who each want to leave their own mark.

For most products with a visible brand identity — the ones people effectively "wear" in front of others, like beer, cigarettes, or cars — it pays to build an image of quality, "a first class ticket." Ogilvy's argument is that consumers often cannot and do not meaningfully distinguish between competing products on taste or performance alone (he cites psychology research in which people rated identical distilled water as tasteless or "horrible" purely based on whether they were told it came from a bottle or a tap, and in which identical whiskey was preferred or rejected purely based on which brand name it was attached to). In categories like this, the brand image *is* effectively most of what is being sold — consumers are "tasting images," not products.

Because of this, sloppy, cheap-looking advertising damages the product itself by association: nobody wants to be seen using a shoddily-marketed product. Ogilvy's own long-running use of a single recognizable spokesperson as the face of a client's product (his tonic-water client campaign) is offered as an extended case study in deliberately building and protecting a stable, recognizable brand image on a modest budget over many years.

## 5. Headline Rules

Roughly five times as many people read a headline as read the body copy that follows it — meaning that if the headline fails to sell, the great majority of the money spent on the advertisement has been wasted before the reader ever reaches the copy.

Rules distilled from readership research (primarily Gallup, Starch, and Ogilvy's own factor-analysis work):

- **Promise a benefit.** Headlines that promise the reader something concrete — value, results, an improvement — consistently outperform headlines that don't.
- **Include news.** Headlines announcing a new product, an improvement to an old one, or a new use for an existing product are recalled by a large margin more than headlines without news content. If you have real news, put it in the headline — don't bury it in body copy that most readers will never reach.
- **Avoid "blind" headlines.** Headlines that withhold the product name or benefit score meaningfully below average on recall.
- **Never run without a headline at all.** A "headless" advertisement is described as the single silliest mistake available, since the headline is doing most of the persuasive work.
- **Use proven high-pull words** like *amazing, introducing, now, suddenly* rather than avoiding them out of some misplaced sense of originality — readers move through a page like a jungle, and the headline's job is to telegraph the message instantly.

## 6. Body Copy Rules

Average magazine-ad body-copy readership is only around 5 percent of the audience, but Ogilvy points out that even a "small" percentage of a mass-circulation publication is a very large number of real readers — so body copy is worth getting right, not skipping.

Core rules:

- **Write to one person, not a stadium.** A reader encounters the copy alone. Address them the way Disraeli was said to speak to Queen Victoria — as one individual to another — not the way Gladstone was said to lecture her as if addressing a public meeting.
- **Be specific, not essayistic.** Tell the reader exactly what the product will do for them, in concrete terms, rather than composing a general-interest essay about the category.
- **Use simple words and short sentences.** Ogilvy learned through direct experience that ordinary readers often don't know words a copywriter assumes are common (he had to change "obsolete" to "old-fashioned" in a soap campaign after discovering many housewives didn't know the first word).
- **Tell it as a story.** Copy structured as a narrative outperforms copy structured as a list of claims — he cites the classic direct-response headline formula "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano" as the model.
- **Avoid analogies.** Research shows readers frequently fail to complete the intended comparison and instead take the analogy literally or become confused by it.
- **Avoid "brag and boast."** Superlative claims ("the best in the world") with no substantiation are a recognized weak pattern that convinces nobody.
- **Use testimonials.** Genuine, specific testimonials from real users are more credible than generic copywriter claims, because readers assume the copywriter is paid to be biased.
- **Long copy, well written, outsells short copy** for most products — a point covered in depth in Section 9 below.

## 7. Layout and Illustration Rules

Ogilvy's design philosophy is summarized in his own acronym, aimed squarely at art directors who chase abstract "movement" and "balance": KISS — Keep It Simple, Stupid.

Layout sequencing rule: readers scan a page from top to bottom, looking first at the illustration, then the headline, then the copy. Layouts should follow this order — illustration on top, headline directly under it, copy under the headline — because reversing it (headline above the illustration) asks readers to scan against their natural habit. Research found headlines placed below the illustration are read by roughly 10 percent more people than headlines placed above it, even though a majority of advertisements in practice put the headline on top.

Illustration rules drawn from readership research:

- Keep illustrations simple, with a single focal person — crowd scenes underperform.
- Avoid showing human faces larger than life size; it tends to repel readers rather than attract them.
- Historical subjects bore the majority of readers.
- Don't assume a subject that interests *you* will interest the target consumer — Ogilvy's own experiment using chefs (his former profession) in food advertising tested poorly with housewives, the actual target audience.
- Always caption photographs — captions are read by more people than body copy, so every caption should carry the brand name and the promise, functioning as a mini-advertisement.
- Photographs of a finished dish outperform photographs of raw ingredients in food advertising.
- Four-color advertisements cost about 50 percent more than black-and-white but are roughly 100 percent more memorable — a good trade in most cases.
- When showing food, "food in motion" (sauce being poured, syrup dripping) tests as more appetizing than a static shot.

Typography rules:

- Use typefaces readers are already accustomed to (the Century family, Caslon, Baskerville, Jenson); unfamiliar or highly stylized typefaces are harder to read, and the drama of an advertisement belongs in the message, not the typeface.
- Serif type outperforms sanserif for body copy; heavy sanserif use in long text is compared to reading through wax paper.
- Never set copy in reverse (white type on black) — it substantially reduces readership, with rare legitimate exceptions like text meant to be read in a darkened theater.
- Never end a headline with a period — full stops arrest the reader's momentum.
- Set body copy at a column width readers are used to (roughly 40 characters), matching normal newspaper measure.
- For long copy: a two-line subhead between headline and body increases appetite for reading on; starting the body copy with a drop-initial increases readership by roughly 13 percent on average; keep the opening paragraph to about 11 words or fewer; break up long copy with cross-heads every couple of inches, written so a skimmer moving only through the cross-heads still gets the core sales story.

## 8. Long Copy vs. Short Copy

Contrary to general assumption, Ogilvy holds that for most products long copy outsells short copy — a conclusion he says he failed to disprove more than twice in his own career (once on a mass-market cigar, once on a premium whiskey). He backs this with a series of real campaigns running from 800 words to well over 3,000 words that performed strongly for banks, oil companies, and financial-services clients, and points to split-run direct-response tests as the cleanest proof: when advertisers can measure exact response rates, long copy invariably wins.

The caveat: long copy only works if it's written well enough to be read. The critical failure point is the opening — a flat, generic first sentence ("going on vacation is a pleasure everyone looks forward to") loses the reader immediately, whereas a sentence that "takes the reader by the throat" (he cites a Harvard lecturer's opening line about Cesare Borgia) holds attention long enough for the rest of the copy to work.

He also notes a secondary finding: the number of concrete, pertinent facts included in an advertisement correlates with how many people can be persuaded to act on it — long copy tends to win specifically because it can carry more real information, not because length itself is persuasive.

## 9. Poster Advertising

Posters need to deliver the selling promise both visually and verbally, in the largest legible type, with the brand name visible from a long distance, using strong pure colors. Ogilvy's rule of thumb: never use more than three design elements in a poster.

He borrows the phrase "visual scandal" (from designer Savignac) to describe the ideal poster — something arresting enough to stop a passerby — while warning that overdoing the "scandal" is a literal traffic-safety hazard, since posters are read by people in fast-moving vehicles. He cites a long-running series of British beer posters from the 1930s as the standard-setting example of a poster campaign that became part of the culture rather than simply being noticed and forgotten.

## 10. Television — What Works (Above-Average Techniques)

Ogilvy catalogs, from factor-analysis research, categories of TV commercial that test above average in changing brand preference:

- **Humor**, which conventional wisdom (and Claude Hopkins, in particular) long held could not sell, but which later research showed can work when executed well — though very few writers can write comedy that is actually funny, and the technique should be avoided by anyone who isn't sure they can.
- **Slice of life** — a staged dramatized argument in which a skeptic is converted to the product. Widely disliked by copywriters as corny, but reliably effective when done with genuine charm.
- **Testimonials**, especially from real, unrehearsed-seeming users caught by a hidden camera defending the product against a planted skeptic — the more amateurish and unpolished the performance, the more credible it reads.
- **Demonstrations** of the product's real performance, which need not be dull — Ogilvy cites demonstrations built around dramatic stunts (a paper-board bridge holding a truck, a glue strong enough to suspend a person by their shoes) as proof that a demonstration can be both rigorous and theatrical.
- **Problem-solution format**, showing a relatable problem and then the product solving it.
- **Talking heads** — a single presenter directly extolling the product. Dismissed by agency creative staff as uncreative, but shown by research to outperform on changing brand preference, especially for launching new products.
- **Continuing characters** who become the living symbol of a brand over years (he cites a long-running fictional baker character for a bread brand, and a fictional coffee-brand spokeswoman, both running for many years).
- **"Reason why" copy**, explaining a rational mechanism for why the product works (freeze-drying as the reason an instant coffee tastes better, for instance).
- **News**, exactly as with print — products attract the most attention when something about them is genuinely new.
- **Emotion** — nostalgia, charm, sentimentality — which Ogilvy came to believe can be enormously effective even though researchers cannot yet quantify it well. He notes that consumers still need a rational-sounding excuse to justify an emotional purchase decision, so it pays to include one even in an emotion-led execution.

## 11. Television — What Doesn't Work, and Sixteen Practical Tips

Categories found to test *below* average:

- **Celebrity testimonials.** Viewers correctly guess the celebrity was paid, and tend to remember the celebrity while forgetting the product entirely — Ogilvy cites his own use of Eleanor Roosevelt in a margarine commercial as a personal example of this mistake.
- **Jingles.** Below average in changing brand preference; if used at all, they must be tested for intelligibility with people who haven't already read the script, since inaudible lyrics are common.
- **Frequent scene changes.** Commercials with many short scenes tend to score below average, because most viewers (and most writers) can't hold together that much visual information without losing the thread.

Ogilvy's sixteen practical production tips for commercials, condensed:

1. Establish brand identification early (within the first ten seconds) and repeat it — viewers frequently remember a commercial while misattributing it to a competing brand.
2. Show the package at the end; this measurably improves brand-preference change.
3. For food, show "food in motion" — the same principle as in print.
4. Use close-ups when the product itself is the hero of the commercial.
5. Open with the fire, not a slow build — grab attention in the first frame, because a dull opening gives an uninterested viewer permission to disengage before the payoff arrives.
6. Music and jingles are, at best, neutral for selling — they don't help and they don't hurt, in the way that a good sound effect can.
7. Sound effects tied to the product (coffee percolating, sausages sizzling) can add real persuasive power.
8. On-camera dialogue outperforms voice-over in holding an audience.
9. Superimposed text ("supers") reinforcing the spoken promise increases sales, provided the on-screen words match the spoken words exactly — any divergence confuses viewers.
10. Avoid visual banality — show the viewer something they haven't already seen a thousand times (sunsets, happy family dinners) or they will mentally tune out amid the roughly 30,000 commercials the average household is exposed to annually.
11. Limit changes of scene.
12. Use a consistent visual "mnemonic" device repeated across a campaign's life to build identification (he cites a car driving through a paper barrier as a running device for a gasoline brand).
13. Show the product in actual use and, where possible, the end result of that use.
14. Remember that anything is technically possible on television — the only limit is imagination, not production capability.
15. Miscomprehension is rampant: a 1979 academic study found that all 25 commercials studied were misunderstood by a meaningful share of viewers (from 19 to 40 percent) — so clarity has to be actively engineered, not assumed.
16. Production costs are wildly inflated relative to necessity — commercials often cost roughly 500 times more per second to produce than the television programming surrounding them, frequently due to unnecessary location shoots, custom music, excess actors, and needless celebrity casting; Ogilvy suspects (without hard proof) a negative correlation between production spend and actual selling power.

## 12. Radio Advertising

Ogilvy calls radio the "Cinderella medium" — reduced to background noise for most listeners, chronically under-researched, and receiving a shrinking share of total ad spend. Because there is little rigorous measurement of what makes radio commercials work, he offers four provisional findings from his own pilot research:

- Identify your brand early in the commercial.
- Identify it often.
- Promise the listener a benefit early.
- Repeat that promise often.

He notes that the great majority of radio commercials do none of these four things. His broader recommendation: since radio competes for attention against a listener's other simultaneous activities, the first job of a radio spot is simply to make the listener actually listen — through surprise, curiosity, humor, or direct human warmth — before any selling can happen at all.

## 13. Corporate (Image) Advertising

Corporate advertising — promoting the company itself rather than any specific product — is common among large companies (the large majority of top US corporations run some form of it) but, in Ogilvy's assessment, is usually done badly.

Documented benefits, when it's done well: companies with strong public name recognition are measurably more likely to receive a favorable opinion from the public; corporate advertising can improve employee morale and recruiting; it can measurably improve a company's standing with investors and even modestly move its stock price (a cited academic study found an average positive stock-price effect large enough to represent a very significant dollar value for a large-cap company); and it can make a company a more attractive target in friendly takeover situations.

Key operating rules:

- **It requires patience.** Ogilvy stresses that corporate advertising cannot be judged on a short time horizon — some of the most durable and credibly successful corporate campaigns ran continuously for 40-plus years, while most fail simply because they are abandoned before they've had time to work.
- **Avoid meaningless slogans.** He surveys a long list of vapid corporate taglines (many built around the interchangeable word "people") as a cautionary catalogue of corporate advertising that says nothing distinctive.
- **Avoid alphabet-soup renaming.** Changing a company's public name to an unexplained set of initials imposes years of costly re-education on the audience, for essentially no benefit, unless the initials are already universally recognized (IBM, CBS).
- **It can influence legislation and public opinion**, but only as a sustained, patient campaign aimed at the general public via television (the medium on which, he argues, public opinion is actually formed) — not as a short-term reactive campaign, and not by trying to target a supposed narrow audience of "thought leaders," who in practice cannot be reliably identified or isolated.

## 14. Business-to-Business Advertising

Business-to-business (what used to be called trade or industrial advertising) follows most of the same rules as consumer advertising, with some category-specific findings drawn heavily from McGraw-Hill research:

- Advertising is dramatically cheaper per contact than a personal sales call or a letter, and functions to pre-sell and generate leads rather than to close the sale directly — closing remains the sales force's job.
- Industrial purchase decisions typically involve multiple "buying influences" inside the customer organization, and a sales force alone is unlikely to know or reach all of them; advertising reaches audiences a salesperson never meets, including the technical "specifiers" who set purchase requirements.
- Promises must be genuinely relevant to what the professional buyer cares about — Ogilvy cites a software vendor who wanted to advertise the size of his company, when research showed customers actually cared about responsiveness and support.
- Promises should be specific — percentages, elapsed time, dollars saved — because the audience is technically literate and skeptical of generalities.
- Testimonials work well when they come from credible experts in the field, not celebrities.
- Demonstrations work best when they let the reader directly compare the product's performance against a competitor's, ideally via a simple test the reader can perform themselves.
- Long copy (over roughly 350 words) outperforms short copy in this category too.
- Four-color ads in trade publications cost only marginally more than black-and-white but attract roughly twice the readership.
- Every advertisement should close with a clear response mechanism — toll-free number, business reply card, and coupon together, since different readers of the same physical publication (an average trade publication is read by several people beyond the original subscriber) need independent ways to respond.
- Track and analyze inquiries systematically — survey a sample of inquirers on purchase intent, debrief the sales team on what happened to each lead, and correlate inquiries back to the specific publication that generated them; this level of analysis has been shown to let advertisers cut wasted spend substantially.

On differentiating supposedly generic "commodity" products: Ogilvy cites the view (from Professor Theodore Levitt) that no product is truly a commodity — all goods and services can be differentiated, most successfully either on cost leadership or on a superior reputation for quality/service, with advertising doing real work either way. He offers an insulation manufacturer's pivot from an undifferentiated builder-focused message to a homeowner-focused fuel-savings message, anchored on the product's distinctive color, as a case study in escaping the commodity trap.

## 15. Direct Mail and Direct Response

Ogilvy calls direct mail his first love and "secret weapon," crediting it as a major driver of his own agency's early new-business success, and treats it as the branch of advertising where claims can actually be proven, because results are measured to the dollar.

Central discipline: **in direct mail, testing is everything.** Because only one variable can be tested cleanly at a time, the practitioner must prioritize which variables to test — after positioning, the highest-value variables are usually price, payment terms, premiums, and mailing format. He cites concrete examples where counterintuitive results overturned assumptions: a lower subscription price with fewer issues generated more net revenue than a higher-priced offer; a mailing offering only part of a coin collection outsold a mailing offering the complete collection.

Once a control (a mailing that reliably produces profitable results) is established, the discipline is to keep testing challengers against it — a new premium, an expiration date, a personalized letter — and to test whether a *cheaper* version of the control (smaller format, fewer colors, less personalization) can match its results at lower cost. Sweepstakes, premiums, and low introductory prices reliably build initial response, but Ogilvy cautions that customers acquired that way are not automatically the same customers who become long-term profitable buyers — asking full price up front yields fewer initial responses but potentially better long-term customers, and only testing settles which trade-off is worth making for a given business.

Direct-mail-specific copy rules echo and extend the general print rules: the headline is the single most important element and can produce differences of several multiples in response; long copy outsells short copy even more decisively here than in general advertising; testimonials increase response, but celebrity testimonials should be avoided unless the celebrity is a genuine subject-matter authority; coupons should be treated as miniature advertisements in their own right, carrying the brand name, the promise, and even a small product photo, because many readers skip straight from headline to coupon.

Direct-response television has its own distinct rules, drawn from the practice of Ogilvy's partner Al Eicoff: commercials shorter than about two minutes rarely produce profitable direct sales; twenty seconds must be reserved purely for ordering instructions (repeating the toll-free number at least twice); the best-performing time slots are often early morning, late night, and weekends rather than expensive prime time; and — counterintuitively — the more gripping the surrounding program, the *fewer* orders a direct-response commercial generates, because a bored audience is a more responsive audience. There is, in his account, no reliable correlation between the size of the audience reached and the number of orders received.

## 16. Advertising for Good Causes

Advertising techniques apply as directly to charitable and public-interest causes as to commercial products, and Ogilvy offers several of his own campaigns as case studies: publishing an orchestra's full season schedule in advance to rebuild public confidence in a struggling institution; a direct-mail letter timed to a commuter train's exact route past a visibly poor neighborhood, which produced a large one-evening surge in charitable donations; and long-running government-style public health campaigns (e.g., Norwegian teen-drinking-awareness advertising) that produced measurable shifts in reported behavior.

The core constraint he identifies for cause-related and charitable advertising specifically: it's difficult to persuade people to give money to a cause they don't already know something about, meaning that awareness-building work generally has to precede — or run alongside — any direct fundraising appeal.

## 17. Competing with Procter & Gamble

Ogilvy treats Procter & Gamble as the most disciplined and effective marketing organization he has ever competed against, and lays out the observed elements of their playbook (credited largely to his partner Kenneth Roman's analysis) as a model worth understanding even — especially — if you are trying to beat them:

- **Discipline over improvisation.** P&G plans thoroughly, minimizes risk, and sticks to proven principles rather than chasing fashion.
- **Heavy sampling on launch.** Their own leadership has stated publicly that the largest part of an initial product investment usually goes into introductory sampling, on the logic that advertising and selling only become fully productive once real customers have had firsthand product experience.
- **Category dominance or nothing.** They avoid categories they don't expect to grow, and set out to dominate every category they do enter, using resulting volume to achieve superior manufacturing economics.
- **Internal competition.** They will run multiple branded products against each other within the same category, with no protection for either sibling brand.
- **Research-driven need-finding**, continuously scanning for emerging consumer trends rather than relying on static assumptions.
- **Genuine product superiority, verified blind.** Their former chairman is quoted stating explicitly that no amount of advertising skill can save a product that does not deliver a real, perceptible consumer benefit — and P&G tests this with blind in-home comparisons before relying on advertising claims.
- **Patient, extensive test-marketing** — sometimes running for years before a national rollout, on the stated principle that being right matters more than being first; only a handful of P&G products in the company's history have skipped a substantial test-marketing period, and most of those failed.
- **Strategic consistency.** Advertising strategies for their flagship brands remained essentially unchanged for decades once established — the company does not chase creative novelty at the expense of a working strategy.
- **A "moment of confirmation" in nearly every commercial** — a demonstrable, visible proof point (a fabric being squeezed to show softness, a stain visibly disappearing) rather than an assertion alone.
- **Heavy use of demonstration** — the majority of their commercials show the product visibly outperforming a comparison.
- **Aggressive, repeated brand-name registration** early and often within each commercial, using short, simple names.
- **No brand-name attacks on competitors** — they refer to "the other leading brand" rather than naming rivals directly, even in highly competitive claims.
- **Year-round, un-flighted advertising** rather than pulsing on and off, which they have found outperforms intermittent scheduling and also produces cost savings.

Ogilvy's identified weakness in this otherwise formidable system: P&G's very consistency makes them predictable, and predictability is exploitable by a competitor who can anticipate their strategy. He cites cases of smaller competitors beating specific P&G brands (a chip brand beating Pringles, a shampoo beating Lilt) purely by shipping a genuinely better product — underscoring his broader marketing principle that a superior product is always the most reliable competitive weapon, more reliable than any advertising technique.

## 18. Eighteen Miracles of Research

Ogilvy, who worked as a professional researcher (including running George Gallup's Audience Research Institute) before becoming a copywriter, lays out eighteen distinct things well-used research can do for an advertiser, including:

- Measure a company's reputation across distinct audiences (consumers, security analysts, government officials, editors, academics).
- Use mathematical models to estimate new-product sales and the advertising spend needed to hit a profit target, and flag when a product isn't even worth the cost of a formal test market (he notes roughly 60 percent of new products fail in test markets).
- Get consumer reactions to a product concept before it's built, potentially saving a company from a costly, doomed investment.
- Find out whether people can physically open your packaging, not just whether they like how it looks.
- Determine optimal product positioning.
- Define a target audience precisely (demographics, life stage, media habits).
- Identify what vocabulary real consumers use when discussing a category, and what factors most influence their purchase decision.
- Determine the best "line extension" for an established brand name (research correctly identified a dish-soap extension as the strongest opportunity for a soap brand already established via a moisturizing positioning).
- Detect early erosion in consumer perception of an established product, which often signals quietly cheapened ingredients.
- "Read" a competitor's test market performance, sometimes even estimating their cost structure and margins from public signals.
- Determine the single most persuasive — and, separately, the most *unique* — promise to make; Ogilvy treats this as possibly the single most valuable thing research can contribute to advertising, and flags that headline promise-testing via split-run (two headlines, two matched audiences, whichever pulls more free-sample requests wins) is his preferred method over asking consumers to simply rate promises in an interview, because it tests the promise in the real context of an advertisement rather than an artificial interview setting.
- Determine which of several possible premiums will perform best (steak knives beat several rival premium options in one Shell test, for example).
- Confirm whether an advertisement communicates what it's intended to communicate.
- Identify which of several television commercials will sell more, while flagging that *recall* testing (a widely used method) is unreliable: recall has never been shown to correlate with sales, celebrity-driven commercials often score high on recall and low on actual brand-preference change, and a clever copywriter can inflate a recall score with a purely attention-grabbing but irrelevant device without improving persuasion at all. Ogilvy prefers testing methods that directly measure brand-preference change over simple recall.
- Measure advertising "wear-out" over time, so a still-working message isn't retired too early or a genuinely exhausted one isn't run too long.
- Measure actual readership of print advertising (the Gallup/Starch tradition of asking readers to point to exactly what they read, which revealed, among other things, that captions and comic strips are read by more people than most editorial articles).
- Settle internal arguments with data rather than opinion or seniority.

He is careful to note research's proper limits: it can tell you *what* consumers think and do, but not *whether you should advertise at all* — that remains a business judgment research cannot make for you.

## 19. Research Pitfalls and Research Among Children

Practical cautions about how research goes wrong:

- Respondents often don't tell interviewers the truth, especially about status-laden questions (readership of serious literature, media habits they feel embarrassed about); Ogilvy describes deliberately planting a control question — asking whether someone would rather hear Jack Benny or a Shakespeare play on the radio — to catch respondents who were answering aspirationally rather than honestly.
- Asking about past behavior can inflate positive answers due to social desirability bias; reframing the same question in terms of future intention can produce a more honest, symmetric result.
- Price itself functions as a quality signal independent of the product: matched product samples in different packaging, or identical products priced differently, have both been shown to shift consumers' actual reported taste and quality judgments — people are, in a real sense, tasting and judging the price, not just the product.
- Small samples (as few as twenty respondents) can be perfectly adequate for simple binary questions (does this word mean anything to people), while tracking *trends over time* requires larger, carefully controlled samples with rigidly consistent question wording.

Research with children requires distinct methods because children understand only simple questions, struggle to articulate answers, and tend to tell adult interviewers what they think the adult wants to hear. Ogilvy describes three practical techniques: group-dynamics play sessions that surface misunderstandings indirectly; "communication discrepancy" testing, which compares what children say they like about a commercial against what they say they like about the actual product, to catch commercials that undersell (or oversell and disappoint) the real product; and a "prize pad" method that measures a commercial's actual persuasive effect on stated toy preference before and after exposure. He also catalogs the substantial regulatory constraints (in the US, at the time of writing) specifically governing advertising to children — restrictions on implying peer rejection, on frightening content, on unrealistic product context, and on omitting disclosures like required batteries — as a real, binding design constraint on this category of advertising.

## 20. Principles of Marketing

A set of marketing observations Ogilvy states plainly are drawn from decades of practical exposure rather than academic marketing theory (which he professes not to understand or trust):

- **New products drive a large share of category sales.** A large minority of supermarket sales, by his account, come from products that did not exist a decade earlier — meaning a company's willingness to keep introducing genuinely new products is a leading indicator of its future health, more so than its willingness to acquire existing brands.
- **Most new products fail for lack of real difference**, not for being too novel — the fix is a genuine, perceptible point of difference (better taste, more convenience, a real cost saving), ideally one anchored to something the consumer already recognizes as valuable.
- **Don't waste time reviving failing products.** Concentrate resources, talent, and ad budget on your winners; abandoning a losing product decisively is described as a mark of good management, not failure.
- **Promotions are not a substitute for advertising.** Price-off deals and coupons produce, at best, a temporary "kink" in the sales curve and can train customers to buy only on deal; heavy reliance on promotion at advertising's expense is described as a recurring, foreseeable mistake that erodes a brand's long-term position.
- **Advertising should be budgeted like a production cost, not a discretionary selling cost** — meaning it should not be the first thing cut in a downturn. Multiple recession studies he cites found that companies which maintained advertising spend through a recession achieved stronger profit growth than those that cut back, and a wartime case study (Unilever continuing to advertise a margarine brand that had temporarily vanished from shelves due to wartime restrictions) is offered as a dramatic illustration of the payoff to maintaining presence through an absence.
- **Pricing is largely guesswork industry-wide**, not a rigorously scientific process, despite appearances — and a cited academic study found almost no real correlation between a food product's price and its actual measured quality. Higher pricing tends to increase perceived desirability regardless of underlying quality, and Ogilvy notes an emerging trend (as of his writing) toward premium products at premium prices outperforming forced low-price/low-quality positioning.
- **Consumers maintain a "repertory" of several acceptable brands**, not a single fixed loyalty, and rotate among them (a finding credited to researcher A.S.C. Ehrenberg). Consumers rarely add an entirely new brand to that repertory after its first year on the market, meaning launch-period advertising investment is disproportionately important — it is closer to a now-or-never decision than an ongoing one. Ongoing advertising after launch mostly reinforces existing users' frequency and habitual repertory position rather than "converting" large numbers of new users.

## 21. Naming and Launching Products

Ogilvy identifies three broad categories of brand name, each with distinct trade-offs:

- **Personal names** (of real or invented people) — memorable, hard for competitors to copy, and implying the product is the creation of an actual human being.
- **Meaningless invented names** — distinctive and legally easy to protect, but requiring years of sustained investment before they carry any inherent sales meaning.
- **Descriptive names** — carrying built-in sales appeal from day one, but too narrowly specific to support later line extensions into adjacent products.

He recommends testing any candidate name for whether it says what you intend it to say, whether it's easily pronounceable, whether it risks confusion with an existing name, and whether it's memorable — and warns specifically that a name intended for international use must be checked for accidental obscene or embarrassing meanings in other languages before launch.

On sequencing launch investment: he cites cases (a mouthwash, a confectionery company) where products that had sold modestly for decades *without* advertising saw dramatic sales increases once advertised for the first time — evidence that even an already-viable, unadvertised product can often be made significantly more profitable by advertising, not just kept alive by it.

He also flags a structural shift he observed in his own era: the escalating cost of launching a brand aimed at outright category dominance (citing a single cigarette launch that cost $100 million) was making the "universal blockbuster brand" progressively rarer, with growth in cable television and audience segmentation making it more realistic — and more common — to aim new brands at narrowly defined market segments instead.

## 22. Foreign and Travel Advertising

Ogilvy's tourism and country-image work (Britain, France, the United States, Puerto Rico, and various airlines) surfaces a recurring political tension: research reliably reveals what a country's target foreign visitors actually want to see (for American visitors to Britain: historical and traditional sites; for Europeans visiting the US: iconic landmarks like Manhattan and the Grand Canyon), but government clients repeatedly pressure the advertising to instead feature what the *government* wants promoted — economically depressed regions in need of tourism revenue, a home-state senator's district, symbols of national modernity — regardless of what the research shows visitors actually respond to. Ogilvy's consistent position is to follow the visitor-preference research over the political preference, and cites Britain's rise from the fifth to the first most-visited European country among American tourists during his campaign as vindication of that approach.

A second recurring theme: country image can be deliberately and successfully repositioned through advertising when the existing image is inaccurate — his Puerto Rico tourism work overturned a mistaken public perception of the island as dirty and unappealing, producing a large increase in real visitor arrivals once the advertising showed the island as it actually was.

He also notes the underlying economics: for many countries tourism is one of the largest sources of foreign exchange, yet most national governments chronically under-invest in the advertising needed to capture that opportunity, with only a handful of countries (Canada, Britain, Greece, Ireland, and some Caribbean destinations, in his account) treating tourism promotion as a serious, adequately funded discipline. Some destinations also face a countervailing anxiety that successful tourism promotion will damage the very cultural or environmental character that made the destination attractive in the first place — he contrasts Bermuda's deliberately curated destination advertising, aimed at attracting the specific kind of visitor it wants, against destinations that either resist tourism promotion outright or advertise indiscriminately and get an audience mismatched to what the location can sustainably support.

## 23. International Advertising and the N.I.H. Syndrome

Multinational advertisers often want a single global campaign, but local subsidiary managers and local agency staff typically resist centrally created campaigns — even when the local agency belongs to the same global network holding the parent relationship. Ogilvy, following Harvard's Professor Theodore Levitt, names the underlying force the "N.I.H. Syndrome" (Not Invented Here): any campaign not created domestically is experienced as a threat to local professional self-respect, regardless of its actual merit, and local teams will readily construct market-difference arguments to justify replacing it.

His resolution: settle the argument empirically rather than politically — actually test the international campaign in the local market, adopt it only where results are genuinely positive, and even then adapt it to local cultural specifics rather than running it completely unmodified. He notes that, more often than the N.I.H. instinct assumes, campaigns that perform well in the US also perform well internationally with only modest adaptation (citing a mascot character that succeeded across three dozen countries, and mileage-focused fuel commercials that worked essentially unchanged across several Western markets).

He surveys national differences in advertising style as background context — British advertising as comparatively indirect, subtle, and nostalgic rather than competitive and literal; German advertising constrained by staff shortages and scarce television time, forcing heavier reliance on print; several smaller European markets lacking the research budgets that support more evidence-based creative decisions in the US and UK — while also tracking a broader trend he calls "the hare and the tortoises": American advertising's historical dominance receding somewhat as advertising quality in Britain, South East Asia (he singles out a Bangkok office's culturally-specific Thai advertising as an internal award-winner over New York, Chicago, London, and Paris), Latin America, and Australia visibly improved during his career.

## 24. Careers and Roles Within an Agency

Ogilvy sets out the distinct skill profiles required by each major agency function:

- **Copywriters** are, in his view, the most important people in an agency despite being among the least visible. Hallmarks of a strong copywriter: obsessive curiosity about products, people, and the craft itself; a sense of humor; a habit of hard work; the ability to write both engaging prose and natural spoken dialogue; the ability to think visually (since television depends more on image than word); and outright ambition to write the best campaign ever written. He cites a colleague's distinction between two copywriter temperaments — "poets," who treat the ad as an end in itself, and "killers," who treat it purely as a means to a sale — noting that combining both traits is the path to real success.
- **Art directors** need training in film, layout, photography, and typography, plus genuine taste; many have evolved from print specialists into television producers as the medium shifted.
- **Account executives** exist to extract the best possible work from the rest of the agency on the client's behalf, and to represent the client's interests and the agency's interests to each other. Ogilvy distinguishes "custodians" (who merely relay messages between client and agency, like a waiter shuttling between kitchen and dining room) from "contributors" (who actively generate ideas and strategy) — and states plainly that only the latter path leads to real success in the role.
- **Researchers** need an analytical mind, genuine intellectual honesty (since biased research does serious damage), the ability to write clearly, and enough interpersonal skill to work with creative staff who are often instinctively hostile to research findings.
- **The Chief Executive Officer** of an agency needs financial acumen, administrative skill, salesmanship (since new-business generation is a core CEO responsibility), resilience under pressure, and the physical stamina for very long working hours and constant travel.
- **The Creative Director** needs, in Ogilvy's own ordered list: to be a good psychologist first and foremost, then able to set and hold high standards, an efficient administrator, capable of real strategic thinking, research-minded, equally strong across television and print and across different account types, well versed in graphics and typography, a fast and hard worker, slow to quarrel, willing to share credit and accept blame, a strong presenter, a good teacher and recruiter, and full of infectious enthusiasm.

He also offers concrete, practical advice for job applicants: write rather than call, correctly spell the agency's name, state clearly and immediately what job you're applying for, be specific and factual about your qualifications rather than using vague self-praise, write in a natural, personal voice rather than trying to sound impressive, and close by proposing a specific concrete next step (a day and time you'll call) rather than leaving the next move up to the busy reader.

## 25. Running an Agency: Hiring, Firing, Culture

Running an agency, in Ogilvy's account, requires sustaining the morale of people who work in a state of continuous professional anxiety — the copywriter fearing the next deadline, the account executive caught between client and agency, the agency head worried about losing clients or partners. His core prescription: make it *fun* to work there, because people under pure grimness do not produce good advertising.

Hiring principles:

- The hardest people to find and the most valuable to identify are those with the raw capacity to become excellent copywriters — marked by unusually well-furnished, curious minds and a strong sense of humor, regardless of age (he notes discovering an excellent copywriter who was only eighteen).
- His personal metaphor for scaling talent, delivered via a nested Russian doll sent to every new office head: hire people smaller than yourself and you build a company of dwarfs; hire people bigger than yourself and you build a company of giants.
- **Who not to hire:** friends (he made this mistake three times and had to fire all three, ending the friendships); a client's children (risks the account if you have to let them go); your own children or partners' children (nepotism drives away ambitious outside talent); people who were successful in unrelated fields (he cites hiring a magazine editor, a lawyer, and an economist, none of whom developed a real interest in advertising); and your own clients (the qualities that make someone a good client are not the qualities that predict success inside an agency).

On office politics: he lists concrete countermeasures — firing the worst offenders (identifiable by how often they circulate poison-pen memos), forcing accusers to repeat accusations face to face with the accused present, banning paper warfare in favor of direct confrontation, starting internal social clubs that turn rivals into friends, discouraging internal poaching, and never playing favorites or engaging in "divide and rule" tactics, which he says will eventually destroy an agency from within.

On discipline: insist on punctuality, prompt phone answering, and rigorous protection of client confidentiality (indiscreet conversations in elevators and restaurants, premature leaks to outside vendors, and posted upcoming-campaign materials are all cited as real, damaging failure modes) — and hold the line on deadlines even when it requires working through the night, provided the leader is personally part of that push rather than merely demanding it.

On alcoholism among staff: he treats it as a serious, common, and often-hidden problem (citing a commonly reported rate of around 7 percent of American executives) and describes a specific intervention model — a scheduled confrontation involving the person's spouse, framed around genuine care rather than punishment, followed by a same-day admission to treatment and a required year of ongoing support meetings after — which he reports succeeding in roughly 60 percent of cases he observed.

## 26. Running an Agency: Leadership

Drawing on decades of observing corporate leaders as clients, Ogilvy concludes that most executives are competent problem-solvers and decision-makers but comparatively few are genuine leaders — some are actively skilled at undermining the confidence of the people who report to them rather than building it.

Traits he associates with the leaders he most admired: self-confidence; refusal to pass the buck; the willingness to make unpopular decisions, including firing non-performers (he quotes Gladstone's line that a great Prime Minister must also be "a good butcher"); fanatical commitment to the work rather than a need to be universally liked; and — repeatedly — a strong streak of genuine unorthodoxy, since companies cannot innovate under leaders who instinctively reject anything that doesn't fit existing convention.

He explicitly rejects fear as a sustainable leadership tool: fear-based management can teach pure technical compliance quickly, but corrodes morale over time and drives the most talented people toward competitors who lead through trust instead. He frames the underlying model as "servant leadership" — inverting the org chart so that the ostensible top leader's real job is to serve and enable the people above them in the chart, cascading support downward until the people in direct contact with the customer are the *best*, not the least, supported people in the organization.

He also notes that effective leadership style is not universal but situational — a leadership approach that works in one country, one company maturity stage, or one type of institution does not necessarily transfer to another (citing the different psychological needs for autocratic versus democratic leadership styles across different national cultures as a concrete reason multinational agencies benefit from installing local leadership in local subsidiaries rather than exporting a single leadership style everywhere).

Finally: he treats a strong follower as a leadership multiplier in their own right, citing the historical anecdote of the Duke of Marlborough's chief of staff pre-positioning artillery at the exact spot the Duke was about to designate, without being told — the model, in Ogilvy's words, of "the kind of follower who makes leadership easy."

## 27. Getting New Clients

Ogilvy's own new-business philosophy, echoing J.P. Morgan's stated approach — "only first-class business, and that in a first-class way" — while acknowledging that early in a new agency's life, taking almost any paying account is sometimes necessary simply to cover costs.

His most consistent, repeated observation: **the single most effective way to win new clients is to produce visibly excellent advertising for existing clients** — prospective clients, in his account, are drawn to an agency primarily by seeing work they envy, not by being persuaded through a sales pitch, case-history statistics, or a research-heavy presentation (which he says tend to put prospects to sleep).

Practical new-business meeting discipline: mix the two teams' seating rather than facing off across a table like adversaries; rehearse thoroughly but never read from a prepared script, since a live meeting can move in directions a fixed script can't accommodate; listen far more than you talk, since how a prospective client behaves when given room to talk reveals whether the account is one you actually want; disclose your own weaknesses proactively before the prospect notices them, which increases the credibility of your subsequent strengths; and follow up in writing the next day, summarizing the case for hiring you, to help the prospect actually make the decision rather than leaving it to fade.

Warnings and constraints: never pay a finder's fee to a third party for steering business your way (a client acquired that way is rarely worth having, and it invites exactly the kind of "dirty work" it appears to reward); avoid clients whose ethical character clashes with the agency's own; be cautious with speculative "someday" accounts that spend little now but promise future growth, since servicing them can be expensive relative to the payoff, while acknowledging the risk of the opposite mistake (he passed on an unknown office-machinery company that turned out to be Xerox); and be alert to credit risk, since an agency's typically thin profit margins cannot absorb a client's bankruptcy.

He also names a specific institutional failure mode to avoid: chasing any account you can get out of anxiety about staff confidence during a dry spell, which risks an agency becoming part of the "melancholy procession" of agencies that repeatedly inherit a dying brand right before its final decline — in contrast to the genuinely long, stable client relationships (some spanning 50 to 70-plus years) that characterize the healthiest agency-client pairings.

## 28. How a Client Should Choose an Agency

Writing from the other side of the table, Ogilvy sets out a simplified process for a company choosing a new agency, deliberately cutting against the grain of the increasingly bureaucratic, questionnaire-driven, multi-round pitch processes he saw becoming standard in his era (which he argues often fail to select the agency that would actually perform best over years, since they reward whichever agency happens to produce the single best pitch-round campaign under artificial contest conditions).

His recommended process: personally review magazines and television for a few days and note down the specific advertisements you genuinely envy; identify which agencies made them; eliminate any already working for a direct competitor; meet the head of each remaining agency and its creative director to judge whether the personal chemistry is genuinely good (since, in his words, happy marriages produce results and unhappy ones don't); deliberately avoid meeting the specific junior staff who would work on the account day to day, since it's easy to be swayed by likability or put off by unconventional appearance in ways that have nothing to do with actual talent; ask to see each finalist's six best print ads and six best television commercials, and select based on which body of work genuinely interests you most; and, on fee negotiation, consider voluntarily paying slightly above the standard rate (he suggests one extra percentage point above a 15 percent commission) on the logic that the marginal cost is trivial to the client but can double the agency's effective profit margin on the account, buying meaningfully better service in return.

Ongoing management guidance for the client relationship: give the agency a formal annual performance review, since early warning of a deteriorating relationship is far more useful than a late one; and never allow advertising decisions to be filtered through more than two levels of internal approval, since he cites one major corporation that allowed five layers of veto power (with only the CEO holding final approval) as a structurally dysfunctional model that stifles good creative work before it ever reaches the client's actual decision-maker.

## 29. Six Giants Who Invented Modern Advertising

Ogilvy profiles six deceased figures he credits with defining the modern advertising business, extracting a distinct operating philosophy from each:

- **Albert Lasker** (Lord & Thomas) — defined advertising itself as "salesmanship in print" (a formulation credited to his colleague John E. Kennedy), built an agency run as a personal dictatorship with no board meetings, distrusted formal research and even art direction for most of his career on the theory that persuasive copy alone was sufficient, and demonstrated an instinctive, low-cost approach to consumer insight (his account of solving a stalled sanitary-product business simply by asking the men in the office to ask their own wives why they weren't buying it, rather than commissioning a large study).
- **Stanley Resor** (J. Walter Thompson) — built the opposite kind of organization: consensus-driven rather than dictatorial, deeply committed to research (he employed an economist, a psychologist, and a former Census director as in-house researchers, and ran an early ongoing consumer purchase panel), a strong believer in celebrity testimonials (in contrast to Ogilvy's own skepticism of the technique), and the first agency head to build a true international office network, at the request of a multinational client.
- **Raymond Rubicam** (Young & Rubicam) — Ogilvy's own personal mentor and closest friend among the six, credited with proving advertising could sell effectively without deceiving the public, insisting on high standards of taste and craft in both copy and graphics (he specifically hired a leading art director once he recognized his agency's early visual output was weak despite strong copy), willing to resign a large account rather than tolerate a client's mistreatment of his staff, and leaving behind the operating aphorism "resist the usual" as his defining legacy.
- **Leo Burnett** (Leo Burnett Company) — architect of the deliberately unpretentious "Chicago school" of advertising, built around plain, vernacular, "sod-buster" language rather than coastal sophistication, credited with the enduring principle that "there is an inherent drama in every product" whose job is to be dug out and dramatized, ran his creative output through an internal review process nicknamed "being nibbled to death by ducks," and explicitly warned his own successors that the moment the agency began prioritizing size and profit over the quality of the work, they should remove his name from the door.
- **Claude Hopkins** (Lord & Thomas) — author of *Scientific Advertising*, which Ogilvy credits as personally formative; an uncompromising experimentalist who insisted nearly any advertising question could be answered cheaply and definitively by an actual test campaign rather than by argument or opinion; downplayed the importance of illustration relative to copy; and, despite his reputation as a pure "hard sell" practitioner, is credited by Ogilvy with grasping the importance of brand personality and image a generation before that concept had a name.
- **Bill Bernbach** (Doyle Dane Bernbach) — Ogilvy's contemporary and, in some respects, his creative-philosophy opposite: a lifelong skeptic of research who believed it was the enemy of creativity, an unapologetic advocate of originality for its own sake, and the figure most credited with integrating copy and art direction as equal creative partners rather than treating art direction as subordinate to copywriting. His defining stated belief was that human nature itself is essentially unchanging across time, and that a communicator's core job is to understand those unchanging underlying drivers rather than chase superficial cultural novelty.

Ogilvy's summary observation across all six: despite sharply different philosophies on research, structure, and creative process, all six shared relentless work ethic, uncompromising personal standards, and — in most cases — a career built on an unusual, non-traditional path into the business rather than a conventional credentialed entry.

## 30. What's Wrong with Advertising

Ogilvy engages directly with intellectual critiques of advertising (citing economists and cultural critics who characterize it as manipulative, wasteful, or morally corrosive) and stakes out a deliberately unglamorous middle position: advertising is "no more and no less than a reasonably efficient way to sell," comparable to a printing press — a neutral tool whose ethical character depends entirely on what it's used to sell, not on some inherent property of persuasion itself.

Specific counter-arguments he offers:

- Advertising volume is genuinely regulated in practice — industry codes and government agencies actively review and can prosecute deceptive claims, meaning the practical odds of a consumer being seriously "manipulated" by a lawful advertisement are, in his view, low.
- The claim that advertising tricks people into buying things they don't "need" rests on an elitist premise that some third party gets to define what any individual actually needs — and he defends ordinary consumer pleasure in buying non-essential things as a legitimate, if unfashionable, position.
- If advertising spending were eliminated, the money would not automatically flow back to consumers as lower prices, and might just as easily be redirected to shareholder dividends or absorbed by media companies losing their primary revenue source — the assumption that killing advertising is a straightforward consumer win doesn't hold up.

His one major exception to the "advertising is basically honest" position: **political advertising**, specifically US television commercials for presidential candidates, which he identifies as essentially unregulated (protected as constitutionally shielded speech, exempt from the codes networks apply to product advertising) and, in several documented campaigns he reviews, flagrantly and deliberately dishonest in ways that would never be tolerated in commercial advertising for a consumer product. He treats this as a real structural gap in an otherwise reasonably well-policed industry, and notes he personally declined to ever take a political party as an agency client, citing conflicts of interest, credit risk, and the practical impossibility of avoiding this kind of dishonesty.

He is also unambiguously critical of billboards specifically, marshaling safety data (roads with billboards showing substantially higher accident rates), aesthetic arguments from public officials of the era, and stories of citizen vigilante action against illegal billboard placement, concluding that the small revenue billboards represent relative to total advertising spend does not justify their cost to public safety and shared visual environment.

His closing critique is less about ethics than about quality: he argues most advertising, particularly automotive advertising, actually contains *too little real information* — describing his own frustrated search for hard facts while shopping for a replacement car as evidence that much advertising is aimed at impressing industry insiders at trade conventions rather than actually informing the buying public, in direct contrast to the fact-dense advertising philosophy he practiced throughout his own career.

## 31. Thirteen Predictions

Ogilvy closes the book with thirteen forecasts for the advertising industry's future, offered explicitly as informed guesses rather than confident forecasting (he states plainly that he has never considered himself a futurist):

1. Research quality will keep improving, expanding the shared body of proven knowledge about what actually works.
2. Print advertising will undergo a renaissance.
3. Advertising generally will carry more real information and less empty rhetoric.
4. Billboards will be abolished.
5. Commercial clutter on television and radio will be brought under control.
6. Government use of advertising for public education, especially health education, will expand significantly.
7. Advertising will play a real role in addressing population growth.
8. Political candidates will abandon dishonest advertising practices.
9. The quality and efficiency of advertising outside the US will keep improving at an accelerating pace — more "tortoises" overtaking the American "hare."
10. Foreign agencies will successfully open offices in the United States.
11. Multinational manufacturers will keep expanding market share globally and will market more brands internationally, with campaigns created centrally but adapted to respect local culture.
12. Direct-response advertising will stop being treated as a separate specialty and will be absorbed into general agency practice.
13. The industry will find ways to produce effective television commercials at meaningfully lower cost.
