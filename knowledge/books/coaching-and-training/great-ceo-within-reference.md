# The Great CEO Within — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing coaching/training programs, or advising on management practice.*
*Book: "The Great CEO Within: The Tactical Guide to Company Building" — Matt Mochary*

---

## 1. Individual Operating Habits

The book's premise: the CEO is both the architect of culture and the central information hub, so the first system to install is a personal one.

**Getting Things Done (GTD), adapted from David Allen.** Process every inbox (email, Slack, text, to-dos) daily. If an item takes under two minutes, do it immediately. Otherwise, capture it into one of these lists:
- Next Actions, split by context (Computer, Calls, Outside, Home) — write each as a single, immediately actionable step, not a vague goal.
- Waiting For — what you delegated, to whom, and the date you asked. Scan it regularly and re-ping aging requests.
- Someday/Maybe — things you want to do eventually but not now.
- Agenda — one list per person/group you meet regularly. Batch discussion items here instead of interrupting people in real time; only true emergencies bypass this list.
- Projects — any objective requiring more than one sequential Next Action; break it into steps and feed them into Next Actions as each completes.
- Review cadence — Next Actions and Waiting For daily; Someday/Maybe, Agenda, and Projects weekly. Put your review sessions on the calendar.
- Goals — Mochary's own addition to GTD: keep your 10-year vision and quarterly OKRs on this list and refer to it when building Next Actions.

**Inbox Zero.** Treat your inbox like a hospital triage room, not a waiting room — separate "notice what's urgent" from "sit and process." Check messages only twice a day (morning and afternoon). Each pass: handle anything under two minutes immediately; everything else gets a Next Action per GTD and gets filed. Constant, all-day checking is a productivity drain, not diligence.

**Top Goal (from Greg McKeown's *Essentialism*).** Block two hours every single workday, as early as possible, to work exclusively on your top quarterly priority — no email, no messages, no calls. If two hours feels unreachable, ramp: start with 30 minutes, hold it for a week, then increase in steps until you reach two hours.

**On Time and Present.** Being late steals productive time from whoever is waiting on you — the obligation isn't "never be late," it's "communicate the delay the moment you know about it, before the other person has to break from their own work to wonder." To make this achievable in practice: schedule meetings at 25 and 50 minutes (not 30/60) so there's always a buffer; arrive 15 minutes early to outside meetings; keep phones face-down or pocketed during meetings — checking messages mid-meeting signals the meeting doesn't matter and erodes the group norm over time.

**When you say it twice, write it down.** The moment you catch yourself repeating an explanation, capture it in writing — a wiki entry, a doc, a wall poster if it's foundational. This eliminates the cost of re-explaining and becomes the seed of company documentation (feeds directly into the "No Single Point of Failure" practice below).

**Gratitude.** Confirmation bias means whatever question you ask, you'll find evidence for the answer. Most people are trained from childhood to ask "what's wrong" — deliberately counter it by asking "what's good about this situation / this person / this life" or completing "I am grateful for ___" daily, ideally first thing in the morning, triggered by a visual cue (a note by the bed, a journal). This isn't about ignoring real problems — it changes the emotional state you bring to solving them, and better emotional state measurably improves performance (analogous to cheering a losing kids' sports team by name and watching the tide turn).

**Appreciation** is gratitude spoken outward. When you notice something good about someone, tell them. Benefits run three ways: the recipient feels better about themselves, the recipient feels connected to you, and you start seeing that person more positively because you're actively looking for the positive in them. Reserve dedicated weekly time (Chris Fralic's model: one hour a week) for outreach and appreciation messages.

## 2. Group Decision-Making and Buy-In

**Writing vs. Talking.** Group inefficiency compounds — one wasted minute is multiplied by every person in the room. Fix: require that anyone raising an issue write it up in advance, along with a proposed solution, so the meeting is spent resolving open questions rather than generating the analysis live (Jeff Bezos's Amazon model: full written memos read silently at the start of the meeting, decision made same-session, or an owner assigned to research and report back next time). Ease a team into this over three stages:
- Stage 1: first 15 minutes of the meeting spent writing, then 10 minutes reading each other's updates, then discuss.
- Stage 2: writing happens before the meeting; no undocumented issues allowed; 10 minutes reading at the start.
- Stage 3: written submissions due by a fixed time the night before; participants must read and comment in the doc itself before the meeting; no verbal comments allowed unless already made in writing.

**Three methods of decision-making**, each trading time for buy-in:
- Method 1 — Manager decides, announces, takes questions. Fast, but generates almost no buy-in and captures none of the team's collective knowledge. Use for low-stakes calls.
- Method 2 — Manager (or a delegate) drafts a written "straw man," circulates it, collects written and verbal feedback, facilitates discussion, then decides. Moderate buy-in, moderate time. Use for the majority of important decisions.
- Method 3 — Full group discussion from scratch, no straw man, consensus sought. Maximum buy-in and maximum use of collective wisdom, but the most time-intensive. Reserve for core, high-stakes decisions (e.g., the 10-year company vision).

**Loudest Voice in the Room.** When using Method 3, the CEO's opinion carries disproportionate weight simply because people assume the CEO has more information. If you share your view before others share theirs, a meaningful percentage of the room will quietly shift toward your position even if they don't actually agree — undermining the real goal of the exercise. Fix: have everyone write down their own view and vote *before* you reveal yours.

**Impeccable Agreements.** Most startup inefficiency traces to sloppy commitments — vague deadlines, undeclared goals, no follow-through. An Impeccable Agreement has two properties: it is precisely defined (an objective third party could judge whether it was kept) and it is fully agreed to by everyone bound by it. Compare "we'll start after lunch" (ambiguous) to "it's 12:04, we start again at 1:00, we agree to be seated by then" (precise, including clock calibration). Write these down unless they're trivial or so routine that no one could forget them. If you realize in advance you can't keep an agreement, notify the other party immediately and state what you *can* do instead — this preserves their ability to adapt.

## 3. Conflict Resolution and Conscious Leadership

**The conflict resolution script** (drawing on Stephen Covey and Chris Voss): most interpersonal conflict is rooted in people feeling unheard, not in actual disagreement. You don't need agreement to earn respect — you need to prove you heard the other person. The fix is a facilitated, structured process:
1. Person A (the person with less power in the relationship) states their rawest, most unfiltered thoughts about Person B, working through one emotion at a time: Anger, then Fear, then Sadness, then Joy, then Excitement. If A won't open up, the facilitator voices the likely thoughts dramatically, in the first person, until A starts correcting and refining them. Ideal structure per statement: Observation (a factual claim, not a judgment) → Feeling ("when I notice that, I feel...") → Request ("I request that you...").
2. Person B repeats back a summary of what they heard and asks "did I get it right?" This repeats until A confirms "that's right," then the facilitator asks "do you feel heard, or is there more?" — continue until A says "I feel heard."
3. Roles switch: B shares their raw thoughts on the same emotion, A listens and reflects back.
4. Repeat the full cycle (share → reflect → confirm heard) across all five emotions in order: Anger, Fear, Sadness, Joy, Excitement. Usually Anger and Fear are enough to produce a breakthrough in understanding; continue through the remaining emotions if not.
5. When the moment of mutual understanding arrives, seal it physically — a hug, handshake, or high-five, matching whatever physical contact the two have used with each other before.
6. Co-create a forward plan: Person A proposes first, B adds, they iterate to agreement.
7. Write the plan down (verbal agreements aren't impeccable) — include named next actions with owners and due dates, plus a check-in date to confirm follow-through.

Use a trained outside facilitator for the first run — if a participant goes off-script and gets genuinely angry, results can get messy fast.

**Conscious Leadership** (drawing on *The 15 Commitments of Conscious Leadership* by Dethmer, Chapman, and Klemp): the core shift is from wanting to be right to wanting to learn. Fear of being wrong makes people defend positions and push to convince others, often with anger; conscious leadership means noticing when fear/anger/sadness has taken over your thinking, releasing it, and returning to curiosity, where creative and elegant solutions actually emerge.

**The Drama Triangle** is a structured exercise for surfacing what a group normally keeps hidden (both "I know I'm right" and "I won't share my real feelings for fear of offending"). For a given conflict or issue, each person stands and states, in turn:
- Hero: "I temporarily solve the situation by ___."
- Victim: "I am negatively affected by this in the following ways ___" / "I am powerless to permanently solve this because ___."
- Villain: "I blame [named person] for creating this by doing [named action]." (Self-blame is allowed.)
People keep cycling roles, naming new people to blame, until no one is left to blame. Then everyone answers: "Are you willing for this issue to easefully and playfully be resolved in this meeting?" — then move to brainstorming solutions. Once the worst thoughts are on the table and nothing is hidden, elegant solutions tend to come easily. Use an outside facilitator the first time.

**Empathy** (drawing on David Deida): to build trust with customers, investors, or team members, genuinely imagine yourself in their situation. People sense real empathy and respond with trust, because they believe you'll actually weigh their interests in your decisions.

**Joy vs. Fear as motivators.** Teams that do conscious-leadership work typically lose their fear quickly — and initially their output can dip because fear had been a (poor) motivator. Push through to the other side, where joy becomes the motivator; joy outperforms fear as a driver of sustained business results.

**Customer obsession as applied empathy.** You aren't building a product, you're solving a customer's problem — so continually live that problem: sit with customers, ask about their lives, observe their routines. This mindset is hardest to install in engineering (furthest from the customer) — the fix is having engineers regularly sit in on support and sales calls. Sales teams tend to already be customer-centric but need discipline not to promise every requested feature — customers know their pain but not what's technically feasible; that judgment is the company's job. Build trust by actively listening to pain, reflecting it back ("it seems you feel anger when..."), and having the customer confirm "that's right." Train this listening method company-wide via role-play.

## 4. Culture Design

Culture is the unwritten rule set governing how people in a group interact — the same person behaves differently in a bar versus a family dinner because the operative rules changed.

**Values exercise.** Determine company values by completing: "The rest of you can make all the decisions about the company, as long as ___." Whatever answers the team supplies are the real values. Repeat quarterly in year one to keep the whole team bought in. Once set, use values as literal hiring and firing criteria — bring in people who want to live them, let go of people who don't, or the values become decorative and meaningless.

**Fun.** Underrated as a performance lever — people who are having fun invest more time, energy, and attention, which compounds into better collaboration and problem-solving. Host events you personally enjoy and invite (don't require) the team. Litmus test: are people voluntarily spending time together outside work? If not, that's a signal to invest more in Conscious Leadership work and values buy-in.

**Hours.** The metric that matters is output, not hours logged — mandating hours produces compliance without effort. Motivate through visible goal progress, agreements, KPIs, open feedback, and fun, so long hours become a choice rather than an imposition. Leaders (including department heads) must model the behavior they want — first in, last out. Still, set a core overlapping block when everyone is present (anchored by a short daily stand-up) so collaboration windows exist.

**Meals.** Company-provided meals build cross-team bonding (people who buy their own food cluster with the same small subgroup daily) and have a pre-tax benefit advantage over people buying meals with post-tax income. Enforce "present" at meals — no phones or laptops at the table.

**Politics.** Defined here as lobbying for personal benefit — corrosive because it redirects energy from customer problems into internal jockeying, and it spreads virally the moment one successful lobby is observed by others (e.g., an ad hoc raise granted after a hallway conversation teaches the whole company that asking works). The only defense is a written, consistently applied policy covering compensation, raises, and promotions, with zero deviation. The recommended model is **Grade Level Planning** (Tesla's term): a detailed definition of every role and seniority level with attached compensation, published company-wide so people can see exactly what earns the next level. Practical timing: start designing GLP around 25-50 employees; most companies wait until 150+, which is usually too late.

## 5. Company Infrastructure

**Folder system and wiki.** Every company needs a structured shared-document system (Drive, Dropbox, Quip) with a folder per department, viewable by the whole company except the folder holding compensation, performance reviews, and PIPs. Maintain a Wiki that's essentially a curated index of links to important docs; reading it start to finish should be a mandatory onboarding step.

**Goal-tracking tools.** Individually, keep it simple (a GTD-compatible tool). At the group level, small teams can track goals in a shared doc; once you exceed a handful of people, adopt a dedicated system — either task-tracking (good at turning issues into next actions and tracking meeting-to-meeting progress) or goal-tracking (better at showing multi-week/month progress and therefore morale). Two rules to prevent tool-abandonment: never assign someone an action without explicit agreement, and keep a separate lightweight tool for individual actions not tracked by the group (group tools have more overhead per item than they're worth for personal to-dos).

**Areas of Responsibility (AORs).** Named after the "tragedy of the commons" — shared ownership of a task usually means it doesn't get done. Solution: group all company functions into categories, and assign exactly one person as owner of each. Maintain a single AOR document (function → owner) as a living company directory; update it as responsibilities shift, and make sure everyone knows how to find it.

**No Single Point of Failure.** A single point of failure is any function only one person understands. Two-part fix: (1) write down every process the first time you catch yourself doing something for the second time, and store it in the company wiki; (2) cross-train a backup for every AOR — the backup co-works with the primary until they can run the function alone. Written processes make this cross-training dramatically faster.

**KPIs (Key Performance Indicators).** Identify five or six metrics per major function that let the whole team see company health at a glance — display them somewhere visible (e.g., a dashboard tool on an office screen). Sample department-metric pairs: Finance → monthly cash burn and cash in bank; Sales → MRR; Engineering → percentage of tickets closed; Recruiting → percentage of offers accepted. Critically, pair every KPI with a **counter-metric** (a concept from Andy Grove's *High Output Management*) — a raw metric optimized in isolation can mislead. Example: closing tickets is meaningless if only the easy ones get closed; a high offer-acceptance rate is bad news if the candidates who decline are the stronger ones.

## 6. The Meeting and OKR System

The critical failure point for scaling companies is the transition past ~10-20 people, when in-room information osmosis stops working and a formal system becomes mandatory — expect this to cost roughly one full day per week per manager, permanently, at any company size from 25 to 25,000 people. Two ways to install it: hire a COO who has run this exact system at a 200+ person company, or bring in an ex-CEO as a "one day a week CEO" for 6-8 weeks to build and run it, then shadow them for two weeks before taking it over.

**10-Year Vision** (set/refreshed quarterly at the Quarterly Goals Meeting, alongside quarterly OKRs). Answer, as if it's already true: what industry do you dominate; who is your customer (a real human, not an entity) and what pain are you solving; what's unique about your solution versus competitors; what asset (human or physical) forms your moat.

**OKRs (Objectives and Key Results).** Aim for three per quarter, company-wide and per department. Objective answers "where do we want to go," Key Results answer "how do we know we're getting there." Example — Objective: reach $500K MRR. Key Results: hire 10 SDRs, hire 5 CS agents, hire a Sales Ops lead. From the Quarterly Goals Meeting, derive near-term weekly actions, each assigned to one named owner; the following week's meeting opens by checking whether each action was completed.

**Standard meeting agenda template** (numbers in brackets are time-boxed minutes; unfinished items get punted to "meetings to be scheduled," never allowed to run long):
- On-time check — who's present at start
- Department updates (5 min each)
- Roadmap check: last week's goals — completed yes/no; if no, why, and what habit prevents recurrence
- Roadmap for next week — what actions keep you on pace for quarterly goals
- Issues (30 min)
- Meetings to be scheduled (5 min) — anything needing a subset of the team
- Feedback (10 min) — what's working, what you wish were different

The meeting types this template serves:
- **Quarterly Goals Meeting** — leadership team spends 1-2 days refreshing the 10-year vision and setting quarterly OKRs; departments then cascade their own OKRs to support the company's. The very first instance should also tackle whatever systemic issues currently plague the company, folding fixes into that quarter's goals.
- **Weekly Team Meeting** — each attendee reports YES/NO on last week's committed action (if NO: why, and what habit prevents repeat), reports department metrics, raises issues with proposed solutions, sets next week's goals, and gives feedback to the leader and peers.
- **Weekly One-on-Ones** — first one happens right after onboarding; manager and report each bring written OKRs and merge them into one shared list (ideally three or fewer). Recommended prep reading for the whole team: *The One Minute Manager* (30-minute read). Team member side of the template: goals (successes/proud of, setbacks/not proud of, preventive habit), updates (KPIs, new customer/product info), issues (missing tools), feedback (liked/wish were different). Manager side: elicit and thank for negative feedback and act on it fast, state what you liked and want changed about the report's actions, update/confirm the OKRs are still the straightest line to the goal. Schedule at a fixed day/time, 25-50 minutes, weekly (can shift bi-weekly once someone's goals stabilize). Note: One-on-Ones can merge into the Team Meeting *only* if the team has explicitly opted into radical transparency and been trained in it (Conscious Leadership Group runs a one-day training for this) — otherwise negative feedback delivered in a group produces shame.
- **Company-Wide Meeting** — weekly to monthly cadence, shares the leadership team's most recent output, same format, open floor for issues/feedback from anyone.
- **Contrarian Office Hour** — one open hour per week per manager where anyone can raise an issue; caps the manager's exposure to unplanned interruptions while guaranteeing everyone a channel to be heard.
- **Meeting Lead** — every meeting needs one designated owner (not necessarily the manager) responsible for enforcing written-submission-in-advance, on-time starts, and ruthless time-boxing; off-topic items get parked, not indulged.

**Organizational structure.** Below ~6 people and pre-Product-Market-Fit, skip formal structure entirely — don't even formally distinguish who "is" the CEO. Once PMF is achieved and the team scales, structure becomes necessary because not everyone fits in one meeting. Org structure should be defined by who attends which team meeting. Typical Leadership Team: CEO, Head of Product, Head of Engineering, Head of Sales/Marketing, Head of Customer Success, Head of Operations (People, Finance, Legal, Office). Once adopted, publish it — no ambiguity about reporting lines or meeting membership.

## 7. Feedback System

**Receiving feedback.** Critical feedback should be treated as valuable, not threatening — your team has ground-level knowledge you don't. Ben Horowitz's framing: good culture moves bad news fast and good news slow; defensiveness reverses that, and you end up isolated from your own company's real problems. Left unaddressed, breakdowns in feedback culture also produce gossip (communication finds an indirect route when the direct one is punished) and drive out your best people, who have no patience for founders who won't face problems. Three concrete practices:
1. Ask for it explicitly, one-on-one — tell people directly that negative feedback is cherished, not punished. A useful prompt: "If you were CEO, what would you change?" (in person or anonymous survey).
2. Listen without interrupting or explaining yourself; only pivot toward solutions once you've reflected the issue back and the person confirms you understood it.
3. Act on it immediately once you agree with it — create a real Next Action (GTD-style). Inaction here is what destroys trust and future candor, more than any single piece of hard feedback.

**Giving feedback.** Use two-way channels only — in person is best, video acceptable, audio call weaker — because you need to see the reaction in real time and address defensiveness on the spot ("I didn't intend to make you feel angry, my intent was to be helpful"). Never deliver feedback via a one-way channel (email, text, voicemail) unless it's 100% positive, or unless you already know the recipient to be unusually open and eager for critical input — a one-way negative message that lands wrong can curdle into unaddressed resentment. Template (adapted from Marshall Rosenberg's *Nonviolent Communication*):
1. Ask permission — "I have something to communicate, is now a good time?"
2. State the triggering behavior factually, not interpretively ("when you are late to meetings..." not "when you disrespect me...").
3. State how that behavior makes you feel.
Then work toward an agreed new way of behaving — the conversation itself often provides relief, but without a concrete behavior change, the underlying frustration will resurface. Expect early implementation of this practice to surface a lot of buried resentment; persistence through that phase is what produces a durably happier, more productive team.

## 8. Fundraising

**Pick a partner, not a firm.** The individual partner matters more than the fund brand — ask other founders who's actually good to work with at each firm, then target that person directly.

**The Triangulation Method for introductions.** Find three to five people in your network who know the target investor. Have each send a separate, individually-worded (not copy-pasted) email recommending you, all within roughly the same week. Multiple independent recommendations landing close together read as "this founder must be exceptional" rather than as an obviously orchestrated campaign — timing compactness is what makes the signal rise above background noise.

**Two fundraising methods.** The Traditional Method pitches the full story (problem, solution, market size, unit economics, projections, competition, team, traction, go-to-market) cold, usually via deck, and requires the pitch itself to spark interest. The Relationship Method builds trust and rapport with the investor *before* ever discussing the business — slower, but the close rate is dramatically higher, because investment decisions are emotional first and rationalized second; if the investor already likes and trusts you, an imperfect company survives scrutiny, whereas if they don't, the pitch has to be flawless to work.

**Building trust and liking as a discipline** — four repeatable actions:
1. Ask genuinely curious questions about the person's life, work and personal.
2. Prove you listened: "I think I heard you say..." with a reflection of what they said.
3. Prove you remembered, at the *next* meeting: "The last time we talked, you said..."
4. Tell them explicitly what you appreciate about them (at minimum, that they took the time to meet).
Three to five rounds of this (in-person, call, or even a short remembered-detail message) is typically enough to build real trust — sometimes a single well-timed, specific message (a handwritten thank-you note, a remembered fact about their life) does as much work as an hour-long meal. If you lack a warm-intro path, it's acceptable to just ask directly for a relationship-first meeting: "We only want to work with investors we have a good relationship with — let's start with a coffee."

**Sell yourself, not just the company** — investors who buy into *you* personally follow you through every pivot and every future company. A structure for telling your personal story humbly while still conveying real achievement (attributed to Tyler Weitzman's research into master storytellers): Credit ("it couldn't have happened without [others]") → Hard Work (what it actually took) → Vulnerability (what was hardest) → Duty (the noble motive driving you) → Gratitude (what you're proud and thankful for).

**Timing and inflection points.** Company value rises in a stair-step, not a straight line — each de-risking milestone (assembling a capable engineering team, landing your first three paying customers, crossing $1M ARR as proof of PMF, building a capable sales team, crossing $5M ARR as proof the sales motion works, filling out senior management) resets the value floor. The best time to raise is immediately after hitting a milestone, because value won't increase again until the next one, which could be months out.

**SAFEs/Convertible Notes vs. priced equity.** Use a SAFE/Note when the round is too small to justify a full priced round or there's no institutional lead — legal costs run under $10K versus $100K+ for a priced round. Rule of thumb: only do a priced round once total raised will exceed $2M (preferably $5M+). Recommended pattern: raise the earliest money as a SAFE, let it convert into your first priced round (Series A) at PMF, immediately open a second SAFE to bridge to the next priced round (Series B) at ~$5M ARR, and repeat. Institutional investors generally require priced rounds, but family offices and strategics are often comfortable with SAFEs as long as they trust a priced round is coming — so there's little downside to always having a SAFE open as a runway extension.

**Voting shares and FF (Founder Friendly) shares.** Structuring founder shares with outsized voting rights (Zuckerberg's Facebook structure) is now an accepted, low-downside setup — implement it before you have equity investors, since it's far harder to add after the fact. FF shares let founders access liquidity at each priced round without inflating the fair-market valuation used for other employees' options, which lets founders keep low public salaries (good optics) while still accessing some liquidity.

## 9. Recruiting: The Who Methodology

Framework credited to Geoff Smart and Randy Street's *Who*. Efficiency principle: minimize time spent with candidates you won't hire, maximize time spent with the ones you will — every minute on a non-hire is a minute stolen from your future hire.

**Scorecard** (built before sourcing starts): a written Mission (the business problem this role solves and why), 3-8 measurable Outcomes with due dates ranked by importance (what the person must accomplish), and Competencies (the behavioral traits required to hit those outcomes and fit the culture — e.g., organized, sense of urgency, coachable, persuasive). Pressure-test the scorecard against the company/department roadmap and against the scorecards of people this role will interface with.

**Source** — three channels, in order of effectiveness: referrals from your own and your team's networks (by far the most effective; make "source one qualifying candidate per quarter" a standing objective on everyone's scorecard), recruiters (only useful if given full transparency into your scorecard and your real-time reactions to their candidates), and researchers (rarely used — they surface names but don't do outreach).

**Select** — four sequential interviews, designed to gather facts about years of past behavior rather than impressions of interview-room performance:
- **Phone Screen** (≤30 min, preview 15 extend to 30 if strong): "What are your career goals? What are you really good at professionally (push for 8-12 specifics)? What are you not good at (push for 5-8 real weaknesses)? Who were your last three bosses and how would each rate you 0-10 (a 6 is really a 2 — dig into why)?" Weed out hard and fast — wasting hours on a borderline B/C candidate costs more than missing an A.
- **Topgrading Interview** (~2 hrs, 3 for a CEO hire, 1 for entry-level): chronological walk through every job in 3-5 year "chapters." Per job, ask: what were you hired to do; what accomplishments are you most proud of; what were the low points (don't let them off the hook); who did you work with (get their boss's name and spelling — signals you'll actually call); why did you leave (push/pull framework — "pull" moves, being recruited toward something better, are the stronger signal). Techniques: interrupt tactfully every 3-4 minutes to keep pace and redirect toward high-value material; apply the "Three Ps" to contextualize any claimed accomplishment (versus prior year, versus plan, versus peers); watch for inconsistencies as "stop signs" and dig in with curiosity, not suspicion.
- **Focused Interview** — 3 team members (not the hiring manager) each run a 50-minute session, each covering roughly a third of the scorecard's Outcomes and Competencies, using "what/how/tell me more" follow-ups.
- **Reference Interview** — never skip this. Get references from bosses, peers, *and* subordinates (sometimes 2-3 levels down) — not the candidate's hand-picked list; have the candidate arrange the calls. Ask: what was the working context; what were their biggest strengths; what were their biggest areas for improvement "back then" (this phrasing frees people to be honest); how would you rate their performance 0-10 and why; probe any stated weakness directly. Faint or qualified praise is itself a red flag.

**Rating: Skill-Will Bull's-Eye.** Only mark a candidate an A if you have >90% confidence they both can (skill) and want to (will) meet every outcome and competency. Red flags to watch for across the whole process: no past failures mentioned, exaggeration, taking credit for others' work, badmouthing past bosses, unexplainable job changes, family opposition to the move, a managerial candidate who's never hired or fired anyone, more interest in comp/title than the actual work, trying too hard to look like an expert, and a lack of curiosity about you or others (self-absorption).

**Sell** — happens at every stage (sourcing, interviewing, offer-to-acceptance gap, acceptance-to-start-date gap, and the first 100 days), organized around five things people care about: Fit (show the company/department/individual roadmap and how it matches their goals), Family (ask directly what would ease the transition for their family, and actually meet those needs), Freedom (autonomy, no micromanagement — invite them to reference-check *you*), Fortune (a real financial upside scenario), Fun (what the team actually does for fun). Persistence matters — candidates are frequently lost to the feeling of being abandoned after the offer or on day one.

**Compensation formula.** Find true market comp for the role/seniority at a big-company benchmark (Google, Meta, etc.). Determine what cash the candidate needs to live comfortably. Pay that cash floor (never less), and bridge the remaining gap to market with equity: (market comp − comfortable cash) × 4 years, divided by a conservative multiple (1 = no expected value growth, 2 = 2x growth; 1.5 is the common default) = the option grant value, vesting over 4 years. Offer candidates a choice of 2-3 cash/equity mixes so they can self-select how much they want to bet on the company (their equity purchase is effectively made with pre-tax dollars).

**Making the offer.** Before drafting anything, get the candidate to complete "I will join the company as long as..." and surface every requirement. Then describe the full prospective offer (cash, equity, benefits) and ask directly, "would you accept this?" Only formalize the offer once they've said yes to that hypothetical — skipping this step invites late-stage renegotiation (signing bonus, moving costs) that either seeds a political culture or forces an awkward "no." Make the acceptance moment ceremonial and memorable.

**Onboarding** deserves more investment than recruiting itself — recruiting time is spent on many people who won't join; onboarding time is spent 100% on people who already have. Build a written/video checklist of everything a new hire needs to know, share it (plus the 90-day roadmap) before day one. Have them arrive two hours after normal start on day one so people are around to greet them. Assign a buddy for 15-minute daily check-ins for the first two weeks.

**Firing and Performance Improvement Plans (PIPs).** Non-performance rarely self-corrects, and everyone on the team already knows it — inaction is a morale and financial drain the company can't absorb. Document to protect against wrongful-termination claims and to create a genuine (if unlikely) chance of turnaround: write a PIP with objective milestones at 7/30/60/90 days, check weekly against them, and terminate at the first missed 30/60/90-day gate — if you don't actually fire on a missed milestone, you've proven the document is meaningless and lost its protective value going forward. Under ~10 people, skip the PIP process entirely — the cost of demoralizing a tiny team outweighs legal risk. When firing: this is a real emotional and financial blow to the person, so give a real severance runway (1 month minimum, realistically 2-3), actively help them land their next role, and treat the failure as partly your own — recruiting, training, and management all contributed — then ask what changes in those systems prevent a repeat.

**Legal traps in hiring.** Hire only against relevance (the scorecard), keep the process standardized across candidates, use non-discriminatory language, and never ask about marital status, plans to have children, pregnancy, date/place of birth, medical conditions, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, or physical/mental disability.

## 10. Sales System

**Three fundamentals of making a sale:** build trust, identify the customer's specific pain, sell results rather than features.

**Building trust** follows the identical pattern used in fundraising and recruiting — early meetings are about the prospect, not your pitch; ask, listen, reflect back, and remember at the next touch. Low-friction ways to get that first meeting without pitching: be explicit ("before we talk about what we do, I'd like to understand your situation first"), ask for very little time (a 10-minute intro call), or invite them to something purely social.

**Customer development questioning** — a three-part structure to surface real pain before presenting any solution: what are their goals; what's blocking them from those goals; what would their ideal solution look like. Use open prompts ("tell me more") and reflect key statements back to confirm understanding. Over enough of these conversations, build a reusable problem/solution statement library per customer segment (format: "For [role] at [company type] who need [X], [product] is a [category] that [does Y], unlike [status quo]").

**Sell results, not features.** Customers care about outcomes, not mechanisms — nobody buys a laptop "because of the chip," they buy it because it helps them do more. DocuSign's pivot illustrates this: reframing from "sign contracts virtually" (a feature nobody cared about) to "close your sales contracts in half the time" (a result) directly preceded their scale-up.

**Integrity and the danger of overselling.** Overselling isn't just an ethics problem — it has three compounding business costs: reputational (customers compare notes, and there are only so many burned bridges before the market catches on), internal (engineering absorbs the resulting pressure to build to an inflated promise), and cultural (sales hires adopt whatever standard leadership models, so overselling at the top propagates through every future hire and becomes very hard to reverse). The alternative is patience: build enough trust that a customer buys despite acknowledged current gaps, trusting you to close them.

**When to build a sales team.** Not before two conditions are both true: you have early proof of product-market fit (a meaningful share of paying customers renew), and you've defined precisely what you're selling and to whom. Founders should be the first and best salespeople — a hire generally can't out-sell a founder on a still-undefined offering.

**Sales team structure** (drawing on Aaron Ross's *Predictable Revenue*, developed at Salesforce): the core insight is that revenue growth is usually blocked by lead generation, not team size — and lead generation and deal-closing are fundamentally different skills that must be organizationally separated (breadth vs. depth). Three roles:
- **Qualifiers / SDRs** — generate and qualify leads, split into Outbound (proactive email/LinkedIn outreach) and Inbound (handling leads who reach out on their own). Compensated base + bonus per qualified lead.
- **Closers / Account Executives** — close leads handed off by Qualifiers. Compensated base + commission. More senior, because relationship-closing skill takes longer to develop — don't burn expensive closer time on lead generation.
- **Farmers / Customer Success** — retain and grow existing accounts; also your best channel for ongoing product feedback. Compensated base + retention bonus or account-growth commission.
Build-out sequence: hire a Qualifier and a Farmer while the founder still closes; only add an AE once lead flow is predictable and farming is in place.

**Common sales-hiring mistakes:** assuming culture/integrity standards don't apply to sales hires (a self-fulfilling prophecy if you hire mercenaries expecting mercenary behavior); skipping training on product/industry/process regardless of a candidate's pedigree; assuming you must hire only senior AEs with track records (relationship-building DNA and process discipline often matter more than tenure); assuming your best individual closer will make a good VP of Sales (managing a pipeline and team requires almost no overlapping skill with closing solo — hire sales management experience for that role specifically).

**Lead generation — three types, each with distinct economics:**
- **Seeds** — word of mouth, referrals, prior relationships. Close fast, high win rate, become your best customers, but can't be proactively scaled beyond building a great product and CS team.
- **Nets** — marketing-driven (events, SEO, whitepapers, ads), optimized for quantity over quality, then qualified by Inbound Reps. Scalable and cost-effective if done well, but real setup/maintenance cost.
- **Spears** — direct outbound targeting by Outbound Reps (email, LinkedIn). Predictable, hyper-targeted, fast results, but requires dedicated headcount and is usually unprofitable below roughly $10K average deal size.
Typical progression: early customers come from Seeds; as you scale, move to Spears before Nets, since Spears produce faster, more targeted results while Nets take longer to build out.

## 11. Marketing and Product-Market Fit

**STP framework — Segment, Target, Promote** (drawing on Bill Aulet's *Disciplined Entrepreneurship*):
- **Segment**: brainstorm every plausible market for your technology/vision without filtering for "correctness" yet, narrow to a top 5-10 using CAC, LTV, and TAM per segment (and values alignment — a customer relationship is like a hiring relationship), then do *primary* research (not just desk research — if a market's already well-documented, you're probably late) on those finalists to pick one beachhead. Use *The Mom's Test* discipline here: ask about past behavior and actual cost of the problem, not opinions about your idea ("when was the last time you hit this problem and what did you do?" beats "what do you think of my app?" and "would you pay for this?"). Three common segment-selection traps: selling to everyone who shows interest; chasing raw TAM without weighing acquisition difficulty or per-customer value (the "China Syndrome" — "if we get even 0.1% of a billion people..."); and over-customizing per customer, which stretches engineering thin and slows overall growth.
- **Target**: build a single, vivid Persona representing your primary end-user (most startups need only one; two-sided marketplaces sometimes need two) — based on real conversations with actual or likely users, never invented from your own assumptions (founders reliably "stack the cards in their favor"). Distinguish the End-User (who uses the product — your primary focus) from the Decision-Making Unit (Champion, Primary Economic Buyer, Influencers/Vetoes — who approves the purchase). Then map the Full Life Cycle: how the persona recognizes the problem, finds you, vets you, buys, onboards, uses, perceives value, pays, gets support, and refers/expands — typically as a flowchart.
- **Promote**: only begin once PMF is confirmed and Segment/Target are validated — promotion before PMF just wastes spend on the wrong message to the wrong audience. Every promotional decision should trace back to the Persona, and every marketing dollar should tie to a measurable result, even qualitative channels like conference talks (track something, e.g., qualified leads generated).

**Competitive analysis.** Dismissing competitors as inferior "because it's yours" is an emotional trap, not a strategic judgment — competitors are working the same problem full-time and have real lessons to offer. Block a full week per quarter to: use their product directly (log what they do better, feed it into your roadmap), talk to their customers (what do they like/wish existed — feeds roadmap and sales collateral), and reverse-engineer their pricing model logic.

**Recognizing real Product-Market Fit.** Ask customers — they'll tell you, and they'll vote with renewals, repeat purchases, and referrals. For B2B, an initial signed contract is not proof of PMF (it's often just a budgeted "test"); PMF is confirmed at the full long-term renewal. For B2C, PMF shows up as repeat orders, subscription renewal, high NPS, and organic word of mouth. The classic failure mode is declaring PMF too early, then over-hiring and over-spending on promotion before the product is actually validated — large teams iterate slower, and morale collapses once the team realizes PMF wasn't real. Discipline: keep the team at 3-6 people until PMF is genuinely achieved (e.g., a CEO plus 2 engineers, or a CEO plus 3 engineers, a designer, and an ops person).

**PR practices** for early-stage companies: skip PR agencies (journalists prefer talking to the founder directly, and agencies mostly just cold-email on your behalf — bring it in-house once you truly outgrow doing it yourself); think like a journalist chasing an audience-exciting story, not just "about us" coverage (proprietary data is a strong hook); cluster press into a tight burst (e.g., 3 articles in one week) rather than spreading it thin over months — concentrated coverage triggers secondary pickup/virality and lets your name "rise above the noise" for prospective customers noticing you multiple times in a short window; build outreach at scale (compile relevant journalist lists, find contact info, send personalized outreach); and consider becoming a contributor at relevant publications once you have some press and a blog track record, since publications need constant content and will approve credible contributors.

## 12. Departments, Operations, and CEO Sustainability

**Executive / Chief of Staff.** When you're consistently missing your own weekly goals, or completing them but with heavy energy drain, hire a Chief of Staff: organized, execution-oriented, non-technical, 2+ years post-college (ideally trained at a top consulting firm or bank). Their job is to shadow the CEO until they understand how she operates, then progressively take tasks off her plate — continuing until the CEO is left working only in her Zone of Genius. A COS with (or who develops) management skill can grow into a COO.

**Product.** The Product Manager role requires both the social skill to sit with customers and enough technical fluency to know what's feasible (a PM background as an engineer helps but isn't mandatory if the PM/engineering relationship is strong). Core responsibilities: sit directly with customers to surface real problems, translate them into a feature list ordered by customer value versus engineering cost; run the cross-functional Product meeting (Engineering + Sales/Marketing) to finalize build order — the general heuristic is high-value/low-cost first, low-value/high-cost last, and high-value/high-cost generally beats low-value/low-cost for the contested middle; and produce wireframes and specs precise enough that engineers know the exact target while retaining freedom over how to get there. Product should report directly to the CEO (never to Engineering, which skews toward technical convenience over customer voice; if it can't report to the CEO, Sales/Marketing is the next-best home).

**Engineering.** The central challenge is management, not code, once the team passes 3-4 engineers — a strong individual engineer is frequently a weak engineering manager. Hire engineering management specifically from companies known for excellent engineering management (Google, Meta, Apple), even at a premium. Adding average engineers to a small excellent team often produces a net productivity *drag* through coordination overhead unless the manager is exceptional. Adopt a real tracking tool (JIRA is the standard) once you cross roughly 10-25 engineers; use something lighter before that.

**People / HR.** Outsource HR compliance early via either an online broker (e.g., handling documentation and benefits purchasing) or a PEO (Professional Employer Organization), where your team members formally become employees of the PEO — this gets you large-company benefits pricing and shifts employment-law liability to the PEO. Recommended until 100-150 employees, after which in-house HR becomes both affordable and more responsive. Whatever benefits you choose, remember they're funded with pre-tax dollars for the employee, so factor that into total-comp comparisons against market cash offers.

**Finance.** Outsource accounting early (cloud tools like QuickBooks plus an outsourced firm), but prefer a firm willing to send someone onsite periodically — communication and oversight quality improve substantially in person. Prefer an outsourced *CFO* firm over a plain bookkeeping firm — a good CFO-level partner can do both the accounting and the forward-looking financial/operational projections (headcount plans, space needs, metrics) that investors specifically look for as a signal of operational maturity, often for the same or similar cost as bookkeeping alone.

**Legal.** Use a large, investor-recognized firm (Wilson Sonsini, Cooley, Orrick, Fenwick, etc.) only for documenting priced equity rounds, where investor comfort with the firm's brand is worth the premium. For everything else, retain a solo-practitioner or boutique outsourced General Counsel who comes onsite and only bills for time physically spent working with you (require written pre-approval for any off-site hours) — this alone prevents runaway billing, since large firms remote from the company both bill at higher rates and tend to log more hours per issue than an onsite counterpart. For the one moment a large firm is unavoidable — the first institutional priced round — control costs with a structured negotiation process rather than unlimited lawyer-to-lawyer back-and-forth: once term sheet basics are agreed, schedule one blocked 4-8 hour session with the four essential people only (company decision-maker, lead investor decision-maker, company lawyer, investor lawyer); one side's lawyer drafts first, the other responds in writing beforehand; in the session, the *business people* negotiate point by point directly while lawyers only clarify meaning (not negotiate); once agreed, lawyers write the final language same-session. This compresses a typical 45-60 day, $100K+-per-side legal process into about a week and under $15K per side, because the multiplying cascade of lawyer-to-client-to-lawyer email/call rounds (each separately billable) never happens.

**CEO personal sustainability.**
- *Sleep*: the body cycles through roughly 90-minute light/deep/light sleep phases; grogginess comes from being woken mid-deep-cycle (usually by an alarm), not from total hours — 7.5 hours ending on a light-sleep boundary can feel better than 8.5 hours cut off by an alarm mid-REM. A sleep-cycle tracking app can help time wake-ups to light-sleep phases. If you're waking with a specific worry, that's usually your mind protecting against forgetting something urgent — a disciplined GTD capture habit removes the need for your brain to hold onto it, which itself improves sleep.
- *Liquidity*: as company equity grows, most founder net worth becomes an illiquid, single-asset concentration — a general risk guideline is no more than ~25% of net worth in illiquid/alternative assets. Two psychologically meaningful thresholds recur across founders: $10M liquid (initial sense of safety, though disaster-scenario thinking often persists) and $100M liquid (where that anxiety typically resolves into genuine abundance, and money beyond that point adds little to quality of life while sometimes adding maintenance burden). Practical implication: sell to secondary buyers as soon as equity has real value, working toward that $10-100M liquid range rather than waiting for a full exit.
- *Where to hold it*: avoid commercial and investment banks for large personal liquidity — both structures let the institution keep the upside on what they do with your deposited capital while you bear the downside risk if the institution fails. A brokerage (e.g., Schwab, Fidelity) keeps assets in your name and under your direction; even a brokerage can go bankrupt, but the practical answer is to first park liquidity in U.S. Treasuries specifically (Treasuries are held directly for the beneficial owner, not in the brokerage's custody pool, so they aren't caught up in a brokerage bankruptcy).
- *Investing*: David Swensen's *Unconventional Success* is treated as the reference text — professional active managers rarely produce genuine after-fee alpha versus public markets, so the recommended default is a low-cost index allocation (Swensen's rough split: ~30% US equities, ~30% non-US equities, ~5% real estate, ~25% US Treasuries) rebalanced regularly, using an automated rebalancing tool to remove the friction of manual trading.
