# Coaching for Performance — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing coaching/training programs, or advising on management practice.*
*Book: "Coaching for Performance: The Principles and Practices of Coaching and Leadership" — Sir John Whitmore*

---

## 1. Core Philosophy: The Inner Game and Performance = Potential − Interference

Whitmore traces modern coaching to Timothy Gallwey's *The Inner Game of Tennis* (1974). Gallwey's central claim: the "opponent within one's own head" (self-doubt, fear, over-trying, self-criticism) is a bigger obstacle to performance than any external opponent or technical flaw. His Inner Game Equation is the foundation of everything that follows in the book:

**Performance = Potential − Interference** (P = p − i)

Coaching therefore has two levers, not one: grow potential, and strip out interference. Instruction from an outside "expert" often does the opposite — it adds interference (self-consciousness, dependence on being told) while doing little to grow real potential, because it isn't tailored to the individual's own body, mind, or way of thinking.

**Coaching's actual definition** (Whitmore's, used throughout): *unlocking people's potential to maximize their own performance. It is helping them to learn rather than teaching them.* This is explicitly not mentoring (passing down the mentor's own knowledge and experience — mentoring can undermine self-belief) and not the "here are the tools, go find out for yourself" abdication of responsibility either.

**Maslow's Hierarchy, reframed for coaching**: physiological → safety → belonging → esteem → self-actualization. Whitmore relabels the two esteem-related needs in a way that becomes load-bearing for the rest of the book:
- **Status and recognition** — esteem sought *from others*. People at this level chase pay, title, and visible markers of status.
- **Self-belief** — esteem generated *internally*, by one's own standards. This is the mindset shift Whitmore calls becoming **independent**.
- **Self-actualizing** (a continuous process, not a state — Maslow deliberately used the -ing form) — driven by meaning and purpose, contribution beyond the self. This is the mindset of **interdependence**.

The single universal internal block to performance, found consistently across Whitmore's workshops worldwide, is **fear** — variously fear of failure, self-doubt, lack of confidence. The average estimate people give, across thousands of workshop participants worldwide, of how much of their own potential they bring to work is **40 percent**. This gap is the addressable market for coaching.

**Self-belief mechanics**: self-belief is not built by conferring prestige or privilege (symbolic, not substantial). It's built by being trusted to make real choices, and by accumulating successes that a person can attribute to their own effort — not to someone rescuing or directing them. A leader's underlying belief about a person's potential leaks out regardless of words used: Albert Mehrabian's research (on communicating feelings/attitudes specifically) found roughly 7% of a message is in the words, 38% in tone, 55% in facial expression — meaning a leader's true opinion of someone's capability is transmitted whether or not it's ever said aloud.

## 2. The Performance Curve: Four Stages of Culture

Whitmore's "Performance Curve" (new to the Fifth Edition) is a culture-mapping model built by extending safety-maturity research (originally used by "high reliability organizations" like Linde, DuPont, oil and airline companies to track safety behavior) into a general theory of organizational performance. It draws on Maslow's Hierarchy, Gallwey's Inner Game equation, Stephen Covey's dependence-independence-interdependence framework, and William Schutz's team-development theory, mapping them all onto the same developmental sequence.

**The four stages** (each stage = an overall cultural mindset):
- **Impulsive** — the lowest-performance stage; reactive, unstructured, "things will happen." Little planning, little ownership.
- **Dependent** — low-to-medium performance. Mindset: "if only they would do what I tell them." Rules-following, blame-prone, command-and-control. People wait to be told.
- **Independent** — the "I am a high performer" mindset. Individuals take responsibility for themselves and may perform well alone, but often at the cost of overwork, self-imposed pressure, and poor collaboration — a leader here tends to fix things personally rather than developing others.
- **Interdependent** — the high-performance stage. Mindset: "we are truly successful together." Trust, collaboration, mutual accountability, shared ownership of outcomes. This is where coaching leadership lives and where The Performance Curve says organizations should aim to operate.

Key evidence cited for the model: Linde AG (a "high reliability organization") measured itself as sitting in the dependent stage using DuPont's Bradley Curve, then introduced a coaching-based safety leadership program (replacing blame/judgment with curiosity and partnering) and saw a **74 percent decrease in safety incidents**. Leadership behavior alone has been shown (Hay Group research, among others) to affect bottom-line performance by up to **30 percent**.

**Practical use of the model**: it's a diagnostic — for a coach or leader to locate where a team, an organization, or even a single conversation is currently operating, and to identify what mindset shift (not just what tactic) is needed to move up a stage. Awareness of "which stage are we operating from right now" is itself considered curative, consistent with the book's overall thesis that awareness activates natural change.

## 3. Coaching as Emotional Intelligence in Practice

Whitmore's framing: *transformational coaching is the practice of emotional intelligence.* He cites Daniel Goleman's research that EQ is roughly twice as important as IQ (66% to 34%) in predicting workplace success generally, and accounts for over 85% of "star performance" differentiation among top leaders specifically.

**Three domains of awareness** that build emotional intelligence through coaching:
- **Awareness of self** — recognizing your own tendencies, internal interferences, and biases so you can choose responses rather than react.
- **Awareness of others** — spotting people's strengths, interferences, and motivations to manage relationships and collaborate well.
- **Awareness of organization** — aligning individual, team, and organizational goals; understanding your impact on the wider culture.

**A diagnostic exercise from the book** (used to make the abstract concept of EQ concrete): recall someone from your youth you loved being with (not a parent) — a grandparent, teacher, role model. What did they do that you liked? How did you feel? Run across the world in Performance Consultants workshops, the answers are remarkably consistent: the person *listened, believed in me, challenged me, trusted and respected me, gave me full attention, treated me as an equal.* The feelings reported: special, valued, confident, safe/cared for, supported, enthusiastic, self-belief. This list functions as an experiential definition of what emotionally intelligent leadership behavior actually looks like from the receiving end.

**Spiritual intelligence (SQ)**: introduced as a companion concept (Ian Mitroff, Elizabeth Denton; Danah Zohar, Ian Marshall) — not religious, but "the basic desire to find ultimate meaning and purpose in one's life and to live an integrated life." This connects to Maslow's self-actualization level and sets up the book's later material on meaning and purpose (Section 10 below).

**The mindset-choice exercise**: Whitmore has readers try on three mindsets toward the same colleague in sequence — "this person *is* a problem," "this person *has* a problem," "this person is on a learning journey, capable, resourceful, full of potential" — and notice the different feelings each evokes. The point: mindset is chosen, like colored glasses, and a coach's core discipline is deliberately choosing the third.

## 4. The Leader as Coach: Telling vs. Coaching

**The traditional management spectrum** (a line from autocratic to laissez-faire, all of it distinct from coaching):
- **Dictates** — quick, gives the dictator an illusion of control, but demotivates, produces poor recall (research — cited from IBM training studies — shows people retain far less of what they are simply told than what they discover or do themselves), and breeds covert resistance.
- **Persuades/sells** — the boss's idea is "sold" to the team; looks more democratic but the outcome is identical to dictating — little real input is taken on board.
- **Discusses** — genuine pooling of resources; the leader may follow a path other than their own preferred one. Attractive but can be slow and can produce indecision.
- **Abdicates** — the leader walks away and leaves people to it. Risky for both sides: the leader still holds ultimate responsibility, but people often lack the awareness to perform well without support, and forced "freedom" doesn't generate real ownership.

**Coaching sits on a different plane entirely**, not a midpoint on this spectrum. It combines the benefits of both extremes (full engagement of the leader's oversight + full ownership by the coachee) with the risks of neither. Because the coachee generates their own action plan by answering questions, the leader ends up *more* informed (knows not just the plan but the thinking behind it) than if they had simply told the person what to do — creating genuine alignment rather than compliance.

**Whitmore's decision rule for when to coach vs. tell**, built around five criteria:
- **Time** — if speed is paramount (a live crisis), telling is faster in the short term, but it creates long-term dependency.
- **Quality** — if the quality of the outcome matters most, coaching for high awareness and responsibility delivers more.
- **Learning** — if maximizing learning and retention matters (e.g., someone doing a task for the first time), coaching wins decisively.
- **Commitment** — if genuine buy-in is required (not just compliance), coaching creates ownership; telling creates resistance or, at best, grudging compliance.
- **Engagement** — if retention of high-potential or younger talent matters, coaching aligns individual wants/needs with organizational mission and creates meaning.

In most real workplace situations, all of time, quality, and learning are relevant simultaneously — but in practice, time dominates and learning is relegated to a poor third, which Whitmore identifies as *why* organizations underperform relative to potential and why leaders struggle to give up telling.

**A leader's job, reduced to its essence**: get the job done, and develop people. The paradox: coaching appears slower up front but frees leaders from firefighting over time, because developing employees shoulder more responsibility, which is what actually buys leaders the bandwidth for strategic work.

## 5. Building a Coaching Mindset: Partnership, Belief, and Trust

Coaching is a conversation between equals — the ICF's own definition is "partnering in a thought-provoking and creative process to maximize personal and professional potential." A coaching ethos is a genuine belief in the capability, resourcefulness, and potential of yourself and others, focused on strengths, solutions, and future success rather than weakness, problems, or past performance.

**Self-motivation**: carrot-and-stick motivation is losing effectiveness as pay increases and promotion opportunities shrink globally. The only sustainable motivator is self-motivation — which by definition cannot be imposed, only unlocked. This requires believing every individual is fully capable and resourceful, and abandoning the "I'm the expert / this is best practice" mentality (Whitmore's example: Dick Fosbury's backward high-jump technique beat "best practice" precisely because his coach didn't insist on the standard technique).

**Belief in potential — the "shell" mechanism**: a leader who only assigns tasks a person is already proven at (staying inside their "shell") reinforces and thickens that shell over time. Growth requires assigning a real stretch task and coaching the person through it, actively suspending the leader's own limiting beliefs about what that person can do.

**Conscious working agreements**: partnership requires explicitly designing (not defaulting into) how two people or a team will work together, ideally at the *start* of a relationship or project. Core questions (Question Bag 2 in the book's toolkit):
- What would the dream/success look like for us working together?
- What would the nightmare/worst case look like?
- What's the best way for us to work together to achieve the dream?
- What do we need to be mindful of to avoid the nightmare?
- What permissions does each of us want from the other?
- What will we do when things get hard?
- (Revisit periodically): What is working and what is not? What do we need to change?

**Permission** is a small but load-bearing coaching behavior — asking before offering a suggestion, sharing an opinion, or shifting into a new leadership style ("Would it be helpful if I shared what works for me?" / "Can I add something to that?"). It hands control to the other person and validates what they've already said, which is often enough on its own to change the tone of a meeting.

**Get curious, not critical**: the antidote to blame and criticism (both of which are shown — citing John Gottman's marital research on the "Four Horsemen" — to predict relationship breakdown with over 90% accuracy when persistent) is curiosity. This applies as much to self-criticism ("I'm my own worst critic") as to criticism of others; recognizing and managing the internal critical voice (Gallwey's "opponent within one's own head") is fundamental to coaching practice.

## 6. Awareness and Responsibility

These are the two pillars the entire practice of coaching stands on — more important, Whitmore stresses repeatedly, than any specific model or mnemonic (including GROW itself).

**Awareness** = high-quality, relevant, ideally self-generated input. Defined via the driving analogy: input → process → output; the better the quality and quantity of input a person receives (and self-generates, rather than simply being told), the better their output. Awareness is inherently curative: *"I am able to control only that of which I am aware. That of which I am unaware controls me."* This isn't mystical — becoming aware of a bodily tension (a furrowed brow, a tight jaw) tends to release it automatically, without effort or instruction; the same principle scales to complex behaviors and organizational blind spots.

**Responsibility** = true ownership, which only arises from *choice*, not from being told, ordered, or "given" responsibility. Whitmore's construction-site example: told to fetch a ladder that turns out not to be where expected, a worker just reports back ("there's no ladder"). Asked instead who is *willing* to get the ladder, the same worker — now having chosen — will actively search elsewhere when the ladder isn't where expected, because their own self-esteem is now on the line. The mechanism: **choice creates responsibility; responsibility (not obligation) creates performance.**

**Blame's mechanics**: unsolicited advice transfers responsibility from the advice-giver back to the receiver in a way that backfires — if it fails, the receiver blames the advisor, having never owned the decision. In a workplace, when advice becomes a command, ownership drops to zero, and can produce covert resentment or even sabotage.

**"Awareness leads to skill"**: in physical/technical skill development, imposing a leader's or "the book's" way of doing something suppresses the individual's own attributes and keeps them dependent on the expert. Raising awareness of a person's own bodily/mental sensations lets them find their own optimized version of a skill — more durable and more owned than an externally imposed "correct" technique.

## 7. Powerful Questions and Active Listening

These are the two foundational *skills* that operationalize awareness and responsibility. Without them, any model (including GROW) is empty.

**Why questions beat instructions**: telling someone to "watch the ball" or "relax" does not reliably produce the behavior — commands don't compel engagement. A well-constructed question does, because it can't be answered without actually doing the thing (e.g., "Which way is the ball spinning as it comes toward you?" cannot be answered without watching closely).

**Four effects of a genuinely powerful question**:
- Compels the person to actually engage/observe (can't answer otherwise).
- Demands a higher order of focus for an accurate answer — raising input quality.
- Elicits descriptive, not judgmental, answers — avoiding self-criticism.
- Creates a feedback loop for the coach, who can verify the quality of the coachee's attention/answer.

**Open vs. closed questions**: open questions ("What do you want to achieve?" "What's happening at the moment?" "What's stopping you?") promote awareness; closed (yes/no) questions don't even require engaging the brain.

**Interrogative word guidance**: favor "what," "when," "who," "how much," "how many" — these seek facts. Avoid "why" (implies criticism, evokes defensiveness) and use "how" sparingly, since both provoke analysis rather than observation, and analysis/awareness are close to mutually exclusive mental modes. If a "why" or "how" question is genuinely needed, reframe it factually: "What were the reasons...?" instead of "Why did you...?"; "What are the steps...?" instead of "How did you...?"

**Focus on detail, progressively**: questions should start broad, then narrow ("What do you want?" → "What do you *really* want?" → "*Exactly* what is happening now?"). This mirrors the "square foot of carpet" analogy — a magnifying glass (deeper questioning) keeps attention and interest engaged far longer than a surface glance.

**Follow the coachee's agenda, not the coach's**: the coach's questions should track the coachee's own interest and train of thought, even into apparent dead ends (trust they'll discover it themselves, or ask "What would it be helpful for us to look at next?"). Avoiding a topic can also be worth naming gently: "I notice you haven't mentioned X — is there a reason for this?"

**Avoid leading questions and criticism-flavored questions** (e.g., "Why on earth did you do that?") — leading questions signal the coach doesn't really trust the process, and criticism reduces trust immediately.

**Summary checklist — powerful questions**: create awareness and responsibility; follow the coachee's interest; inspire creativity/resourcefulness; increase possibilities/vision; are goal-oriented and solution-focused; are non-judgmental; compel attention, thought, observation; demand focus, detail, precision; demand answers showing quality of thought; are supportive/challenging; create a feedback loop.

**Active listening skills** (a working taxonomy):
- **Reflecting/mirroring** — repeating the person's exact words back.
- **Paraphrasing** — restating in slightly different words without changing meaning.
- **Summarizing** — restating more briefly without changing meaning.
- **Clarifying** — distilling the essence and adding something noticed intuitively (tone, body language) not said in words: "It sounds like... what would you say?"
- **Encouraging self-expression** — building trust/intimacy to invite openness.
- **Suspending judgment, criticism, and attachment** — keeping an open mind; judgment triggers defensiveness.
- **Listening for potential** — focusing on capability and strength, not past performance or "problem" framing.
- **Listening with heart** — reading non-verbal signals (tone, phrasing, facial expression, body language) for the underlying feeling/intent.

**Transference and countertransference**: any hierarchical relationship (leader/report, coach/coachee) carries the risk that one or both parties unconsciously replay childhood patterns around authority — giving away power ("they know best, I'll make myself small") or covertly rebelling/sabotaging. **Countertransference** is when the person in authority unconsciously perpetuates dependence or rebellion from their own history. Good coaches recognize the risk in themselves and consciously work to empower the coachee rather than reinforce the pattern.

## 8. The GROW Model: Goal, Reality, Options, Will

**Origins**: developed by Whitmore and Graham Alexander in the mid-1980s while consulting to McKinsey (which had commissioned an underpinning framework for the "Inner Game"-based coaching work being done on tennis courts as part of leadership programs). Early attempts (a "7S Coaching Model," mirroring McKinsey's existing 7S Framework) proved unwieldy; the four-stage structure that emerged was simple, action-and-outcome-focused, and became the GROW acronym. First published in the 1992 first edition of this book.

**GROW is a sequence, not a checklist** — and the order (Goal before Reality) is deliberate and counterintuitive. Goals set only from current reality tend to be reactive, limited by past performance, or extrapolative rather than creative; setting an inspiring vision-based goal first, then examining reality, produces more ambitious and motivating targets. GROW is also explicitly recursive: coaches move back and forth between stages as new information surfaces (a vague initial goal often needs re-sharpening once reality is understood; options must be checked back against the goal; final "what/when" commitments must be checked against both).

**Critical warning in the book**: *GROW without awareness and responsibility is not coaching.* An autocratic boss can walk through G-R-O-W mechanically (state a target, berate the team about a shortfall, unilaterally rule out certain options, dictate the action) without asking a single real question — this is not coaching, just a script.

### G — Goal

Two layers of goal-setting happen in every session:
- **The goal for the session itself** — "What would you like to get out of this time together?" "We have half an hour — where would you like to have got to by then?"
- **The goal for the issue at hand** — distinguishing goal types:
  - **Dream goal** — the inspirational, often childlike vision that ignites action in the first place (e.g., "I am going to climb Everest").
  - **End goal** — the final objective (become market leader, land the account, win the medal) — usually *not* fully within your control, since it depends partly on others/competitors.
  - **Performance goal** — a specific, controllable level of performance believed to give a strong chance of hitting the end goal (e.g., "95% of production passes QC first time"). This is what people can actually commit to and take responsibility for, since it's within their control.
  - **Process goal** (or work goal) — "How much are you willing to invest in the process?" — the training/preparation commitment underneath the performance goal.

Qualities of a good goal, per the book's three mnemonics:
- **SMART** — Specific, Measurable, Agreed, Realistic, Timeframed.
- **PURE** — Positively stated, Understood, Relevant, Ethical.
- **CLEAR** — Challenging, Legal, Environmentally sound, Appropriate, Recorded.

Ownership matters more than the leader's preferred number: if a team sets a lower target than the leader wants, overriding it usually demotivates more than it gains — better to accept the team's figure, or coach them upward by exploring and dismantling their own perceived obstacles, so they retain ownership of whatever figure is finally agreed.

Positive framing matters mechanically, not just motivationally: a negatively stated goal ("don't finish bottom of the league") focuses attention on the very thing to be avoided — reframe to the positive equivalent ("aim for 4th or higher").

### R — Reality

The purpose is to establish the current situation with maximum **objectivity** — recognizing that true objectivity doesn't fully exist, only degrees of it, and that the coach's job is to strip out distortion from opinions, judgments, and fears (both the coachee's and the coach's own).

Key discipline: **descriptive language, not judgmental language** — this keeps detachment and reduces the self-criticism that distorts perception. Reality questions follow the same "watch the ball" rules as all powerful questions: they compel engagement, demand high-resolution detail, seek descriptive (not evaluative) answers, and create a feedback loop. "What" / "when" / "where" / "who" / "how much" dominate this phase; "why" and "how" are used sparingly since premature analysis biases fact-gathering (an explicit parallel drawn to police investigation).

One near-universally useful reality question: *"What action have you taken on this so far?"* followed by *"What were the effects of that action?"* — this often reveals that a person has done a great deal of thinking about a problem but taken no actual action.

Reality has two components to probe:
- **External reality** — organizational strategy, policies, politics, unwritten rules, power dynamics, behavioral norms.
- **Internal reality** — the coachee's own thoughts, feelings, beliefs, values, attitudes.

A caution on depth: transformational coaching addresses cause, not symptom, and can surface issues deeper than expected. If a coach is not adequately trained, or suspects deep-seated personal/psychological origins, the right move is to bring in a professional — one distinction offered between coaching and counseling is that coaching is proactive/future-focused, counseling reactive/past-focused.

It's common for reality-stage questioning alone to surface the answer before Options or Will are even reached — so coaches should resist rushing past Goal and Reality.

### O — Options

Purpose: generate the widest possible *quantity* of alternatives before worrying about feasibility — quantity, not immediate quality, is the goal, because the brainstorming process itself unlocks creative thinking that a narrower search would miss.

**Negative assumptions block option generation** — Whitmore lists common ones ("it can't be done," "they'd never agree," "we can't afford the time") and the antidote question: *"What if there were no obstacles, what would you do then?"* — and, targeted at specific blockers: "What if you had a large enough budget?" "What if you had more employees?" "What if you knew the answer — what would it be?"

**The nine-dot exercise** is used to illustrate self-imposed constraints that aren't actually part of the stated problem (e.g., assuming you must stay inside the implied square). The takeaway for coaching: identifying the false assumption is usually the whole unlock — the resolution follows easily once the assumption is named.

**Techniques for expanding option generation**:
- Perspective-shift questions: "What would you do if you were the leader / the CEO / the world's leading expert?" "Think of the person you most admire — what would they do?"
- Metaphor: invite the coachee to create a metaphor for the situation and develop it fully (without immediately mapping it back to "reality") — often the resolution shows up inside the metaphor itself.
- Coach-offered brainstorming: only after the coachee has exhausted their own list, and offered with explicit non-attachment ("I have a couple of ideas — let me know if you'd like to hear them").

Once a list exists, sort by benefits/costs, or a simple 1–10 "how much do you like this option" rating, before moving to Will.

### W — Will

The purpose of Will is to convert discussion into decision — Whitmore chose "Will" deliberately over a simpler "What," to foreground intention and commitment, not just a plan on paper. This stage splits into two distinct sub-stages:

**Stage 1 — Accountability Set-up.** Core questions, in order of importance:
- *What will you do?* (distinct from "what could you do" — this is decision time, not brainstorming.)
- *When will you do it?* — described as the toughest and most important question; requires an exact time/date, not "sometime next year." For recurring actions, specify the interval.
- *How will this action serve your goal?* — a check-back against both session goal and long-term goal before finalizing.
- *What obstacles might you meet along the way?* — pre-empting both external and internal (e.g., faintheartedness) obstacles.
- *How will I know? Who needs to know?* — ensuring the right people are informed, not left to find out secondhand.
- *What support do you need? How and when will you get it?*
- *What other considerations do you have?* — a catch-all so nothing is left unaddressed.
- *How committed are you to this action, on a scale of 1–10?* — rating intention, not the certainty of the outcome (which may depend on others).
- *What prevents it from being a 10?* — if below 8, either shrink the task/extend the timeframe to raise the rating, or the coachee should cross the action off the list, since sub-8 commitment rarely converts to follow-through.

A written record should always exist and be confirmed by the coachee as accurate — this is the coachee's plan, and they must own it.

**Stage 2 — Follow-up and Feedback.** The frame is *checking in, not checking up* — maintaining trust so the coachee will proactively flag deviations before a deadline rather than concealing them. Ties to the well-known **70:20:10 model** for adult learning: roughly 70% of learning for successful leaders happens through on-the-job experience, 20% from others, only 10% from formal training — which is the underlying rationale for why follow-up coaching (not classroom instruction) is the highest-leverage development lever available to a leader.

**The five levels of feedback** (A least helpful, E most productive):
- **A** — Personalized criticism ("You are useless.") — devastates self-esteem, provides nothing actionable.
- **B** — Judgment of the work, not the person ("This report is useless.") — still damaging, still no actionable information.
- **C** — Specific but coach-owned observation ("The content was clear, but the layout was too down-market for the audience.") — some actionable information, but the coach retains ownership of the evaluation, so learning is limited.
- **D** — Open but vague question ("How do you feel about the report?") — creates ownership but often produces a non-answer ("Fine") rather than useful description.
- **E** — A structured sequence of open, non-judgmental questions ("What are you most pleased with?" "If you could do it again, what would you do differently?" "What are you learning?") — the only level that reliably produces detailed, owned, learning-generating description. This is the level coaching aims for.

**The GROW Feedback Framework** — a complete feedback conversation structured on GROW's own four letters, with one golden rule: *the coachee shares first, the coach adds their perspective second*, at every step:
- **G — Set intention**: "What do you want to get out of this?" / coach adds: "I want..."
- **R — Recognize** (focus on the positive first): "What went well? What are you most proud of? What specific strengths did you use?" / coach adds acknowledgment of what worked.
- **O — Improve**: "If you could do it again, what would you do differently? What got in the way? How would you overcome this next time?" / coach may offer a suggestion, with permission.
- **W — Learn**: "What is the learning here? What will you do differently next time? Where else would you apply this?" / coach shares their own learning too.

This framework, deployed at scale (e.g., cited case: a global financial-services client rolling it out to 1,500 leaders), is credited with measurably raising employee-survey scores specifically on "I receive regular, useful feedback" — reframing feedback from a judgment event into a joint-learning conversation.

## 9. Coaching Applications: 1:1 Sessions, Teams, Lean, and Safety

**Formal 1:1 (executive) coaching engagements** — structural guidance:
- Recommended engagement length: roughly **six months**, to allow real practice and sustained behavior change; a shorter "laser coaching" format (three 60-minute virtual sessions on one specific challenge) exists for narrower needs.
- Format options: face-to-face (e.g., 6 sessions × 120 min, monthly); virtual/phone (e.g., 12 × 60 min, biweekly); or mixed. Format and length vary by region (e.g., virtual-first where commute times are extreme; longer face-to-face sessions common in some regions).
- **Chemistry meeting** — a free first meeting to check mutual fit before committing.
- **Confidentiality** is foundational and must be explicitly established — the coach must be clear who engaged them (a leader, HR, the individual directly) and who they're accountable to, and all communication with sponsors/leaders about the coaching should route *through* the coachee, to keep empowering the coachee's own relationship with the organization rather than substituting for it.
- **Foundation session checklist**: format/logistics; conscious agreements (permissions, assumed support/challenge level, accountabilities); "training" the coachee on what coaching is (not mentoring/consulting/counseling) and that it's a shared responsibility; goal-setting (short and long term); a "potted history" of pivotal life moments to surface patterns; and a discovery of values, strengths, and limiting beliefs.
- **360° feedback** at the start (and optionally midpoint) of a longer engagement creates a baseline to measure development against.

**Coaching teams** — Whitmore uses a four-stage model of team development (the first three drawn from William Schutz's FIRO-B theory of interpersonal behavior):
- **Inclusion** — the anxiety-driven question of whether one belongs; low mental productivity since focus is on emotional safety; the leader's own openness sets the norm others will mirror.
- **Assertion** — a phase of competing for status/role/territory ("the pecking order"); can be productive individually but at the expense of cohesion; most teams never progress past this stage; a good leader lets challenges to their authority happen rather than clamping down.
- **Cooperation** — genuine mutual support (a member's bad day draws help, not indifference or quiet celebration); risk of becoming too comfortable and suppressing healthy dissent if overdone.
- **Co-creation** — the team recognizes it's greater than the sum of its parts and becomes a vehicle for organizational transformation, not just task completion.

This maps directly onto Maslow's hierarchy (belonging → inclusion; esteem-from-others → assertion; self-esteem → independence/"do their own thing"; self-actualizing → co-creation) and onto Bruce Tuckman's forming-storming-norming-performing labels, and onto The Performance Curve's dependent/independent/interdependent stages.

Sample **team-level GROW questions** (the coach's lens shifts from the individual to the collective "we"):
- Goal: "What is our goal? If this were a success, what would be different for our stakeholders? If we worked together in the best possible way, what would that look like?"
- Reality: "What strengths do we have as a team? What challenges (internal and external) might we face? On a scale of 1–10, how ready are we?"
- Options: "How can we get more prepared? Who can be our allies? What can we do?"
- Will: "What will we do as a team? What will we do individually?"

**Coaching for Lean performance** — Lean (Toyota Production System) and coaching are framed as mutually reinforcing: Lean's continuous-improvement cycle requires people to step from their comfort zone into a "learning zone," which is exactly what coaching is built to support (balancing challenge and support so people can tolerate the discomfort of experimentation). Lean's **Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA)** cycle maps directly onto GROW: Plan ≈ Goal, Do ≈ action/Will, Check ≈ Reality/reviewing, Act ≈ standardizing the new learning. The "kaizen" principle ("good enough never is") mirrors coaching's core belief that people hold more potential than they currently express.

**Coaching for safety performance** — the sharpest illustrated contrast between instruction and coaching in the book (a forklift-driver incident dialogue). An instructive/directive manager response (lecturing, listing every infraction, threatening consequences) produces surface compliance without understanding *why* something was dangerous — meaning the behavior likely repeats when the manager isn't watching. A coaching response instead: stops the unsafe behavior immediately, then asks open questions to build the driver's own awareness of what happened ("What are you noticing about your driving?"), moves to closed questions only to establish specific facts, has the driver articulate the actual risk in their own words, and — critically — reframes any "I must" statement into "I will," converting externally imposed obligation into internally owned commitment. The claimed effect on real HRO clients (e.g., Linde): a 74% reduction in incidents from adopting this style organization-wide.

## 10. Advanced Coaching: Meaning, Purpose, and Subpersonalities

**Meaning vs. purpose — a deliberate distinction**: *meaning* is the significance ascribed to an event *in hindsight* (psychological); *purpose* is the intent behind a chosen course of action, forward-looking (more of a spiritual/existential concept). Whitmore frames the broader business context: rising numbers of employees (and job candidates) now ask about an organization's social purpose and ethics before joining — a "crisis of meaning" playing out at both the individual and organizational level, driven partly by generational shift and partly by environmental/social pressures on business itself.

**From victim to creator**: the single biggest unlock toward meaning and purpose work is recognizing that one's current reality is an *opportunity*, not a fate imposed from outside — the shift from being a victim of circumstance to being the author of what happens next. Coaching questions used for this ("Imagine that challenge contains the perfect gift you need to grow — what is that gift? What are you grateful for? Who will you become to rise to this challenge?") deliberately reframe adversity as material for growth rather than something merely to be endured.

**Two Dimensions of Growth** (a psychosynthesis-derived model): a horizontal axis of material success/psychological integration, and a vertical axis of values/spiritual aspiration. Pure horizontal movement (all achievement, no values-examination) or pure vertical movement (values-driven but ungrounded in practical competence) both eventually produce a "crisis" — a **crisis of meaning** (excess knowledge/achievement outrunning values) or a **crisis of duality** (idealistic vision colliding with mundane practical reality). The 45-degree line between the two axes is offered as a rough proxy for *wisdom* — neither pure accumulation nor pure aspiration, but an integration of both.

**Subpersonalities** (from Roberto Assagioli's psychosynthesis, developed 1911; Assagioli was Freud's first Italian analyst before breaking from Freud's more pathology-focused model, as Jung also did): the idea that each person contains multiple internally competing "parts" (e.g., the voice that wants to get up and walk on the beach vs. the voice that wants to stay in bed), each often formed in childhood as a strategy to get needs met from parents, later generalized into adult life. Coaching for inner conflict works by naming these parts explicitly (invite the coachee to name their own — "Hero," "Chocoholic," "Victim," etc.) and then interrogating them: *Which is most disruptive? When does it show up? What does it want? Did it get what it wanted, and how did others feel? What's another way to get that need met?*

**Self-mastery**: the developmental end-state is becoming the "conductor of your own orchestra" — able to call on any subpersonality without being unconsciously run by any one of them. In psychosynthesis terms this observer-position is called the **"I,"** described as a center of pure consciousness (awareness) and pure will (responsibility) — explicitly the same two qualities (awareness + responsibility) that anchor the entire book's definition of coaching. Whitmore links this directly to Jim Collins's *Good to Great* description of the highest level of leadership: personal humility (self-awareness) paired with professional will (responsibility to something larger than the self).

**Sequence toward inner alignment** (four steps): (1) recognize you have subpersonalities at all and identify the active ones; (2) acknowledge to another person that conflicting parts exist, and explore what each wants; (3) get the parts to negotiate/cooperate with each other (e.g., "two early runs a week in exchange for three guilt-free lie-ins"); (4) true synthesis — full collaboration of all parts toward one integrated end.

**Structured visualization/daydreaming** is offered as one concrete advanced-coaching technique: a guided journey (walking toward a mountain, encountering an obstacle, a speaking animal, a gift, and a wise figure who answers three questions) used to surface symbolic material for later coaching exploration — what the obstacle represented, what quality was used to overcome it, what the gift and the questions/answers meant to the coachee.

A stated ethical rule for this entire domain: **never ask a coachee a question you would not be willing to answer yourself** — advanced/meaning-level coaching requires the coach to have done their own equivalent work first.

## 11. Measuring Coaching ROI, Leadership Qualities, and the Ladder to Mastery

**Documented benefits of coaching** (as claimed and organized in the book): improved performance/productivity; improved career development (on-the-job development beats occasional training courses); improved relationships and engagement (the mere act of asking someone's opinion signals value); improved job satisfaction and retention; more time freed up for the leader (less firefighting); increased innovation (psychological safety removes fear of ridicule); better use of existing people/knowledge (leaders often don't know what talent they're sitting on until they start asking); greater willingness to "go the extra mile"; greater organizational agility; a genuine high-performance culture; and coaching as a transferable life skill beyond the workplace.

**Coaching for Performance ROI methodology** — Performance Consultants' own evaluation framework, built specifically because, in Whitmore's view, the coaching industry's inability to measure ROI has held back its credibility and adoption. Mechanics:
- Record three things throughout any engagement: **goals and objectives** (owned by the coachee), **ongoing actions taken**, and **notes on what happened** (including peer feedback).
- Produce a **qualitative review** — behavioral change and its felt impact on the coachee's leader, team, and peers, from the coachee's own vantage point.
- Produce a **quantitative review** — where possible, translate behavioral change into a monetized business impact, each estimate carrying the coachee's own self-rated confidence level (since ROI estimation here is explicitly "an art, not a science").
- Calculate ROI as monetized impact relative to the coaching investment. The book cites an average ROI across Performance Consultants engagements of roughly **800 percent**, and a specific case (an operations manager coached against a goal of becoming a director within three years) reaching director three years ahead of schedule, with an estimated £78,000 of quantified impact over three months.
- A parallel tool — the **Performance Curve Survey** — measures the prevailing organizational (or team, or individual) mindset against the four-stage model in Section 2, producing both a diagnosis and an implied next-focus for improvement.

**Qualities of leadership for the future** (a five-part model):
- **Values** — genuinely personal (not merely company-stated) values, which Whitmore argues are innate and universal at a deep level but get buried under parental/social/cultural conditioning; surfacing them is itself a coaching process. He illustrates with his own personal journey (via activism, then therapy, then coaching) toward identifying "opposition to the abuse of power" as his own specific, actionable value — arrived at only after multiple rounds of narrowing from an initially too-broad commitment to "justice."
- **Vision** — the ability to *envision* (create a bold, long-horizon, boundary-crossing image of a desired future) combined with the ability to *communicate* that vision compellingly enough to generate followership.
- **Authenticity** — being who you really are without fear of others' judgment; the ongoing work of shedding conditioning, false beliefs, and ego-driven fears. Connected back to the psychosynthesis "I" and to Collins's "personal humility + professional will."
- **Agility** — flexibility and willingness to change course as conditions demand; explicitly not about abandoning core values, but about shedding habitual patterns and fear of the unknown. Includes a practical "break-a-habit" exercise (deliberately varying small routine behaviors for a week) as a way to build the muscle.
- **Alignment** — internal coherence among one's own competing subpersonalities/values, without which outward team or organizational alignment is very difficult to achieve; a leader who is inwardly conflicted will read to others as inconsistent or untrustworthy even if they can't say why.

**The Ladder to Mastery — four stages of learning** (a widely used training model that the book adopts and applies specifically to self-coaching):
- **Unconscious incompetence** — low performance, no awareness of the gap.
- **Conscious incompetence** — low performance, but flaws/weak areas are now recognized.
- **Conscious competence** — improved performance through deliberate, somewhat effortful practice.
- **Unconscious competence** — natural, integrated, automatic high performance.

A key refinement offered for self-coaching: rather than *trying* to fix a flaw (which stays in effortful "conscious competence" indefinitely), simply and non-judgmentally *observe and rate* the quality you want to improve (e.g., rate smoothness of gear changes 1–10 after each one) — with no added effort, the rating trends upward and unconscious competence emerges faster and more durably than through willed correction. This "monitoring, not forcing" distinction is presented as one of the book's more counterintuitive but highest-leverage coaching techniques.

**Performance, learning, and enjoyment** are described as inextricably linked — neglect any one and, sooner or later, the other two degrade. Kahneman and Tversky's own account of their collaborative breakthroughs (explicitly cited) is used as real-world evidence that enjoyment and rigor are not in tension but mutually reinforcing.

**Effecting cultural change — the "living system" model**: sustainable culture change requires addressing *both* the "harder" technical elements (processes, systems, structure) and the "softer" people/behavioral elements simultaneously — with leadership sitting at the center connecting the two. Organizations that address only one side (a new org chart with no behavior change, or a behavior-change program with no supporting systems/incentives) reliably fail to sustain the shift. Three clarifying questions for any change effort: **Why** are we changing (internal/external drivers)? **What** are we changing into (what changes, what stays)? **How** will we design and deliver it, and who does what?
