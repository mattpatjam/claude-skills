# The Coaching Habit — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing coaching/training programs, or advising on management practice.*
*Book: "The Coaching Habit: Say Less, Ask More & Change the Way You Lead Forever" — Michael Bungay Stanier*

---

## 1. Core Premise & The Three Vicious Circles

The book's central behaviour change: **a little less advice, a little more curiosity.** Managers default to telling people what to do and solving problems for them. This feels productive and in-control, but it creates three self-reinforcing vicious circles that make managers work harder while having less impact:

- **Circle 1 — Creating Overdependence.** You train your team to rely on you for answers. This is disempowering for them and turns you into a bottleneck. The more you help, the more they need your help; the more they need your help, the more time you spend helping them.
- **Circle 2 — Getting Overwhelmed.** You're pulled in every direction, distracted by email and back-to-back meetings, and lose focus. The more you lose focus, the more overwhelmed you feel; the more overwhelmed you feel, the more you lose focus.
- **Circle 3 — Becoming Disconnected.** You and your team spend time on work that gets things done but has no real impact or meaning. The less engaged people are, the less likely they are to do or recognize Great Work.

**Coaching for Performance vs. Coaching for Development** is the key distinction that runs through the whole book:
- *Coaching for performance* addresses and fixes a specific problem or challenge — putting out the fire, building up the fire, banking the fire. Everyday, necessary, but limited.
- *Coaching for development* shifts the focus from the issue itself to the person managing the issue — calling them forward to learn, improve and grow rather than just getting something sorted out. This is rarer and significantly more powerful. Adding the two words "for you" to almost any question is the simplest way to nudge a conversation from performance-mode toward development-mode.

**Three situations that block a real conversation from starting** (relevant when opening any coaching exchange):
- *The Small Talk Tango* — small talk has value for rapport but rarely bridges to what matters.
- *The Ossified Agenda* — the same meeting, same agenda, recited out of habit, no longer serving what's actually needed.
- *The Default Diagnosis* — everyone assumes they already know what the issue is and jumps straight to solving it, often the wrong thing.

**The Seven Essential Questions** (the book's spine, in order): the Kickstart Question, the AWE Question ("And What Else?"), the Focus Question, the Foundation Question, the Lazy Question, the Strategic Question, and the Learning Question. Six of the seven start with "What" by design (see Section 3). The first and last pair up as the "Coaching Bookends" for any conversation.

The underlying neuroscience concept threaded throughout: your brain scans the environment roughly **five times a second**, asking "is it safe here, or is it dangerous?" Safety produces sophisticated, engaged thinking; perceived danger triggers a fight-or-flight "amygdala hijack" that shuts down nuance. Every question in this book is, among other things, a tool for making a conversation feel safe rather than risky (see the TERA model in Section 7).

## 2. The New Habit Formula (The Habit-Change Model)

Before teaching any question, the book insists you need a model for how behaviour change actually works — because "simple" doesn't mean "easy," and asking more and telling less is a genuinely hard habit to build.

**Five essential components of an effective new habit**, drawn from behavioural science:

1. **A reason (Make a Vow).** Don't motivate the change by imagining the successful outcome — research shows too much time spent imagining success actually reduces motivation to do the work. Instead, connect the new habit to serving someone else: a person or people you care about, not just "what's in it for me."
2. **A trigger (Figure Your Trigger).** If you don't know what triggers the old behaviour, you'll be doing it before you notice — so you can't intervene. Charles Duhigg identifies five trigger types: location, time, emotional state, other people, and the immediately preceding action. The more specific the trigger definition, the more usable it is. Weak: "at the team meeting." Strong: "When Jenny asks me for feedback on her idea in the team meeting."
3. **A micro-habit (Double-S It: Be Short & Specific).** Per B.J. Fogg's tiny-habits research, define the new behaviour so it takes less than sixty seconds to do. Vague, abstract, or lengthy new habits get hacked by your own brain before they take hold. Each of the Seven Essential Questions fits this bill — every one can be asked in under sixty seconds.
4. **Effective practice (Practice Deeply).** Drawing on Dan Coyle's "Deep Practice" (from *The Talent Code*), three components: (a) practice small chunks of the bigger action rather than the whole skill at once, (b) repeat constantly — fast, slow, differently, but repeat, (c) be mindful and notice when it goes well; celebrate the small win (a fist pump is enough).
5. **A plan for slipping (Plan How to Get Back on Track).** You will miss a moment, miss a day — that's guaranteed. Per Jeremy Dean's *Making Habits, Breaking Habits*, build a resilient system with an obvious next step for when (not if) it breaks down, rather than treating one lapse as permission to abandon the habit entirely.

**The New Habit Formula itself — three parts, filled in for each new habit:**
- **"When this happens…"** — the specific trigger moment (crossroads between old and new behaviour).
- **"Instead of…"** — the specific old habit being replaced, named honestly.
- **"I will…"** — the specific new behaviour, framed to take sixty seconds or less.

Worked example from the book: "When I'm feeling frustrated (emotional state) in my weekly meeting (time) with Bob (people) because he says 'I haven't really thought about it' (action)… Instead of asking, 'Have you thought about X?' and hoping he takes the hint… I will ask Bob, 'So what ideas do you have now?'"

**Additional tips for making any new habit stick:**
- Start somewhere easy — pick a person likely to be receptive, or one with whom things are already going so badly you have nothing to lose.
- Start small — master one habit and get it "in your bones" before adding the next.
- Buddy up — a check-in partner, coach, or mastermind group for accountability.
- Expect to slip — you'll move through "conscious incompetence" (awkward, feels wrong) toward "conscious competence" (deliberate, still effortful but working) through repetition.

## 3. Question-Asking Disciplines (Cross-Cutting Technique)

These are format and delivery rules that apply to *all seven* questions — the "how to ask," independent of "what to ask." The book calls these Question Masterclass lessons.

- **Ask one question at a time.** Stacking multiple questions ("drive-by questioning") overwhelms the other person and feels like interrogation, not conversation. New habit: after asking a question, stop — do not add another question, however curious you are about the answer to a second one.
- **Cut the intro and ask the question.** Most people bury a question in a long, rambling setup. Skip the preamble and ask directly. If you need a softening lead-in, use "Out of curiosity…" — it lessens the perceived weight of a question and makes it easier to both ask and answer.
- **Don't ask rhetorical (fake) questions.** "Have you thought of…?", "What about…?", "Did you consider…?" are advice with a question mark stapled on — they don't count as real questions. If you're sure you have the right idea, hold it back and ask "And what else?" first; the other person will often surface it themselves. If they don't, offer your idea explicitly as an idea, not disguised as a question.
- **Stick to questions that start with "What," avoid "Why."** Two reasons to avoid "Why": it puts people on the defensive (tone easily reads as accusatory — "What the hell were you thinking?"), and asking "why" is usually really a bid for backstory so you can go fix the problem yourself, which pulls you back into the overdependence/overwhelm circles. Reframe: "Why did you do that?" becomes "What were you hoping for here?" "Why did you think this was a good idea?" becomes "What made you choose this course of action?" Six of the Seven Essential Questions start with "What" by design.
- **Get comfortable with silence.** After asking a question, a pause of even three or four seconds can feel unbearably long, but silence usually signals the person is genuinely thinking — building new neural pathways. New habit: after asking, wait at least three more seconds before saying anything else, rather than filling the space with a rephrased question, a suggestion, or filler words.
- **Actually listen to the answer.** It's easy to go through the motions of "active listening" (nodding, small encouraging noises) while your mind is elsewhere. The discipline is to genuinely listen, and when you notice you've drifted, consciously come back and re-engage.
- **Acknowledge the answers you get.** Before moving to the next question (especially before another "And what else?"), briefly acknowledge what was said — not judging it, just signaling it was heard. Simple acknowledgments work fine: "Fantastic." "I like it." "Good one." "Nice." "Yes, that's good." "Mmm-hmmm."
- **Use every channel to ask a question.** These questions work as well typed as spoken — in email, IM, Slack, text. When a long, rambling email triggers your urge to write an equally long advice-laden reply, instead pick one of the Seven Essential Questions and ask it by email. Example replies: "Wow, there's a lot going on here. What's the real challenge here for you, do you think?" or "I've scanned your email. In a sentence or two, what do you want?"
- **A robust three-question opening script** (combining Questions 1–3): Open with "What's on your mind?" Check in with "Is there anything else on your mind?" Then focus with "So what's the real challenge here for you?" This alone reliably deepens a conversation past small talk or a rehearsed agenda.

## 4. The Kickstart Question — "What's on Your Mind?"

**Purpose:** the opening question for any coaching conversation. It is deliberately a "Goldilocks question" — open enough to invite the person to go straight to whatever matters most to them, but focused enough that it isn't an invitation to ramble about anything and everything. It dissolves the Ossified Agenda, sidesteps the Small Talk Tango, and defeats the Default Diagnosis (see Section 1).

**Exact wording:** *"What's on your mind?"*

**Why it works (per the book's research digest):** whatever occupies your working memory unconsciously shapes what you notice and the choices you make — holding an unresolved concern in mind is a constant, low-grade energy drain (the brain uses about 20% of the body's energy despite being about 2% of its weight). Naming what's on your mind acts as a pressure-release valve, surfacing something that was otherwise narrowing how you see the situation.

**The 3P Model — deepening focus after the Kickstart Question.** Once someone answers "What's on your mind?", use the 3P framework to decide where in a challenge to dig: a challenge is typically centred on a **Project**, a **Person**, or a **Pattern**.
- **Project** — the content of the situation, the actual work being tackled. Most familiar territory; this is where coaching-for-performance conversations usually live.
- **People** — not really about the other person, but about the relationship and specifically the coachee's own role in a relationship that isn't working as well as it could.
- **Patterns** — the person's own behaviours and ways of working that they'd like to change. This is where coaching-for-development conversations tend to emerge — personal, more challenging, and currently under-used in most organizations.

**Putting the 3Ps to use:** after "What's on your mind?", offer the frame explicitly — "There are three different facets we could look at: the project side, any challenges around the actual content; the people side, issues with team members, colleagues, other departments, bosses, customers; and patterns, if there's a way you're getting in your own way. Where should we start?" Whichever P they pick will be a strong start; when that thread is exhausted, you can pivot to one of the other two P's with "If this was a thing, what would the challenge here be for you?"

**New Habit Formula for this question:**
- When this happens: any conversation-starting moment — someone drops by, a scheduled one-to-one, an email or IM that opens a topic.
- Instead of: small talk, jumping straight to advice, defaulting to a standing agenda, or telling the person what the topic of conversation is.
- I will: ask, "What's on your mind?"

## 5. The AWE Question — "And What Else?"

**Purpose:** described in the book as "The Best Coaching Question in the World." It is the self-management tool that restrains the manager's own urge to jump in with advice, while generating more and better options from the other person. It pairs with, and amplifies, every one of the other six questions.

**Exact wording:** *"And what else?"* (A softer closing variant to invite wrap-up while still leaving the door open: *"Is there anything else?"*)

**Three reasons it works:**
1. **More options lead to better decisions.** Citing Paul Nutt's research on 168 organizational decisions: 71% of decisions were framed as a binary "do this, or not" choice, and decisions made from binary framing failed more than half the time. Adding just one more option (do this, or this, or not) nearly halved the failure rate, down to about 30%. The first answer someone gives is almost never the only one, and rarely the best one.
2. **It tames the Advice Monster.** The "Advice Monster" is the book's term for the deeply ingrained urge to jump to expert/answer-it/fix-it mode. (Cited stat: the average doctor interrupts a patient describing symptoms after just eighteen seconds.) Asking "And what else?" is a way of staying lazy and staying curious instead of leaping to solve.
3. **It buys you time** to think when you're not sure what's going on.

**Four practical tips for asking it well:**
- **Stay curious, stay genuine** — don't let it become a rote, bored-sounding tic; apply Deep Practice (Section 2) to how you ask it, not just how often.
- **Ask it one more time than feels natural** — people generally under-ask this question, not over-ask it. Guideline: at least three times, rarely more than five.
- **Recognize success** — when the person says "There is nothing else," that is the successful end point of this line of inquiry, not a failure. Take it as a cue to move to another question.
- **Move on when the energy drops** — if you can feel momentum draining from the conversation, switch to the closing variant, "Is there anything else?", which invites closure while still leaving room for a final point.

**Caution — the Paradox of Choice.** More options are good only up to a point. Citing Barry Schwartz's jam-tasting study (a 24-flavour table drew more browsers but 10x fewer purchases than a 6-flavour table) and George Miller's "Magical Number Seven" research (later refined toward roughly four as the number of items the unconscious brain can easily chunk): the goal of "And what else?" is not to generate unlimited options, just to surface what the other person already has in them. Three to five answers is genuine progress — don't force more.

**New Habit Formula for this question:**
- When this happens: someone has given you an idea, and you're tempted to jump in with your own, or assume you already know the answer, or are ready to wrap up the conversation.
- Instead of: going with the first (or second, or third) idea offered, or announcing your own brilliant idea before they've finished sharing theirs.
- I will: ask, "And what else?"

## 6. The Focus Question — "What's the Real Challenge Here for You?"

**Purpose:** slows down the reflexive rush to fix the *first* problem mentioned, so effort goes toward the *real* problem instead. Jumping straight into solving creates three failure modes: you solve the wrong problem (the first thing named is rarely the actual issue — it may be a symptom, a secondary issue, or a half-baked solution to something unarticulated); you do your team's work for them (classic overdependence); and, paradoxically, you end up not solving your own work either, because you're now the bottleneck for everyone else's problems too.

**How the question is built, phrase by phrase:**
- *"What's the challenge?"* — too vague on its own; produces an obvious or overly abstract answer.
- *"What's the real challenge here?"* — implies there are multiple possible challenges and forces a choice of the one that matters most; slows people down to think harder.
- *"What's the real challenge here for you?"* — the "for you" pins the question to the specific person in front of you, keeping it personal rather than allowing them to stay in the abstract or talk about someone else's problem.

**Exact wording:** *"What's the real challenge here for you?"*

**The three "Foggy-fiers"** — recurring patterns that keep a challenge vague, and how the Focus Question cuts through each:
- **Proliferation of Challenges** — a flood of unrelated problems comes tumbling out at once (the "popcorn" pattern). Solution: don't pick one for them; ask, "If you had to pick one of these to focus on, which one here would be the real challenge for you?"
- **Coaching the Ghost** — the conversation is really about a third party (a difficult colleague, a project, a reorg) rather than about the person in front of you; entertaining, but it's gossip, not coaching. Solution: acknowledge what's been said, then redirect — "I think I understand some of what's going on with [the person/situation]. What's the real challenge here for you?"
- **Abstractions & Generalizations** — the conversation stays at a high, academic, "we/us" level with no "I/me," never landing on anything concrete enough to solve. Solution: "I have a sense of the overall challenge. What's the real challenge here for you?"

**Coaching for Performance vs. Coaching for Development, applied here:** appending "for you" to as many questions as possible is the everyday technique for shifting a conversation from performance-mode toward development-mode — the problem still gets addressed, but personal insight and growth come along with it.

**Three strategies to make the question land well:**
- **Trust that you're being useful** even though asking questions instead of giving answers can feel like you're not adding value — watch for the pause where you can see the person actually thinking; that's the signal it's working.
- **Remember there's still a place for direct answers** — if someone just needs to know where the folder is, tell them; don't ask "What's the real challenge here for you?" reflexively for simple factual requests.
- **Stack the AWE Question on top of it** — "What's the real challenge here for you?" followed by "And what else? What else is a real challenge here for you?" goes even deeper.

**New Habit Formula for this question:**
- When this happens: the conversation has already jumped to solutions, or someone's clearly wrestling with a problem but you're not sure they've named the real one, or you feel scared/anxious/uncertain about a challenge yourself.
- Instead of: assuming the first-named challenge is the real one, leaving the challenge vague and high-level, trying to fix every challenge mentioned, or moving straight to actions without establishing focus.
- I will: ask, "What's the real challenge here for you?"

## 7. The Foundation Question — "What Do You Want?"

**Purpose:** the question the book frames as sitting at the heart of every adult-to-adult relationship. Drawing on Peter Block's definition, an adult-to-adult relationship is one in which each party is "able to ask for what you want, knowing that the answer may be No." The question is hard to answer for a stack of reasons: people often don't actually know what they want beneath the first, fast answer; even when they do know, they often don't feel entitled or safe enough to ask; even when they ask, they often bury the request under hedges and hints so it isn't clearly heard; and even when it's heard clearly, both sides can struggle with the possibility that the answer is No, Maybe, or Not that but this instead.

**Exact wording:** *"What do you want?"* (Nicknamed the "Goldfish Question" for the response it often gets — mouth opening and closing with no sound.)

**Untangling Wants and Needs (Marshall Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication model):** a *want* is the surface, tactical ask (get a report done by a date, know whether to attend a meeting) — this is what typically surfaces from "What do you want?" A *need* is the deeper, more universal human driver behind the want. Drawing on economist Manfred Max-Neef, Rosenberg lists nine universal needs: **Affection, Creation, Recreation, Freedom, Identity, Understanding, Participation, Protection, Subsistence.** Practical use: when someone states a want, try to guess the need underneath it — "I want you to talk to the VP for me" might really be about Protection (I'm too junior) or Participation (I need you to do your part). Recognizing the need behind a want changes how you can best respond to it — and the same works in reverse, when framing your own requests.

**Ask a Question, Trade Answers.** The power of "What do you want?" increases when you not only ask it of the other person but also answer it yourself for the same situation — a mutual exchange, consistent with the adult-to-adult frame from Peter Block.

**The TERA model — the neuroscience of engagement (Evan Gordon / David Rock lineage):** the brain's "fundamental organizing principle" is a constant risk-versus-reward scan, roughly five times a second, asking "is it safe or is it dangerous?" When it reads safety, thinking is more sophisticated, subtle, and collaborative; when it reads danger, it drops into fight-or-flight ("amygdala hijack"), thinking gets black-and-white, and people disengage. Four drivers, spelling TERA, determine which way the brain reads a situation:
- **T — Tribe.** "Are you with me, or against me?" Being seen as on someone's side raises their TERA quotient.
- **E — Expectation.** "Do I know what happens next, or don't I?" Clarity about the future feels safe; uncertainty feels dangerous.
- **R — Rank.** "Am I more or less important than you, right now?" This is about how power and status play out in the moment, not formal titles.
- **A — Autonomy.** "Do I get a say, or don't I?" (Ties to Dan Pink's *Drive*.) Perceived choice makes an environment feel rewarding.

Asking "What do you want?" raises TERA by increasing the other person's sense of tribe (you're helping them solve their own challenge, not dictating), rank (you're letting them "have the floor"), and autonomy (you're trusting them to generate the answer). Expectation may dip slightly, since a question carries more ambiguity than a ready-made answer — an acceptable tradeoff.

**Related concept — the miracle question** (from solution-focused therapy, referenced as an intellectual cousin, not a direct part of the Seven Questions): "Suppose that tonight, while you're sleeping, a miracle happens. When you get up in the morning, how will you know that things have suddenly got better?" Its value is pushing people to imagine a 10x improvement rather than a 10% tweak, and to focus on the destination (the "what") before the means (the "how"). The Foundation Question does the same job more directly.

**New Habit Formula for this question:**
- When this happens: the conversation or the other person feels stuck, someone is procrastinating and you're not sure why, or you're in a slightly fraught exchange that's gone off the rails.
- Instead of: assuming you already know what they want without having asked, or pushing your own idea/opinion/course of action.
- I will: ask, "What do you want?" (Bonus: also state what you want.)

## 8. The Lazy Question — "How Can I Help?"

**Purpose:** the self-management tool for escaping the trap of being "helpful" in a way that actually disempowers people — Edgar Schein's core insight (from his book *Helping*) that offering help unasked "one-ups" the helper and lowers the status of the person being helped, regardless of good intentions, and is often met with resistance, frustration, and disempowerment rather than gratitude.

**Exact wording:** *"How can I help?"* A blunter variant: *"What do you want from me?"* — strips the exchange down to its essentials but needs a genuinely curious tone or it reads as aggressive (Persecutor), whiny (Victim), or smothering (Rescuer). Softening phrases that keep it in "noble inquiry" territory rather than interrogation: "Out of curiosity…", "Just so I know…", "To help me understand better…", "To make sure that I'm clear…"

**The Karpman Drama Triangle** (Stephen Karpman's practical rendering of Transactional Analysis) — the model for why unsolicited helping backfires. Three roles people cycle through under stress, each with its own core belief, dynamic, payoff, cost, and characteristic "stuck" feeling:
- **Victim** — core belief: "My life is so hard/unfair, poor me." Dynamic: "It's not my fault, it's theirs." Payoff: no responsibility, attracts Rescuers. Cost: no sense of agency, seen as ineffective, "no one likes a whiner." Stuck feeling: powerless, useless.
- **Persecutor** — core belief: "I'm surrounded by fools." Dynamic: "It's not my fault, it's yours." Payoff: feels superior, in control. Cost: becomes responsible for everything, creates Victims, seen as a micromanager, people do the minimum, "no one likes a bully." Stuck feeling: alone, trusts no one.
- **Rescuer** — core belief: "Don't fight, don't worry, let me fix it." Dynamic: "It's my fault/responsibility, not yours." Payoff: feels morally superior, indispensable. Cost: help gets rejected, perpetuates the triangle by creating Victims, "no one likes a meddler." Stuck feeling: burdened, rescuing doesn't actually work.

These are **roles people play in a given moment, not fixed identities** — most people cycle through all three within a single conversation, and most managers, when asked, self-identify Rescuer as their default. "Rescuers create Victims" is the book's blunt summary of why over-helping perpetuates the very dependency it's trying to fix.

**Managing the anxiety of asking "How can I help?"** — worrying the answer will be an unreasonable ask is natural, but every answer still leaves a full range of responses available: **Yes** (always an option, but not obligatory — obligation is the actual source of the anxiety); **No, I can't do that** (a legitimate answer on its own); **"I can't do that, but I could do [counter-offer]"** (a middle ground — decline the specific ask while still offering something); or simply **buying time** ("Let me think about that").

**Resisting the direct-appeal trap.** When someone asks a direct advice-seeking question ("How do I…?" or "What do you think I should do about…?"), the described new habit is: "That's a great question. I've got some ideas, which I'll share with you. But before I do, what are your first thoughts?" Acknowledge the answer, then ask "And what else could you do?", acknowledge again, then "Is there anything else you could try here?" — only after that sequence is exhausted do you offer your own idea, and only as one option among the others generated.

**New Habit Formula for this question:**
- When this happens: someone asks "How do I…?" or "Could you…?", or describes a situation and a solution pops immediately to mind, or you think "it's faster to just do this myself."
- Instead of: jumping into solution-providing/action mode, assuming you know the request before it's been clearly made.
- I will: ask, "How can I help?" (or, more bluntly, "What do you want from me?")

## 9. The Strategic Question — "If You're Saying Yes to This, What Are You Saying No To?"

**Purpose:** targets overwhelm at the level of commitments, not just conversations. Michael Porter's definition of strategy — "the essence of strategy is choosing what not to do" — is the frame: a Yes is meaningless without a clear No to give it boundaries. The question forces clarity and commitment about what's actually being agreed to (too many Yeses are half-hearted or vague — "I never said I was going to do that!"), and it surfaces two distinct kinds of No:
- **No of omission** — what gets automatically ruled out by saying Yes to this (if you say yes to this meeting, you're saying no to whatever else is scheduled at the same time).
- **No of commission** — what you now actively need to stop doing, hand off, or decline in order to make room for the Yes to actually happen. This is the deeper, more useful layer — it's easy to assume a new Yes will "just fit" into an already full plate; it won't without a deliberate No somewhere else.

**Exact wording:** *"If you're saying Yes to this, what are you saying No to?"* Related framing questions: *"Let's be clear — what exactly are you saying Yes to?"* and *"What could being fully committed to this idea look like?"*

**Applying the 3P Model to the No** (same three lenses as the Kickstart Question, Section 4, reused here to map what needs to be released):
- **Projects** — what needs to be abandoned or postponed, which meetings you'll stop attending, what resources need to be diverted to the new Yes.
- **People** — what expectations need managing, which Drama Triangle dynamics you need to exit, which relationships may need to wither.
- **Patterns** — what habits need breaking, what old stories or outdated ambitions need updating, what self-beliefs need releasing.

**Good reasons vs. bad reasons to say Yes or No** (crowd-sourced from the author's LinkedIn network):
- Bad reasons to say Yes: wanting the person to leave/get off the phone; knowing you won't actually do it; wanting to be liked; pure habit.
- Good reasons to say Yes: you asked questions and got good answers; you're clear what you'll stop doing to make room; it's Great Work — has impact and meaning for you; your boss made it non-negotiable.
- Bad reasons to say No: disliking the person (unless you really dislike them); comfort/resistance to change; "attack is the best defence"; pure habit.
- Good reasons to say No: you asked questions, got good answers, and determined it's not a fit; you've thought through your core priorities and are holding the line; you're building a reputation as strategic and thoughtful.

**How to say No when you feel you can't (Part 1) — saying Yes more slowly (Bill Jensen's framing):** what gets people into trouble is committing too fast, before understanding what they're actually agreeing to. Stay curious first, with questions such as:
- "Why are you asking me?"
- "Whom else have you asked?"
- "When you say this is urgent, what do you mean?"
- "According to what standard does this need to be completed? By when?"
- "If I couldn't do all of this, but could do just a part, what part would you have me do?"
- "What do you want me to take off my plate so I can do this?"

Four possible responses to this line of questioning: the person gets annoyed and insists you just do it (sometimes legitimate, depending on culture/urgency); the person has good answers, confirming it was a thoughtful ask; the person doesn't have answers but is willing to find them (buys you time); or the person decides you're "too much like hard work" and takes the ask elsewhere.

**How to say No when you feel you can't (Part 2) — the "third point" technique.** Saying No to a task feels like saying No to a person, which creates social friction. Creating a "third point" — e.g., writing the request down on paper — lets you point to the written request and say "I'm afraid I have to say No to this" rather than "I'm afraid I have to say No to you." Say Yes to the person; say No to the task.

**The Other Five Strategic Questions**, referenced from Roger Martin & A.G. Lafley's *Playing to Win* as the deeper strategy framework this question distills, worked iteratively rather than linearly:
1. What is our winning aspiration?
2. Where will we play?
3. How will we win?
4. What capabilities must be in place?
5. What management systems are required?

The book's Strategic Question is presented as the single question that sits behind all five: what are you choosing not to do, in order to genuinely win at what you chose to do.

**Cognitive biases the Strategic Question helps counter** (Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow*):
- **Planning fallacy** — systematically underestimating how long things take, compounded by underestimating the degree of that underestimation. The question forces more realistic accounting of actual capacity.
- **Prospect theory** — losses are felt more intensely than equivalent gains, so once something is held onto, people overvalue it and resist releasing it. The question surfaces exactly what's being held onto so it can be weighed honestly against the new Yes.

**New Habit Formula for this question:**
- When this happens: someone is about to move from overwhelmed to more overwhelmed by adding something new, or is avoiding a real choice by saying Yes to everything, or scope is visibly creeping.
- Instead of: assuming capacity can expand indefinitely, or defaulting into Rescuer (saying Yes to everything to keep people happy) or Victim (feeling you have no choice but Yes) mode.
- I will: ask, "What will you say No to, to make this Yes rock-solid and real?"

## 10. The Learning Question — "What Was Most Useful for You?" & The Coaching Bookends

**Purpose:** closes a conversation in a way that locks in learning rather than letting insight evaporate the moment people leave the room. The underlying mechanism is Chris Argyris's **double-loop learning**: the first loop is fixing the immediate problem; the second loop — where real learning happens — is pulling back to reflect on the problem-solving process itself. People don't learn simply by being told something, and they don't even fully learn by doing something; they start learning only when they get a chance to recall and reflect on what just happened.

**Exact wording:** *"What was most useful for you?"*

**Why this phrasing specifically, over similar alternatives** ("What did you learn?", "What was the key insight?", "What do you want to remember?"), across six distinct effects:
1. **It assumes the conversation was useful** — framing that itself creates a moment to look for the value, rather than letting people hurry past a moment of insight (referencing Churchill's line about people who "stumble over the truth" and hurry off as if nothing happened).
2. **It asks people to identify the One Big Thing (OBT)**, not a list — feedback and takeaways land better as a single memorable point than as twelve items that trigger overwhelm.
3. **It makes it personal** — the "for you" shifts it from abstract to subjective; people telling themselves what was useful lands as more credible than being told what should have been useful.
4. **It gives you feedback too** — the answer tells the coach what to do more of next time, and reassures them that asking questions instead of giving advice is in fact landing as useful.
5. **It's learning, not judgment** — unlike "Was this useful?" (a closed Yes/No that elicits judgment, not insight), "What was most useful?" forces genuine extraction of value from the conversation.
6. **It reminds people how useful you are** — a cumulative effect that shows up later, e.g. in upward feedback during performance reviews.

**The neuroscience of retention:**
- **The AGES model** (Josh Davis and colleagues, NeuroLeadership Institute) names four drivers of longer-term memory: **Attention, Generation, Emotion, Spacing.** The relevant one here is **Generation** — creating your own connection to an idea (versus passively receiving it) substantially increases retention, because it engages the hippocampus more directly. This is the neurological case for why advice (received passively) is retained far less than a self-generated answer.
- **"To Learn, Retrieve"** (Peter Brown, Henry Roediger, Mark McDaniel, *Make It Stick*): forgetting begins immediately after any conversation or learning event; retrieval practice interrupts that decay. Asking the Learning Question at the end of a conversation is the first interruption of the forgetting curve. It can be layered elsewhere too — e.g. opening a meeting or one-on-one with "What have you learned since we last met?"
- **The peak-end rule** (Daniel Kahneman): how people remember an experience is disproportionately shaped by its peak/trough moment and by how it ends. Cited evidence: colonoscopy patients whose procedures were extended slightly but ended with less discomfort in the final minute rated the whole procedure as 10% less painful and were 10% more likely to return for a follow-up, compared with patients whose objectively shorter procedure ended on a worse note. Ending a conversation on the Learning Question creates a more positive final note, which colors how the whole conversation gets remembered — independent of how it actually went.

**The Coaching Bookends.** The Kickstart Question ("What's on your mind?") and the Learning Question ("What was most useful for you?") pair up to open and close any coaching conversation: the opener drives quickly past small talk and rehearsed agendas into what actually matters; the closer extracts and locks in the value before everyone scatters. For an even richer exchange, the coach can reciprocate by sharing what *they* found most useful about the conversation — a mutual exchange that, per the book, strengthens the relationship's "social contract," echoing the Foundation Question's "ask a question, trade answers" principle (Section 7).

**New Habit Formula for this question:**
- When this happens: the end of any exchange — in person or virtual, with a direct report, colleague, boss, team meeting, or client — any moment where the instinct is "good, we're done!"
- Instead of: wrapping up with generic praise, jumping straight to the action list, or rushing off to the next meeting without capturing the takeaway.
- I will: ask, "What was most useful here for you?" (or a variant: "What did you find most valuable about this chat?", "What worked best here?")
