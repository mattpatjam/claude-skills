# Design for How People Learn — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing coaching/training programs, or advising on management practice.*
*Book: "Design for How People Learn" — Julie Dirksen*

---

## 1. The Learner's Journey and Gap Analysis

A learning experience is a journey from where the learner is now to where they need to be. The end point isn't "knowing more," it's "doing more." Before designing any solution, diagnose which kind of gap you're actually dealing with. Treating the wrong gap as a knowledge gap is the single most common design failure (e.g., building a training course to fix a turnover problem that was never about knowledge).

**The five gap types:**

- **Knowledge gaps** — the learner is missing information. This is the easiest gap to fix and the one most learning defaults to, often incorrectly. Information is "supplies for the journey," not the destination itself — having it doesn't mean the learner knows what to do with it.
- **Skill gaps** — the learner needs practice, not just information. Test: *"Is it reasonable to think that someone can be proficient without practice?"* If no, you're dealing with a skill, and no amount of content delivery substitutes for practice.
- **Motivation/attitude gaps** — the learner knows what to do but isn't doing it. Signs include resistance to the destination itself, anxiety about change, distraction, or lack of big-picture buy-in.
- **Environment gaps** — the surrounding process, incentives, tools, or reinforcement don't support success even when knowledge/skill/motivation are all present.
- **Communication gaps** — not a learning problem at all. The direction-giver doesn't know what they want, can't articulate it, or contradicts themselves (says one thing, rewards another). Best handled by documenting the issue and not pretending it's a training problem.

**Diagnostic questions to identify the real gap:**

- "What do they actually need to *do* with this?" (Ask again if you get "just be aware of it.")
- Follow a novice around, then follow an expert around — note what's different.
- "Would they be able to do it if they wanted to badly enough?" — if yes, it's not knowledge/skill.
- "Is there anything at all we could do, besides training, that would make it more likely people do the right thing?"
- "Is this going to involve changing the way they do things now?" (change = motivation gap risk)
- "What's the consequence if someone does it wrong?"
- "If someone is getting this exactly right, what would that look like?"
- "Is it reasonable to assume someone gets this right the first time, or will they need practice?"

**Special case — change/unlearning:** When new learning requires replacing an existing automated behavior, the learner has *momentum*. Automated skills free up conscious attention, but that same automaticity means old patterns actively interfere with new ones (similar to L1 language interference). Two rules: (1) change is a process, not an event — never expect a single explanation to produce lasting change; (2) backsliding and grumpiness are a normal, expected part of the process, not evidence of failure.

**Why gap identification matters:** If you don't correctly diagnose the gap, you build a solution sized wrong for the problem — a suspension bridge over a pothole, or a rope bridge across a canyon. Define the problem before designing the solution.

## 2. Knowing Your Learners

Understanding your audience isn't just demographics — it's their motivations, existing mental models, and how they differ from you (the expert designing for them).

**What learners want — types by motivation source:**

- **Intrinsically motivated learners** — interested in the topic itself or have a specific problem to solve. Design for them by: getting out of their way, giving them time to work on their own real problems, and leveraging them as teachers/resources for others.
- **Extrinsically motivated learners** — driven by external reward/requirement. Design for them by: scouring for any intrinsic motivator you can find, identifying their pain points and connecting the material to relieving them, avoiding theory/background with no clear payoff, and using interesting hypothetical problems to shift them toward intrinsic engagement.

**The "don't make them feel stupid" principle:** Learners want a safe path into unfamiliar material, not a display of their own ignorance. Tactics: leverage what they already know, engineer an early success, and create safe, non-judgmental places to fail or practice.

**Skill level and structuring for it:**

- Novices need: heavy guidance, a slow careful start, structured achievable goals, confidence-building, gradual difficulty increases with rest points, and frequent coaching/feedback.
- Proficient learners need: some new-concept practice, advanced information, coaching for refinement, and much more autonomy.
- Experts need: minimal setup, expert-level coaching only on genuine edge cases, progress-measuring tools, full autonomy, and the opportunity to teach/coach others.
- **Pull vs. Push:** novices don't know what they don't know (push structure at them); experts usually know what they need (let them pull resources on demand). Don't force experts through material built for novices — it creates the "expert stuck behind Grandma in the TSA line" experience.
- **Scaffolding:** when content is too complex for a novice to handle in full realistic context, build temporary supports (reduce environment complexity, use simplified walkthroughs, embed easy references) and fade them out as competence grows.

**How you differ from your learner — the "closet" problem:** Experts have a rich, well-organized mental model (many labeled shelves); novices have a shallow one (a pile on the floor). This creates two failure modes for designers: (1) you assume the learner has context they don't (the Bransford "tire-changing paragraph" effect — meaning is much easier to process once you know what the passage is about); (2) you forget what it was like not to know the material, so you skip foundational structure. Fixes — help learners build shelves via: a high-level organizer/road map up front, visuals, story structure, working through problems, having learners construct their own organizing scheme, or using a metaphor/analogy tied to something they already know well.

**Learning styles — what actually works:** The evidence for matching instruction to individual "learning styles" (VAK/VARK, Kolb, etc.) is weak. What *is* useful: vary your delivery methods (fights habituation and covers more bases), remember there are different kinds of intelligence (Gardner), everyone is essentially a visual/auditory/kinesthetic learner to some degree, and it can make sense to match approach to the *content* rather than to the individual learner.

**Methods for learning about your learners (in priority order):**

1. **Talk to your learners directly** — not just stakeholders/SMEs. Learners tell you how work actually happens (vs. how it's "supposed to" happen), where it hurts, and give you current, real examples.
2. **Follow learners around (contextual inquiry / job shadowing)** — reveals context that interviews miss, since people have already automated and stopped noticing steps in their own process.
3. **Try things out with learners** — prototype and test early and often. Reviewing materials on paper isn't the same as watching someone actually use them; testing surfaces problems no amount of theory will predict.

## 3. Setting Goals and Learning Objectives

**Start with the problem, not the goal.** Projects that start with a stated goal (rather than the underlying problem) risk solving problems the organization doesn't actually have. Ask "why do they need to know that?" repeatedly until you hit the real driver, and break oversized goals ("students need to be better managers") into specific, dischargeable pieces.

**Writing usable learning objectives:** Avoid vague verbs like "understand" — they can't be observed. "Doing" words (define, describe, explain) are a partial fix but still miss the point, because recall/recitation isn't the same as the ability to actually perform. Every learning objective should pass two tests:

- Is this something the learner would actually do in the real world?
- Can I tell when they've done it?

If either answer is no, rewrite the objective. (Note: for genuinely conceptual/foundational material with no direct real-world task, there's usually still *something* the learner is meant to do with the concept, even if it's just to enable later understanding.)

**Why you communicate objectives at all (Thalheimer's four objective types):** objectives serve different audiences and purposes — to focus learner attention, to set learner expectations, to set a performance bar, and to give the designer a clear internal target. Don't try to cram all four functions into one learner-facing bullet-point slide; that produces jargon-heavy, useless objectives statements. Communicate objectives to learners via a challenge, scenario, or mission framing instead of a bulleted list.

**How sophisticated should understanding be? — Bloom's Taxonomy (revised):** Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyze → Evaluate → Create. Treat this as a menu of cognitive demand levels to target, not a mandatory sequence — a lesson can validly start at Analyze or Evaluate and work backward, or even invert the whole order.

**How proficient should the learner become? — Gery's proficiency scale:** Familiarization → Comprehension → Conscious Effort → Conscious Action → Proficiency → Unconscious Competence. Getting past "Conscious Action" always requires substantial distributed practice; you cannot reach the higher levels of either sophistication or proficiency from a single point of contact (one class, one e-learning module). If a stakeholder wants "unconscious competence" from a 30-minute course, that's a scope mismatch to name explicitly.

**How long is the trip? — Pace layering (Stewart Brand's concept, applied to learners):** some capabilities change fast, some change slowly, some (personality, deep-seated habits) change only very slowly. Diagnose which category a target skill falls into before promising results:

- **Fast skills** — can usually be taken start-to-finish in a single learning experience (information-based, or reactivating rusty prior knowledge).
- **Slow skills** — take years (judgment calls, values-driven skills, deeply practiced capabilities). Design accordingly:
  - **Throw pillows** — cheap, easy, immediate-impact aids (a checklist, a job aid) that solve a small piece of the problem right away. Don't expect them to solve the big problem.
  - **Sturdier pieces** — more substantial tools/training that take real setup time and multiple supported steps to integrate.
  - **Respect the foundation** — don't fight a learner's deep personal bedrock (culture, personality); if your design doesn't fit it, change the design.
- The practical test: for any given skill, ask whether it's fast or slow before setting expectations for what a single learning intervention can achieve.

## 4. How Memory Works

Learning designers need a working model of memory-in (encoding) and memory-out (retrieval) — remembering without the ability to retrieve and use it accomplishes nothing.

**Three memory filters:**

- **Sensory memory** — nearly everything sensed is held briefly; most gets discarded unless something is unusual. Key risk: **habituation** — people stop noticing/responding to unchanging stimuli (banner blindness is the classic case). Implication: some consistency reduces cognitive load (learners don't have to re-orient every time), but too much consistency causes tune-out; vary delivery deliberately, not randomly (random/annoying variability just irritates).
- **Short-term/working memory** — short duration, limited capacity (the "7±2" figure is a rough guide, not a law). Information persists here only as long as it's needed for a purpose, or is refreshed by repetition. **Chunking** — grouping discrete items into fewer, larger units — is the main lever for expanding functional capacity (e.g., breaking a long procedure into 3-4 labeled phases rather than one 16-step list). Primacy and recency effects mean people retain the beginning and end of a sequence better than the middle.
- **Long-term memory** — the goal destination for anything meant to last. Best understood via the **"closet and shelves" metaphor**: information gets stored via associations, and the more shelves (contexts/associations) an item is placed on, the easier it is to retrieve later. Pure memorization (flash cards, rote repetition) puts information on only one shelf and makes it hard to retrieve flexibly or transfer to new contexts. Poorly-contextualized material (a topic you have little background in) sits on rickety shelves and slips through; overloaded, unstructured material sits on an overcrowded shelf that's hard to search.

**Context and retrieval:**

- **In-context learning** — encode information in the environment (physical, situational) closest to where it will be retrieved. The further the learning context is from the use context, the fewer retrieval "shelves" are built. When lives are at stake, training is almost always high-context (flight simulators, driver's ed) — the same principle should be applied more broadly wherever practical.
- **Emotional context** — one of the hardest contexts to replicate. If material is learned in a calm classroom but must be retrieved under stress or hostility, the gap between encoding and retrieval context can cause total failure to apply what was "known." Fixes: role-playing, artificial time/resource pressure, and high-quality dramatized scenarios.
- **Recall vs. recognition** — design practice to match how information will actually be retrieved. Recognition (multiple choice) is far easier than recall (open, unprompted retrieval), and most e-learning defaults to recognition activities purely because they're easy to grade by computer — not because they match real-world use. If the real task requires recall or physical performance (e.g., CPR), practicing via recognition-only activities does not transfer. Job aids can legitimately convert a recall task into a recognition task in the real world — but let learners practice *with* the job aid as part of training.
- **Real vs. perceived knowledge** — recognizing an answer feels like knowing it, but recall is the real test. Practicing only via review/recognition creates false confidence; practice retrieval, not just re-exposure.

**Types of long-term memory:**

- **Declarative/semantic memory** — facts, principles, ideas you can state explicitly.
- **Episodic memory** — memory for specific personal events; closely related is why **storytelling** is so effective: stories map onto an existing "story shelf" framework (beginning/middle/end, characters, logical/sequential flow), so they require less structuring effort to store and are easier to recall than isolated facts.
- **Conditioned memory** — automatic reactions to triggers, whether deliberately trained or incidentally acquired.
- **Procedural memory** — memory for step-by-step "how to do it" processes, much of it implicit (hard to verbalize). **Muscle memory / overlearning** is procedural memory practiced to the point of requiring zero conscious effort, freeing attention for other tasks.
- **Flashbulb memory** — vivid, detailed memory for highly emotionally charged events, likely a survival adaptation ("remember how you got away from the bear"). Emotion generally enhances retention even at lower intensities.

**Repetition and memorization:** Repetition is necessary but is a blunt instrument — like building thick walls instead of using clever engineering. Its core weaknesses: it puts information on only one shelf, doesn't build multi-context transfer, and creates sequential (not random) access to the memory. Reinforcement should ideally happen through varied re-encounters in different contexts, not monotonous repeated drilling.

## 5. Getting and Keeping Attention: The Rider and the Elephant

**The model (via Jonathan Haidt):** the brain has a rational, conscious "Rider" and an automatic, emotional, visceral "Elephant." The Rider can force the Elephant to comply for a while, but willpower is a finite, exhaustible resource (demonstrated by the classic cognitive-load experiment where subjects holding a 7-digit number were twice as likely to choose cake over fruit salad as those holding a 2-digit number). Design that only appeals to the Rider is exhausting to sustain; the goal is to *attract* the Elephant so the Rider doesn't have to drag it.

**Caution:** any technique used to attract the Elephant must be intrinsic to the material itself — decorative, unrelated "seductive" tricks (irrelevant humor, unrelated bells and whistles) can actively hurt learning and retention.

**Five levers to engage the Elephant:**

1. **Tell it stories.** Effective because stories land on existing "story shelves," follow a logical/sequential flow, create suspense (the brain starts predicting the ending), and (when well told) simply aren't boring. Make the learner the hero: show them a before/after capability gap, give them real achievements (organize content around accomplishments, not topics), and frame the material as a first-person problem to solve rather than a topic to survey. Build **urgency** through: a compelling story structure (protagonist/antagonist/obstacles/inciting incident), showing rather than telling, real (not punishing) time/resource constraints, immediacy (the fire is happening NOW, not "you may need to know this someday"), interesting dilemmas (never right/wrong — use good-vs-very-good, bad-vs-bad, or mixed tradeoffs), and consequences instead of generic feedback. Add **emotional resonance** — bare facts (a 10% commission rate) mean nothing until framed with emotional context (is 10% great or terrible? depends what you're comparing it to).
2. **Surprise it.** Unexpected rewards trigger stronger reward-circuit activation than predictable ones (variable reward schedules are why slot machines work). **Cognitive dissonance** — encountering something that doesn't fit the existing mental model (a purple dog) — forces active reconciliation and creates a "teachable moment." **Curiosity** is triggered by a perceived information gap: ask genuinely interesting questions (not ones answerable by a quick search), be mysterious/use case studies with a hidden reveal, deliberately leave information out, and consider Dan Meyer's "**Be Less Helpful**" philosophy — give learners a less-complete problem so they have to do the work of framing it themselves.
3. **Tell it all the other elephants are doing it (social engagement).** People pay more attention and perform better when they believe another person, not a machine, is involved (MIT Media Lab avatar study). Three sub-levers: **collaboration** (Treisman's finding that minority students underperformed not from lack of preparation but from studying *alone* while high-performing peers studied *together* — closing that gap by structuring group study closed the performance gap); **social proof** (Cialdini — people rate an activity as more worthwhile when they see others doing it); **competition** (works, but use sparingly — not everyone is competitive, it teaches learners to focus on winning rather than mastery, and it's a poor long-term motivational strategy).
4. **Show it shiny things (visual/tactile).** Know *why* you're adding a visual — decoration (use cautiously; can distract), progression (showing a process over time), or conceptual/metaphorical (infographics that make otherwise-hard-to-process numeric data comprehensible). Visuals distribute cognitive load across visual and verbal channels, help build initial "shelves" for novices, and provide scenario/emotional/behavioral-trigger context efficiently. Humor works if you're genuinely confident it lands with your specific audience (test it — humor is subjective).
5. **Prizes/rewards — use very carefully.** Rewards work best when they're immediate and intrinsic (satisfaction from the activity itself, a new capability, anticipation of using a new skill). **Extrinsic rewards can demotivate** — Ariely's LEGO experiment showed people built significantly fewer shapes for identical pay once their work was treated as disposable/valueless (broken apart immediately) rather than displayed. The "gift card effect": token extrinsic rewards ("finish this and get a $10 card") are fine as after-the-fact appreciation but weak-to-counterproductive as a way to *drive* behavior. Crucially, **you don't get to decide what's intrinsic to the learner** — intrinsic reward design requires giving learners real autonomy and choice (what to learn, what order, what problems to bring). Without autonomy, skip reward-based motivation entirely.

## 6. Designing for Knowledge (Retention, Understanding, and Guidance Level)

**Make the ground fertile before planting content:**

- Draw out what learners already know before telling them anything (ask the group to generate the list first, then edit it — this activates existing shelves and makes removal/addition an active process instead of passive reception).
- Use **metacognition** — awareness of one's own learning/knowledge state. Problem-based learning (originating in medical education) has learners track "what we know / what we think / what we don't know" explicitly on a board, making gaps visible to the learner, not just the instructor.

**Make it sticky — create friction:** Passive, smoothly-delivered information "flows past" a learner like water through a pipe, especially when they think they already know it (which is often exactly when misconceptions are hiding). Research on physics videos found that students who were first confronted with common misconceptions felt *more confused* but retained *significantly more* than students given only a clean explanation — friction that forces reconciliation beats smooth clarity.

- **Show, don't tell** (Cathy Moore's framing): feedback that shows the *result* of a choice and lets the learner draw their own conclusion beats feedback that simply states "correct/incorrect." Fiction-writing principle applies directly to instructional design — give the learner something to actively assemble, don't hand them the conclusion.
- **Social friction** — discussion, debate, and group tasks with a concrete deliverable (create something, teach something back, argue a side, rank/critique examples) add productive friction beyond individual reading.

**The right amount of content is less than you think:** working memory limits mean you will almost always want to convey more than learners can actually carry. Practical rule: **start lean**, test with real learners, and add only where confusion actually surfaces (gaps "bubble up like leaks"); it's much harder to detect and cut unnecessary excess than to detect and patch a gap. There is no fixed "average attention span" number — attention is elastic and depends entirely on whether the Elephant is engaged or being dragged.

**Preventing misconceptions — examples and counter-examples:** a single good example clarifies less than one good example paired with one clear counter-example (the "Goofus and Gallant" pattern). An even stronger variant: open with a bad/flawed example and have learners diagnose what's wrong with it, then build the "good" guidelines from their own diagnostic list.

**How much guidance to give — three positions on the spectrum:**

- **Step-by-step directions (GPS-style)** — fast and reliable in the moment, but if the learner steps off the prescribed path (an unexpected variation), they're stuck with zero ability to self-correct or troubleshoot, and they don't actually *learn* the terrain.
- **Full conceptual/DIY navigation** — genuinely capable of independent troubleshooting, but usually overkill relative to what the learning objective actually requires, and takes far longer to develop.
- **Wayfinding — the practical middle ground.** Give learners enough conceptual structure to self-correct, but require them to do real navigating (not just following turns). Practical techniques:
  - **Examples before concepts** — let learners work several examples and self-identify the pattern, then clarify/correct the concept using those same examples as reference, then apply it further. (Driving yourself to a destination beats being a passenger for later recall — same logic applies to letting learners "drive" through the material.)
  - **Tell them less, not more** — deliberately leave gaps in the given information ("season to taste") so learners must practice the actual skill of filling ambiguity, which is what real troubleshooting requires.
  - **Build confidence directly** — use real tasks (not just "content-based activities"), engineer early wins before showing tricky edge cases, let learners apply material to their own real problems, and let them drive rather than ride along.

## 7. The CCAF Model for Designing Learning Experiences

Michael Allen's **CCAF model** (Context, Challenge, Activity, Feedback) is a practical four-step design process for any learning intervention, from e-learning to classroom to blended programs.

**Step 1 — Determine Context.** Ask four questions before designing anything:

- **General context** — where does this sit in the workflow, how often is it used, what's the purpose? (This tells you the natural spacing interval for practice.)
- **Emotional context** — will the learner be under pressure, bored, stressed? (Design should mimic that emotional state, not a calm classroom default.)
- **Triggers** — what in the environment signals the learner that they need to retrieve and use this knowledge right now?
- **Physical context** — what objects, tools, and screens will the learner actually be interacting with? (Use the real artifacts in training wherever feasible — real interfaces, real headsets, real forms.)

**Step 2 — Define the Challenge.** What's an appropriate real-world accomplishment the learner would actually pursue — not "learn about X," but a task with a real outcome (e.g., "successfully handle N irate customers").

**Step 3 — Design the Activity.** Options to combine, chosen based on constraints:

- **Pre-activity priming** — gather real examples/problems from learners in advance and build the training scenarios out of their own material.
- **E-learning scenarios** — good for recognition-based practice at scale; start with an untimed walkthrough scenario to build interface/process familiarity before adding time pressure or difficulty. Spread scenarios over multiple days rather than one sitting (distributed exposure improves retention and mimics real spacing).
- **Role-plays** — necessary whenever the real skill involves recall, live judgment, or emotional context that e-learning can't simulate. Model a few in front of the group first, and give structured feedback tools to whoever is critiquing.
- **Blended combinations** — use low-cost e-learning to build familiarity, and reserve expensive class/role-play time for active practice rather than re-explaining content already covered.
- **Follow-up activities and job aids** — the best way to sustain a new behavior after formal training ends. Learner-built job aids (using a template) are more meaningful than pre-built ones.
- **Rapid, ugly prototyping** — build rough functional prototypes (even in PowerPoint) as soon as goals/audience/context are defined, rather than spending a long time on polished storyboards first.

**Step 4 — Design the Feedback.**

- **In e-learning**, feedback can come from character/client reactions, a visible progress or "heat" meter, and — most importantly — actual outcome success/failure requiring another attempt, not just a "Good job!" message.
- **In role-plays**, structure feedback with observer checklists, learner self-assessment sheets, and physical/tangible feedback devices (e.g., "heat cards" a role-play partner hands over or withdraws as the learner performs).
- **Ongoing coaching** — real-world supervisor observation (e.g., call monitoring) paired with specific, timely feedback is the strongest reinforcement mechanism available after formal training ends.

## 8. Designing for Skills — Practice and Feedback

**Two required components of skill development: practice and feedback.** An "introduction" to a skill is a legitimate, bounded scope — but if the actual goal is proficiency, practice and feedback are non-negotiable; skipping them just means the learner practices unsupervised, on the job, in whatever haphazard way real work provides.

**What's happening physiologically:** learning something new consumes significant glucose/cognitive resources (like biking uphill); using an already-learned behavior is dramatically more efficient (like coasting downhill) — demonstrated by the brain-imaging Tetris study showing far lower glucose use after weeks of practice on the same task.

**Structure practice around Flow (Csikszentmihalyi):** the core mechanic is balancing challenge against current ability. Too far above ability = frustration; too far below = boredom; right at the edge = engagement. Practical implication: don't structure a learning experience as an unbroken uphill climb of all-new material — alternate new-material stretches with consolidation/coasting stretches, both because it prevents exhaustion and because new material *stands out* against a backdrop of already-mastered material (in a constant uphill of all-new information, nothing stands out because everything is equally novel).

**Spacing practice over time (distributed practice):** practice sessions massed together yield fast initial competence but weaker long-term retention than sessions distributed over time, with real gaps (and ideally sleep) between them — sleep appears to help consolidate learning. Rule of thumb: **time the spacing to match how often the learner will actually need to use the skill in real life.** A skill used weekly should be practiced roughly weekly; a skill used monthly should get reinforcement timed just before the monthly need arises, not crammed once up front.

**Feedback for skill development:**

- **Frequency** — most non-game learning gives feedback far too rarely (a midterm and a final, months apart); look for every opportunity to shorten that interval.
- **Variety** — repeating the identical feedback format (even if it's frequent) becomes background noise; vary the feedback mechanism (sounds, visual cues, character reactions, scores, natural in-world consequences) the way well-designed games do.
- **Follow-up/ongoing coaching** — annual performance reviews are functionally useless for shaping behavior (like a long-range weather forecast); build a specific follow-up schedule (when, what's evaluated, against what criteria) into the design itself.
- **Specific, standardized criteria** — a shared checklist/rubric both improves external feedback and enables learner self-assessment.
- **Assessment caution** — multiple-choice tests are efficient and consistent to administer, but they mainly measure test-taking skill, not the target competency. If the real goal is coaching toward proficiency: have the learner actually perform the task, and give them useful feedback on the performance. That's the whole formula.

**Design for accomplishments — structure content like a game, not a syllabus:** James Paul Gee's "cycles of expertise" — practice a skill to near-automaticity, have it fail/get challenged by a harder situation, learn anew, re-automate, repeat. Practical structure: define **immediate, short-term, medium-term, and long-term goals** that roll up into each other (e.g., a shift-level goal → a week-level goal → a quarter-level goal for a manager-training course), rather than one module per topic covered once. Benefits: learners practice real target behaviors repeatedly across time rather than once; they can't progress until they've actually mastered the prerequisite level; they get multiple, spaced exposures to core concepts (e.g., "safety" shows up at the shift level, the inspection level, and the quarterly-improvement level); and there's always a visible next goal to move toward (the marathon needs mile markers, not just a distant finish line).

## 9. Designing for Motivation and Behavior Change

Two distinct motivation problems: **motivation to learn** (covered via the Rider/Elephant material in Section 5) and **motivation to do** — the "I know, but..." gap, where the learner has the knowledge and skill but isn't acting on it.

**Why "I know, but..." happens:** people learn heavily from direct experience, and experience can teach the wrong lesson when consequences are rare, delayed, or probabilistic (e.g., someone who has texted-and-driven 50 times without incident has "learned" from direct experience that it's fine — right up until it isn't). The Elephant is a creature of immediacy and habit; abstract future consequences carry little weight against present comfort, and switching an existing habit requires real, sustained conscious effort.

**Frameworks for designing behavior change:**

- **Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)** — adoption hinges on two perceived variables: **usefulness** and **ease of use**. Design checklist: Is the new behavior genuinely useful (and if it's mainly useful to the organization, say so honestly rather than pretending otherwise)? How will the learner *know* it's useful (show, don't just assert — case studies, simulated experience, trusted peer testimony)? Is it easy to use, and if not, can practice, job aids, phased rollout, or interface/process redesign close that gap?
- **Diffusion of Innovations (Everett Rogers)** — five perceived attributes drive adoption or rejection: **Relative advantage** (is it visibly, viscerally better — not just statistically better — than the old way?), **Compatibility** (does it fit existing values/workflow, or can learners help redesign it to fit?), **Complexity** (reduce both actual and *perceived* complexity through practice and simplification), **Observability** (can learners see it working — ideally via a respected peer/opinion leader using it first?), **Trialability** (can learners experiment with it on a low-stakes basis before committing?).
- **Self-efficacy** — belief in one's own ability to succeed at the specific task. Dweck's fixed-vs-growth-mindset finding is directly actionable: praising *effort* ("you worked hard") produces learners who seek out harder, growth-oriented challenges; praising *fixed traits* ("you're smart") produces learners who protect their image by avoiding challenge, and who perform *worse* on subsequent tasks. Build self-efficacy by starting with smaller, winnable applications of a new skill before the high-stakes version, and by letting learners observe others succeeding with it first (modeling).
- **Modeling and practice** — walking a learner partway down the real application path during training (having them actually plan out how they'll use the material on their own specific problem) increases follow-through via imagined future use, real practice under support, sunk-cost/loss-aversion investment, and a head start once they're back in the real environment.
- **Social proof and opinion leaders** — identify who your specific audience actually respects (not necessarily the CEO — often a peer with more field experience), and get that person's endorsement, case study, or modeled use visible early. Making visible progress (without publicly shaming laggards) reinforces this further.
- **Visceral experience** — direct or simulated visceral consequences (an interactive reaction-time test, a graphic dramatized consequence video) appear to have more behavioral impact than abstract statistics, though the underlying research base here is thinner than for the other levers.

**Universal rule: change is a process, not an event, and must be reinforced over time.** No single intervention — however well designed — survives without a deliberate reinforcement plan built into the design from the start.

## 10. Designing for Environment

Not every gap should be closed by adding more to the learner's head. Often the better fix is moving knowledge (or the behavior itself) out of the learner and into the surrounding environment.

**Knowledge in the head vs. knowledge in the world (Donald Norman):** a poorly designed system (four stove burners, four ambiguous dials) forces the user to memorize a mapping that could instead be made obvious by design. The design question to ask constantly: *what's everything else we could do, besides training, that would let learners succeed?*

**Match the fix to what each side does well:** people and technology are good at different things — don't ask a human to reliably do what a job aid, interface, or process redesign could offload entirely, and don't over-automate the parts that genuinely require human judgment.

**Proximity is the key variable.** The further a learner has to travel (physically or procedurally) to access needed knowledge, the less likely they are to actually use it — past a certain distance, they'll just ask a coworker or guess instead. Three categories of environmental knowledge, ordered by proximity to the point of use:

1. **Resources** — job aids (Dave Ferguson's framing: they act as *training wheels*, letting a novice approximate expert results without internalizing all expert knowledge, or as *guard rails*, protecting against unsafe/incorrect performance — think airline preflight checklists), decision trees, quick-reference information, augmented reality overlays, and **supply caching** (surfacing advanced options like keyboard shortcuts only at the moment they're contextually relevant, rather than front-loading them).
2. **Prompts/triggers** — Peter Gollwitzer's **implementation intentions**: an "if-then" plan ("if situation Y occurs, I will do Z") is measurably more effective than a bare goal ("I will quit smoking"), and gets stronger the more specific the trigger and response are. The environmental design question: can the trigger itself be given physical presence in the learner's actual environment (a sign, a mark, a checklist posted at the point of decision), and can novelty be used to avoid habituation to that prompt over time?
3. **Embedded behaviors** — the strongest fix: build the correct behavior directly into a tool or interface so the learner doesn't have to recall or decide at all (a single-button soda dispenser instead of a timed pour; an interface that asks three questions and calculates the loan program automatically instead of requiring the rep to remember and compute the steps). This deliberately shifts a *recall* burden onto a *recognition* one — the reverse of what you want during assessment (Section 4), but exactly what you want when designing the permanent working environment.

**Electronic isn't always required.** Simple, durable physical solutions (a literal painted line to follow through a historic district) can out-perform a technically sophisticated app, because they require no compatibility, no charge, no login, and are nearly impossible to misread.

**Clearing the path:** walk the actual process step by step looking for friction points, and treat "that's just how we've always done it" as a red flag demanding scrutiny, not an answer. Ask what could be done *beforehand* to make people more ready, and *afterward* to reinforce the behavior — environment design doesn't end when the formal learning experience does.
