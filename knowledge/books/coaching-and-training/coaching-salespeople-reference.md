# Coaching Salespeople into Sales Champions — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing coaching/training programs, or advising on management practice.*
*Book: "Coaching Salespeople into Sales Champions" — Keith Rosen*

---

## 1. The Executive Sales Coach vs. the Manager

**Core claim: "management is dead."** Managers today mostly manage process, data, reports, and the status quo, not people. If there's no defined process moving people forward, what's being managed is mediocrity. Leadership training fails because it teaches ideology (principles, attributes) instead of a measurable, repeatable skill. Coaching is the missing discipline that converts leadership theory into tactical, daily action.

**Definition of coaching.** Coaching is built on one belief: the question is the answer. The coach's job is to help people find their own answers and develop their own problem-solving ability, using a process of inquiry rather than the dissemination of facts and solutions. Coaching is collaborative, not prescriptive — "like a dance rather than a premeditated process." It builds accountability by giving people a safe forum to honor commitments, and it moves people through possibility rather than obligation.

**Overview of a coach's role:**
- Focuses on strengths, not weaknesses.
- Facilitates ("makes things easier"), rather than directs.
- Brings out the best in people by supporting and maximizing their existing strengths.
- Requests change and growth; informs and guides rather than commands.
- Has the *right* questions, not necessarily all the answers.
- Empowers people to own both their successes and their failures.

**Coach vs. Mentor.** A coach is an expert on people and development: objective, works on the whole person, relationship is by choice, and both parties grow. A mentor is an expert in a field or company who advises based on personal experience, often has an agenda for the mentored person's career, and the relationship is need-driven rather than chosen.

**Consultant vs. Trainer vs. Coach — three distinct roles:**
- **Consultant**: delivers market research, data, and a proposed solution/action plan (may recommend which specialists to bring in).
- **Trainer**: teaches the fundamentals and mechanics — "how to play the game" — through content, role play, scripts, and skill practice.
- **Coach**: ensures the training sticks. Provides ongoing accountability and support after the training event, sharpens the game, and builds the champion mindset. "Trainers teach you how to play the game; coaches teach you how to perfect it."

A given situation may call for training, coaching, consulting, or a blend — the manager's first job is to correctly diagnose which one is needed (see "The Gap" model, Section 4).

**Nine Barriers to Coaching a Sales Team** (obstacles that must be addressed for an internal coaching program to work):
1. No coach-the-coach program — changing a manager's title doesn't change their skill set.
2. Coaching is a choice, not an obligation — remedial/sanctioned coaching breeds resistance.
3. Surrendering your agenda — the coaching relationship must be 100% about the person being coached, which is hard for a manager whose own numbers depend on the team.
4. Coaching people vs. changing people — coaching is a process of discovery, not an overnight fix.
5. Connection has to be real — restricted vulnerability on the employee's side blocks the deeper communication coaching requires.
6. Confidentiality and no-judgment are hard to guarantee when you're also the boss.
7. Not everyone who manages should coach, and not everyone who's pushed into coaching wants to.
8. Full accountability — the manager must be 100% accountable for the team's success/failure; the team reflects the manager's own weaknesses.
9. Competitive managers who withhold tools/best practices from their own people out of ego or insecurity.

**Five Core Characteristics of the World's Greatest Sales Coaches:**
1. You can't take someone where you haven't been yourself — real experience shortens the learning curve.
2. A top coach is a model of what's possible — "do as I do," not "do as I say."
3. Sometimes they really need the answer — masterful coaches know when to give a direct answer rather than lob back another question.
4. Coach from your heart, not your head — beyond scripts and models, the best coaches operate from instinct and authenticity.
5. Develop your own personal style of coaching — style should be an organic expression of the coach's real personality, not a borrowed formula.

## 2. Six Universal Principles of Masterful Coaching

These are the mindset shifts a manager must make before any tactic works. Introduced through a coaching dialogue in which a business owner ("Michele") is stuck deciding whether to terminate or turn around an underperformer.

1. **Make fear your ally.** Fear is just an emotional feedback loop, not a fact — it's the negative assumption of a future that hasn't happened. You can't coach what someone fears (it lives only in the future); you can only coach their *relationship* with fear. Reframe fear as a teacher rather than an adversary.

2. **Be present.** Roughly 95% of waking hours are spent reacting to the past or worrying about the future. Coaching power comes from being fully in the present moment — undistracted by "what was" or "what will be." Presence increases energy, lowers stress, and surfaces possibilities that pushing for an outcome would obscure.

3. **Detach from the outcome.** Attachments that limit a coach: the need to be right, to look good, to be understood, to have agreement, or to avoid hearing "no." Attachment creates a power struggle, blinds you to better solutions, and invalidates the other person. The antidote is to **create possibilities rather than expectations** — an expectation is a rigid, future-based agenda; a possibility is co-created in the present without a fixed grip on the result.

4. **Become process driven, not results driven.** "The result is the process." Chasing the end result keeps you living in the future (i.e., not present, not creative, not able to listen). Managers fixated on results usually lack a trusted process — they push instead of manage a system. Once you trust and honor the process (documented, repeatable systems for prospecting, follow-up, time management, onboarding, etc.), results follow as a natural by-product.

5. **Be creative.** Creativity only happens in the moment — active listening, well-crafted questions, and genuine presence are all downstream of it. The most effective coaches leverage creativity to generate possibilities and outcomes that didn't exist before; living in the future or the past (or being attached to your agenda) kills creativity.

6. **Become fully accountable for everything.** A masterful communicator owns not just the message sent but the message received. Applied to management: if a salesperson fails, the manager is still 100% accountable for the team's success and failure ("avalanches roll downhill") — echoing how JetBlue and Southwest took full public accountability for 2007 weather-related failures rather than deflecting blame.

**The Top 19 Excuses Managers Use to Justify Why Salespeople Fail** — a diagnostic list (e.g., "I inherited this person," "we don't do background checks," "turnover is normal in my industry," "they're independent contractors, it's on them," "I don't have hiring/firing authority," "the market is too competitive"). The common denominator in every excuse is the manager. Buying into any of them is a surrender of personal power; the response to every excuse is: "Ultimately, you have a choice, yes?"

## 3. Six Fatal Coaching Mistakes (and the S.C.A.M.M. Framework)

**Mistake 1 — Believing the S.C.A.M.M.** A S.C.A.M.M. is a self-constructed justification a person hides behind to avoid accountability:
- **S** — Stories
- **C** — Cons
- **A** — Assumptions
- **M** — Meaning (imposed)
- **M** — Mindset

A S.C.A.M.M. is always, at its core, a choice. Common salesperson S.C.A.M.Ms: fear of failure/success (inaction as self-protection), perfectionism/either-or thinking, "I have to do it all myself," "I tried that before, it doesn't work for me," playing it safe (servicing existing accounts instead of prospecting), the accountability trap (to-do lists with no deadlines), no schedule/routine, "everyone comes before me" (can't say no to clients), interrupt-driven/distracted, and playing the victim. **Tactical rule: don't coach the story — coach the person's *relationship* to the story**, to surface the real, factual truth underneath it.

**Mistake 2 — Wanting more for others than they want for themselves.** A coach can over-deliver value to the point of imposing their own agenda: pushing loftier goals, shorter timelines, or more activity than the client actually wants. Warning sign: feeling drained or exhausted after every coaching call — that's a sign your own agenda, not theirs, is driving the session. The real measure of coaching value is defined by the person being coached, not the coach. Sometimes the highest-value coaching move is simply listening — being a safe sounding board rather than "adding" anything.

**Mistake 3 — Coaching vs. judging.** Judgment shows up as "should" statements ("they should be farther along," "they should know that by now") — the coach's own agenda, values, and pace imposed on someone else. Judging blinds you to the individual's actual talents and aspirations and creates resistance. (A legitimate, evidence-based performance judgment — e.g., deciding to terminate after months of documented underperformance — is different from should-based ego judgment.)

**Mistake 4 — Making coaching about the coach.** Any internal monologue that centers "I" ("I don't want to look bad," "I don't want to be the bad guy," "I have numbers to hit") pulls focus off the person being coached and is the #1 roadblock to effective coaching. Shifting focus fully onto the salesperson relieves the coach of the pressure to perform.

**Mistake 5 — Share ideas, not expectations.** An opinion/idea, offered with humility ("here's one thought... I'd love your input so we can make it even better"), keeps dialogue and collaboration open. An expectation delivered as an ultimatum ("here's how it is") shuts conversation down instantly, especially when delivered by someone senior — it removes the space for anyone to contribute a better idea.

**Mistake 6 — Mismanaging expectations: failing to prepare your team for change.** Especially critical for new managers. Before introducing coaching or any new management approach, a manager must proactively surface and manage the team's expectations — otherwise people invent their own (often negative) narrative. Ten diagnostic questions to check whether you've prepared your team for change include: Did you meet one-to-one with each person? Ask how they like to be managed? Ask about their experience with prior managers? Set expectations of the relationship explicitly? Explain your management style and how it differs from your predecessor's? Explain the difference between coaching and traditional management? Enroll them in what's in it for them? Share your intentions and goals for the team? Learn the internal culture from them rather than assuming you already know it?

## 4. Who and What to Coach

**Not everyone is coachable.** Coachability is about who a person is, not their title, age, or experience. The **A.G.R.O.W.T.H. Success Indicator** measures personal coachability:
- **A**ctionability — both willingness to act and the underlying skill/aptitude to execute.
- **G**ap — the space between where the person is and where they want to be (see below); this is where coaching happens.
- **R**esponsibility and ownership — a symbiotic pair; without both, coaching sessions become an excuse factory and the coach becomes a scapegoat.
- **W**illingness — evidence of drive and determination to reach the stated goal.
- **T**rust — earned, not assumed; if you can't trust someone, don't coach them.
- **H**onesty — the person's willingness to be open, vulnerable, and self-examining rather than defensive.

**Don't coach the squeaker.** Managers routinely over-invest time in toxic or needy underperformers ("C players") and under-invest in top performers and promising new hires. Time should go to people who demonstrate the coachability indicators above, not just to whoever is loudest.

**Coaching the whole person — three things that block sales performance:**
1. Lacking core selling skills/competencies.
2. Lacking the right strategy/system (including the specific language/dialogue used).
3. Lacking the right mindset — limiting or negative thinking that undermines otherwise-sound skills and strategy. ("How you think determines what you get.") Most training programs address only #1 and #2 and miss #3 entirely.

**The Gap model — "coach the gap."** The gap is the space between: what people know vs. don't know/realize is possible; what they do vs. what they need to do; the resources/skills they have vs. lack; and current results vs. desired results. Identifying the gap in every coaching interaction (rather than assuming you already know the solution) is the single unifying model across all coaching scenarios.

**Deciding: coach, train, or advise/consult?** Use the gap to diagnose which is needed:
- Pure **training** gap: someone has never done the task before (e.g., a rookie who's never cold-called) — needs foundational skills and best practices, not coaching.
- **Training + coaching** gap: someone whose approach worked before but has stalled — needs a documented, repeatable process (training) plus mindset work around fear/scarcity thinking (coaching).
- Pure/primarily **coaching** gap: someone with the skill and experience but a limiting belief blocking performance (e.g., a veteran closer who's lost his process for the objections he's newly encountering) — needs belief-level coaching plus some tactical refresh.

**What exactly can you coach?** Ten coachable dimensions: the who (values, standards, boundaries, integrity), the attitude (belief/mindset/philosophy), the lesson (are they learning, or repeating?), the ideal characteristics of a sales leader, the skill, the activity, the strategy, the commitment (energy/consistency), the communication (language/delivery), and the relationships a person has with intangible concepts and their own stories/S.C.A.M.Ms.

**Top 10 Characteristics of Highly Effective Salespeople (sales champions):**
1. Continually invest in their own learning and development.
2. Are masterful communicators.
3. Are creative — selling is creating new solutions and possibilities.
4. Maintain a positive, healthy attitude.
5. Are passionate and deliver unconditional value for its own sake.
6. Create an experience that allows the sale to occur (rather than force it).
7. Are responsive, proactive, adaptable, flexible — not reactive.
8. Embody what customers want most: integrity, honesty, resourcefulness, reliability.
9. Are process driven — specific measurable goals, a documented selling system, tools/templates, a prospecting system, a personal vision.
10. Are masters of time management and organization.

## 5. The Seven Types of Sales Managers

A typology used to diagnose a manager's dominant (often unconscious) communication style, its costs, and the ideal style to move toward.

1. **The Problem-Solving Manager** — believes their core job is firefighting; reflexively hands salespeople the answer. Cost: trains staff into dependency, and the manager becomes the scapegoat when the manager's own solution fails. Fix: respond to problems with questions instead of answers — "the question is the answer." **Three Rules of Sales Coaching**: (1) let your salespeople do all the work, (2) let your salespeople do all the work, (3) see rules 1 and 2. Distinguish **problem-oriented questions** ("Why can't you get this right?") from **solution-oriented questions** ("What's the lesson here?" "What needs to change?" "What system can you put in place?") — problem-oriented questions only collect evidence for why something can't be done.

2. **The Pitchfork Manager** — manages through consequence, threat, scarcity, and fear (a "push" structure). Costs enormous energy, only works while the manager is actively pushing, and momentum dies the moment pressure stops. Fix: shift to a "pull"/attraction-based model — motivate through pleasure, personal vision, and goals people actually want, not the consequences they're avoiding. Communicate from **abundance** (what will be present/gained) rather than **scarcity** (what's missing or will be lost) — e.g., reframe "if you don't hit quota you're fired" into "if you hit quota, here's what becomes possible." Ask each salesperson directly what motivates them and how they want to be coached rather than assuming a one-size-fits-all incentive works (a Maritz poll cited: feeling genuinely listened to and cared about mattered nine times more to employees than pay satisfaction).

3. **The Pontificating Manager** — charismatic, inconsistent, thrives on winging it, gives unsolicited advice without real listening. Weakness: masterful listening. **Eight Barriers to Masterful Listening**: doing something else while listening; waiting for a pause to jump in; discomfort with silence; faking listening to get your own point in; selective listening (hearing only what you want); ignoring body language/tone; letting environmental noise interfere; and (the most insidious) **listening through filters** — prejudging based on age, appearance, past experience, or a person's status. (See Section 8 for the full listening framework.)

4. **The Presumptuous Manager** — ego- and production-driven, puts personal needs above the team's, breeds unhealthy internal competition, withholds best practices from fear of being outdone. Underlying weakness: an inability to separate fact from assumption/story. Illustrated by the "Peter" story (Section 9) of costly, unexamined selling assumptions.

5. **The Perfect Manager** — a technical/product expert and "talking spec sheet," constantly refining approach, low risk tolerance, poor delegator ("if you want it done right, do it yourself"), never sticks with one method long enough to know if it works. Antidote: vulnerability-based leadership (Section 7) — focus on being human, not perfect.

6. **The Passive Manager** (also "Parenting" or "Pleasing" Manager) — avoids confrontation at all costs, takes the salesperson's side reflexively, sends unread articles instead of having real conversations, tolerates toxic behavior to keep the peace. Cost: erosion of the whole team's culture. Antidote: the **Coaching Edge** (Section 7) — getting explicit permission to have a tough conversation, which removes the fear/tension around confrontation for both parties.

7. **The Proactive Manager** — the synthesis/ideal type, combining the best trait of each of the others without the corresponding flaw: the pitchfork manager's persistence and edge, the presumptuous manager's confidence, the pontificating manager's enthusiasm and presence, the problem-solver's drive to support others, the passive manager's humanity and sensitivity, and the perfect manager's product/industry mastery and focus — all delivered with genuine humility and detachment from ego.

## 6. Push vs. Pull: A Model of Motivation

Two motivational philosophies: **push** (force, fear, consequence, threat — requires constant external effort and produces only temporary movement) vs. **pull/attraction** (pleasure, vision, goals people actually want — requires less ongoing effort and produces lasting, internally-driven movement). Push structures rob people of power; pull structures empower them.

**Practical application:** ask your salespeople directly what motivates them and how they want to be managed/coached, rather than assuming money or any single lever works universally. Example: a security company owner had staff write their own job descriptions and personal career goals — the results revealed wildly different individual drivers (flextime, career growth, job stability, acknowledgment) that a single blanket incentive program would have missed entirely.

**Ten questions to uncover each person's internal drive and preferred coaching style** (used in one-to-one meetings): What do you want to be doing that you aren't? What do you want to strengthen? What matters most to you in life/career? What three things right now would give you deep fulfillment? What's your action plan? What obstacles might block you? How can I best support you? How can I hold you accountable without it feeling like micromanaging? How do you want me to raise it if you don't follow through on a commitment?

**Communicating from abundance, not scarcity.** Same message, two framings — scarcity: "If you don't get organized, your workload and stress will keep increasing." Abundance: "If you keep following your routine, you'll eliminate the overwhelming workload and feel more in control." Abundance framing works for ongoing internal culture; scarcity/pain framing has a legitimate but narrower use in actual sales conversations (surfacing the cost of a prospect's inaction), not in daily management communication.

**Acknowledgment must be unconditional, measurable, and specific**, not generic ("good job!"). Reinforce the *specific* behavior, technique, or mindset shift that produced the result, so the person knows exactly what to repeat. Reasons managers under-acknowledge: not knowing how, fear people will slack off if praised too much, believing it's unimportant, or fear it won't land as genuine.

**"The Code of Rightness" — make your people right, even when they're not.** Eight ways managers unintentionally make people wrong (and should recognize and avoid): comparative statements ("this is better than last time"), blame statements, dredging up past failures, targeting only the negative, negative/problem-oriented questions, always/never statements, negative acknowledgment (praise immediately undercut by a "but"), and condescending humor. Making someone wrong shuts down listening and puts them on the defensive; it's driven by the coach's own need to be right. Instead, respect the other person's viewpoint even while correcting a factual error, using questions like: "What else do you feel might be possible?" "Is it possible there may be another approach here?" "I'm not too sure what you mean — can you say more about that?"

## 7. Vulnerability-Based Leadership and the Coaching Edge

**Trust is the core issue in most workplaces** (cited Maritz poll: only ~12% of employees strongly agreed their company's leaders were completely ethical/honest; only ~7% felt senior leaders' actions were fully consistent with their words). No incentive or tool substitutes for trust.

**The only way to build deep trust is through vulnerability** — a manager modeling their own humanity (mistakes, uncertainty, humanness) before expecting it from the team. Vulnerability must come from the manager first; salespeople won't risk it until they've seen it's safe. Evidence of an emerging vulnerability-based culture: people taking ownership of mistakes, asking for help, acknowledging skill gaps, and addressing uncomfortable interpersonal issues directly rather than avoiding them. Structured exercises that build this culture: sharing personal stories in meetings, peer-to-peer critique exercises (one contribution + one growth area per person), or having every team member share one weakness so the team can support each other on it.

**The Coaching Edge — getting permission to be direct.** Rather than confronting bluntly, the coach first asks for explicit permission to deliver a hard truth or push harder than usual. This removes defensiveness because the person has consciously opted in. Example openers: "Can I point something out that may be tough to look at right now?" "I have something to tell you that's going to sting a little, but it's for your own good — can you be open to hearing it?" "If you want me to coach you toward this goal faster, I need to know you'll be okay being challenged and held accountable more than you're used to — is that fair?"

**The "wanting for" statement** — a stand-alone communication tool (also step 4 of the Enrollment process, Section 10): explicitly declaring what you want *for* the other person, not from them. Format: "What I want for you is [specific positive future state]." Used to deliver a turnaround message, prepare a team for change, reinforce a stand, reignite someone's motivation, or open a coaching conversation.

**The "I'm sensing that..." statement** — a non-confrontational way to surface something you suspect is being withheld, without accusing. Structure: name what you're sensing as *your* feeling/observation, then ask if there's truth to it ("I'm sensing that there may be something else getting in the way of scheduling this — is that true?"). Signs something's being withheld: sudden shifts in performance, attitude, or behavior; reluctance to do something previously routine; broken commitments that weren't a problem before; a mismatch between stated intentions and actions.

## 8. Masterful Listening

**Listening for what's not said, not just what is said** is a coach's second core proficiency (after questions). Five things to listen *for* in every conversation:
1. **Needs** — what people actually need (vs. what they say they want).
2. **What's important to them** — their real priorities and goals.
3. **The truth (and assumptions of the truth)** — separating fact from story/S.C.A.M.M.
4. **Pain, concerns, and fears** — what's being withheld.
5. **The gap** — the space between present and desired state (see Section 4).

**Practical listening disciplines:**
- **Just the facts** — before reacting, ask "Do I have evidence for this, or am I assuming?"
- **Encourage silence** — wait 3–5 seconds before responding; this signals safety, produces more disclosure, and prevents the shallow, reflexive "rebuttal" response.
- **Focus on message over messenger** — evaluate content, not the person's age/appearance/status/voice/car.
- **Use clarifiers** to confirm you received the intended message and to make the other person feel heard: "What I'm hearing you say is..." "Can you say more about that?" "Here's what I'm hearing..." "How do you mean?"
- **Making someone feel heard** is a level above simply listening effectively — it requires active confirmation, not just accurate reception.

## 9. Costly Assumptions and the Universal Selling Laws

Illustrated through "Presumptive Peter," a salesperson shadow-coached for a full day who unknowingly made 17 distinct costly assumptions across his entire sales cycle (e.g., assuming the goal of a cold call is to get the appointment; assuming a prospect is qualified or the sole decision maker; assuming collateral material contains what the prospect wants; assuming silence/non-response means disinterest rather than an unclear process; assuming his product is "a commodity" rather than researching real differentiation; assuming what the coach would tell him to do, rather than staying open). The tactical fix for every one of these: replace the assumption with a well-crafted question.

**Universal selling laws that challenge conventional wisdom:**
- Closing isn't something you do *to* someone — it happens naturally *with* them.
- The greatest salespeople aren't great closers; they're skilled openers of new selling opportunities.
- Salespeople don't overcome objections — prospects and customers overcome their own objections (with the right questions).
- Salespeople often create the very objections that block their own sales.
- Well-crafted questions, not statements, defuse objections.
- Dropping price often loses the sale rather than saving it.
- "Put yourself in the prospect's shoes" is itself a costly assumption that destroys selling opportunities (you're guessing their perspective rather than asking).

**Managerial version of the same trap** — assumptions managers routinely make about their own people: that salespeople understood a training session, have the ability to execute a delegated task, share the manager's own level of knowledge, are using the most effective strategy, are consistently engaging in the right activities, or are as loyal/committed as assumed. The universal fix, again, is **be curious** — ask rather than assume, especially with emotionally loaded words ("stressed," "overwhelmed," "affordable") that carry different meanings for every individual.

## 10. Facilitating an Effective Coaching Conversation

**The Anatomy of a Coaching Session (9 steps, used as loose guidelines, not rigid law):**
1. Opening/small talk.
2. Confirm expectations for the session.
3. Review the prep form and prior week's progress in detail.
4. The L.E.A.D.S. coaching model (see below) — the core conversation.
5. Identify value and takeaways — confirm expectations were met.
6. Take their pulse — how are they feeling; what do they want more/less of.
7. Action steps and fieldwork — specific, measurable commitments for next time.
8. Schedule the next meeting.
9. Conclusion / final thoughts.

**The Coaching Prep Form** — a short (~10 minute) form the salesperson completes and sends *before* each session, covering: achievements/wins since last call; what upgrades in thinking/attitude they've made; their focused intention for today's session; a self-rated accountability score (1–10) plus what they're most proud of and where they fell short; a self-rated "fearless" score (1–10) plus their most persistent fear; current challenges/tolerations; goals and opportunities; commitments for next call; whether their activity is actually moving them toward their goals; and a "shadow coaching" section for specific real situations they want coached on. Rationale: it saves ~15 minutes per session, builds accountability on both sides, and prevents the salesperson from later blaming the coach for unmet expectations that were never made explicit.

**The L.E.A.D.S. Coaching Model** — a five-step structure for any single coaching conversation:
- **L — Listen.** For what's said and unsaid; resist filling gaps with assumption; tolerate silence.
- **E — Evoke.** Ask questions to surface more information or clarify — the question is the answer.
- **A — Answer/Respond.** Use clarifiers to confirm you understood; acknowledge what was shared; keep the discussion anchored to the actual root issue.
- **D — Discuss.** Go deeper — is this a blind spot, a S.C.A.M.M., a training gap, or a coaching gap? Let the person arrive at their own diagnosis in their own words.
- **S — Support.** Provide the resource, structure, observation, or challenge needed — but hand the *solution* back as a question wherever possible ("How do you envision accomplishing this?" "What do you feel you need?" "Who do you need to be to achieve this?").

**Frequency of coaching**: a minimum of two individual coaching sessions per month per salesperson is the floor; weekly is ideal. Team size and workload determine feasibility, but the book's position is unambiguous — if you're not meeting individually with each salesperson weekly or biweekly, "you are not coaching them," regardless of what else you call your process (sales meetings + annual reviews do not constitute a coaching program).

## 11. The Art of Enrollment

**Definition:** Enrollment is an authentic, powerful form of communication that grabs attention, stimulates interest, and empowers people to embrace, support, and take ownership of an idea, cause, or change — as opposed to "selling" or dictating it to them. "People don't want to be sold. They want to be enrolled."

**When to use it:** relocating salespeople, launching an incentive program, defusing hostility, communicating a policy or compensation change, introducing a coaching/training initiative, hiring, firing (to reduce collateral gossip/damage), requesting a behavior change, getting someone to own a problem they've been avoiding, or holding someone more accountable.

**The Six Steps of an Enrollment Conversation:**
1. **Get connected** — open with a relevant personal story (yours or someone else's) to build safety and rapport.
2. **State the possibility** — a broad, compelling statement of the desired outcome, opened with language that suspends doubt ("Imagine...", "What if...", "Wouldn't it be great if...").
3. **Ask permission to have the conversation** — confirm it's a good time and that the person is open to hearing it.
4. **Take a stand** — narrow the broad possibility into a specific "wanting for" statement (Section 7) that names exactly what you want for this person.
5. **Have the conversation** — deliver the actual idea, request, or invitation.
6. **Get complete** — confirm next steps, gauge their response, and set a completion timeline ("How do you see handling this?" "What concerns do you have?" "When can you have this back to me?").

**Supporting principles:** surrender your personal agenda entirely; it's never about you but about a shared/greater goal; genuine vulnerability is the purest form of self-expression; frame around the pleasure/vision you're creating, not the consequence being avoided; and read the other person's state of mind before attempting to enroll them (timing matters as much as wording).

**Written enrollment** (e.g., company-wide memos for appraisals, vision-building exercises) follows the same logic: state genuine positive intent, explain the "why" and the benefit to the reader, make participation feel voluntary and safe (confidentiality, no link to compensation when applicable), and close with an explicit, easy call to action and deadline.

## 12. The Seduction of Potential

**Central distinction: potential vs. possibility.** Potential is unproven and lives entirely in the future — "something you have not seen yet nor have evidence to support." A possibility is created and evidenced in the present. Managers routinely collapse the two, which is the real seduction: hiring, retaining, or continuing to invest in someone based on *hoped-for* future performance rather than *demonstrated* present evidence.

**"Mother Teresa Syndrome"** — the manager's internal narrative of "just one more week, I can save him," which escalates the sunk-cost trap. "Wait and see" is not a strategy; hope is not a plan. The hard cost of complacency: every week of tolerating an underperformer costs more in lost sales, morale, resources, and opportunity than the (often smaller and more contained) cost of a clean decision.

**You can't build a business on potential.** If someone hasn't been living their potential *before* you hired them, nothing about hiring them changes that; it's management's job to build systems (a structured 30/60/90-day new-hire program, ongoing coaching) that let people live their potential daily — not to keep gambling on unproven future upside.

**Ten Trigger Points of Seduction** — traits that, on their own, seduce managers into over-tolerating someone despite missed numbers: loyalty, raw skill, effort/commitment, passion, product knowledge, likeable "overcompensating" personality traits, perceived (unrealized) network value, fear of the alternative, ego (unwillingness to admit a hiring mistake), and Mother Teresa syndrome itself.

**Master the art of abandonment.** The most productive people and managers are skilled at letting go of what no longer works — toxic relationships, failing strategies, outdated limiting beliefs — the same discipline as decluttering physical possessions that no longer serve you. Exercise: list 10 things (behaviors, beliefs, people, tolerations) you can consciously abandon.

## 13. The 30-Day Turnaround Program

A structured four-week process to either turn around an underperformer or reach a clear, evidence-based decision to let them go — replacing the seduction of potential with certainty built from a documented process.

**Step 1 — Initiate and schedule the first conversation** using an **enrollment conversation** (Section 11), not an ambush. Script skeleton: "I'm fully committed to supporting you and giving you what you need to perform like the producer I know you can be. What's more important is your own commitment. Let's set measurable targets and a plan; at the end of four weeks we'll regroup and determine the best path forward for you — building on new success, or a different fit elsewhere. Is this something you're willing to commit to?" Always give the person a genuine out: "If you're not committed to this, that's fine too — I support your decision either way." This framing shifts later accountability entirely onto the salesperson's own choice.

**Week One — establish measurable targets** across concrete sales activities (appointments, follow-up contacts, cold calls, networking, referral asks, collateral development, proposals requested/delivered, and process components like scripts/templates/time-management routine). Let the salesperson generate as much of the plan themselves as possible. Close with commitment questions: "What can you do this week that would give you a sense of accomplishment by our next meeting?" "What would be a stretch for you?"

**Week Two — review, and expect setbacks.** If commitments weren't honored, resist the urge to lecture; use coaching questions to find the real root cause (often a limiting belief, e.g., call reluctance rooted in "if I keep calling, I'm annoying them" or "the goal of a cold call is to get the appointment"). Reframe the belief through questioning rather than telling ("If the way you're thinking about this isn't working, what would be a different way to look at it?"), then move to a tactical fix (documented scripts, qualifying questions, time blocks). Consider adding brief daily check-ins (e.g., 5 minutes) between weekly sessions to sustain momentum on a fragile new habit.

**Week Three — build on momentum.** Continue uncovering gaps in the person's process (e.g., no documented follow-up cadence, no primary objective per call, unexamined limiting beliefs about "being annoying," no way to qualify ongoing fit/timing). Use the "prospect's-eyes" reversal technique — have the person imagine receiving their own sales approach as a prospect — to generate self-diagnosed insight. Close the week by both reconfirming a stretch commitment and acknowledging genuine progress.

**Week Four — the decision meeting.** If targets were hit, acknowledge and celebrate genuinely and specifically, then invite the person to continue ongoing coaching (e.g., biweekly) as a permanent part of their role, not just a remedial phase. If targets were not hit, the prior weeks' documented evidence lets the manager deliver the decision as a shared, fact-based conclusion rather than a unilateral verdict — sample framings include: "Based on what we agreed and the results over the last four weeks, it looks like you've already made your decision — what do you think?" or "We both committed to specific measurables. Your results tell us this isn't the right fit — would you agree?" Where the fit issue is role-specific rather than a character/effort problem, consider redeploying the person elsewhere in the company ("fire them and then hire them") rather than losing them outright.

**Seven components of any effective turnaround/coaching program:**
1. Consistency — weekly or biweekly 1:1s treated as non-negotiable, not optional.
2. Clear, mutual commitment from both manager and salesperson.
3. Focus on accountability and specific, measurable objectives.
4. Authentic, unconditional support.
5. Strategic, well-timed questions (plus tactical training content where the gap calls for it).
6. Weekly measurable action steps that create continuous momentum.
7. A realistic timeline that gives both sides a fair shot at a successful turnaround.

**Caveat:** not everyone is salvageable, and not every failure is the manager's fault — sometimes it's a poor culture fit, a genuine skills ceiling, a communication/language mismatch, a wrong hire from the start, or purely personal circumstances. The point of the structured program is to replace guesswork and emotional decision-making with an evidence-based, time-boxed, and humane process either way.

## 14. The Playbook of Coaching Questions (Reference Categories)

The book's appendix organizes dozens of coaching questions into ~38 situational categories. Rather than reproduce the full list, the operative categories and their purpose:

- **Accountability / Ownership** — shift responsibility back to the salesperson ("What role are you playing in this?" "What are you committed to?").
- **Acknowledgment** — reinforce specific positive change ("What are you most proud of accomplishing?").
- **Action / Fieldwork** — convert insight into measurable next steps ("What are the three activities you can commit to this week?").
- **Attachment / Control** — surface what someone is gripping too tightly ("What are you attached to?").
- **Attitude / Limiting Beliefs** — challenge the story behind the behavior ("How is that way of thinking working for you? What is it costing you?").
- **Challenging** — stretch someone past their stated limit ("If you could, how would you do it?").
- **Consequence / Cost** — surface the price of inaction without resorting to threats ("What is this costing you to keep doing it this way?").
- **Empowerment** — hand the answer back to the person ("If you were me, how would you coach yourself on this?").
- **Enrollment** — build the space of possibility ("What would be possible if...?").
- **Fear** — separate the feeling from the (unreal, future-based) content of the fear.
- **Truth-seeking / Uncovering the S.C.A.M.M.** — cut through story to fact ("What's the real truth?" "What else is true?").
- **Tactical / hands-on** — extract concrete process detail ("What does your cold call actually sound like?").
- **The Coaching Edge** — earn permission to deliver a hard message (see Section 7).
- **Setting Coaching-Relationship Expectations** — establish, at the outset, how someone wants to be coached, held accountable, and challenged.
- **Vision** — orient someone beyond the current month/quarter ("If you could describe your ideal career, what would it look like?").

**The 80/20 Rule of Coaching Questions:** in any true coaching conversation, the person being coached should do about 80% of the talking; the coach about 20%. A single well-crafted question can create more breakthrough than ten mediocre ones — resist the urge to cram in every possible question. Treat the full question list as a buffet: select what fits the moment rather than trying to use all of it.
