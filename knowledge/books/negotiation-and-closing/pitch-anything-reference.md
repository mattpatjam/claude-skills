# Pitch Anything — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing negotiation/closing/sales processes, or advising on deal strategy.*
*Book: "Pitch Anything: An Innovative Method for Presenting, Persuading, and Winning the Deal" — Oren Klaff*

---

## 1. The Core Problem: Croc Brain vs. Neocortex

Klaff's foundational claim: pitches fail not because the idea is weak but because of a structural mismatch in how brains send and receive information.

- **Three-part brain model** (evolutionary layers):
  - Crocodile brain ("croc brain") — oldest, handles survival filtering, fight-or-flight, basic emotion. Low capacity, primitive reasoning.
  - Midbrain — determines meaning and social context (friend, threat, stranger).
  - Neocortex — newest, handles abstract reasoning, language, problem-solving. This is where pitches are *composed*.
- **The disconnect**: messages built in your neocortex are always received first by the other person's croc brain, not their neocortex — regardless of how sophisticated your content is. The croc brain acts as gatekeeper for everything.
- **Croc brain's filtering instructions** (its de facto operating policy):
  1. If it's not dangerous, ignore it.
  2. If it's not new and exciting, ignore it.
  3. If it is new, summarize as fast as possible and drop the details (up to 90% of message content gets discarded before reaching higher brain areas).
  4. Don't escalate anything to the neocortex unless it's genuinely unexpected and out of the ordinary.
- **Consequence**: complicated, abstract, detail-heavy pitches get coded by the croc brain as one of three things — boring (ignored), dangerous (triggers fight/flight and routes to the amygdala, the fear center), or complicated (radically and lossy-summarized). None of these outcomes helps you.
- **Rules of engagement for the croc brain** — it is:
  - Going to ignore you if it can.
  - Focused only on the big picture, needing high-contrast, clearly differentiated choices.
  - Emotional, and that emotion defaults to fear.
  - Short-attention-span, craving novelty, focused on the here-and-now.
  - In need of concrete, verifiable facts — allergic to abstraction.
- **Practical implication**: every pitch must be engineered for the croc brain first — simple, visual, novel, non-threatening, low cognitive load — before it can ever reach the neocortex for a rational "yes."
- **Exception**: if the offering itself is so inherently dopamine-loaded (a Ferrari, a hot gadget) the croc brain may wave almost anything through. Absent that, you must follow the method.

## 2. Frame Control: Principles and Frame Collisions

A **frame** is the instrument that packages your power, authority, status, and point of view around a situation. Frame control is presented as the single most important skill in the entire method — it precedes and enables everything else (status, attention, closing).

- **Five governing principles of frames**:
  1. Everyone uses a frame whether they realize it or not.
  2. Every social encounter brings different frames into contact.
  3. Frames do not coexist for long in the same interaction.
  4. Only one frame survives a collision — the stronger absorbs the weaker (frames don't merge or blend).
  5. The surviving frame governs the entire interaction ("frame control").
- **How to tell who owns the frame**: whoever is *reacting* to the other person's words and behavior is subordinate. Whoever is *not reacting*, and to whom others react, owns the frame. This is the single diagnostic test to apply in real time during any meeting.
- **Frame collisions are primal, not rational**: they are settled by the croc brain, not by logic. A strong frame is impervious to rational argument — weak arguments simply bounce off it. This is why over-explaining, justifying, or citing your credentials during a collision *weakens* your frame (having to explain your authority proves you don't fully hold it — illustrated by the "cop frame": a traffic cop never has to justify his authority, and that's precisely why his frame always wins).
- **Frame-based business vs. traditional sales technique**: conventional sales training (rapport-building, feature/benefit pitches, trial closes, "law of large numbers" — make 100 calls to land 2 sales) is what people do *after* they've already lost the frame collision. It is a subordinate-position survival tactic, not a strategy. Frame-based business stacks the social dynamics in your favor *before* the pitch even starts, making volume-based selling unnecessary.
- **Practical technique for winning a collision — "plowing"**: once you commit to your frame, hold it with total consistency regardless of social pressure or discomfort. No self-doubt, no backpedaling. Like an ox plowing a field: always forward, never stopping.
- **Small acts of denial and defiance** are the primary mechanical tool for provoking and winning frame collisions early in an interaction — done with humor, not hostility (see Section 3 for concrete examples). They shift status instantly and put the other party into a "hot," fully-engaged cognitive state.

## 3. The Three Opposing Frames and How to Defeat Them

Klaff identifies three frames you will most commonly meet walking into a business situation, and pairs each with a counter-frame. A fourth counter-frame (the prize frame) works against all three and is covered in Section 4.

- **The Power Frame** — comes from the person with the big ego, status derived from others' deference (arrogance, disinterest, "I'm more important than you" energy). Power-frame people expect fawning obedience and never anticipate resistance — which is exactly their weakness.
  - *Counter: the power-busting frame.* Never reinforce their status rituals (deferential small talk, waiting quietly, letting them dictate terms). Instead, cause a mildly shocking, non-hostile disruption: a small denial ("Uh-uh, not yet") or a small defiance ("You said 15 minutes? I've only got 12.").
  - Concrete examples from the book: pulling a pitch book back out of a distracted trader's hands and negotiating over his idle doodle instead of the deal; cutting an apple in half on a hostile executive's desk mid-pitch and eating it without asking.
  - Escalation pattern: power shifts start small and compound — once the target's croc brain registers "something just happened," attention snaps fully onto you (a "hot cognition" is triggered).
- **The Time Frame** — usually surfaces *after* an initial frame collision, when the target tries to reclaim control by pressuring you on the clock ("I only have 10 minutes").
  - *Counter: the time-constraining frame.* Never thank someone for "fitting you in" — that reinforces their power. Instead, respond by qualifying *them*: "There's no sense meeting unless we trust each other. Are you good to work with? Can you keep appointments?" This flips who is on the clock.
  - Also applies to your own pacing: when you sense attention dropping, set your own time boundary and end the meeting first, rather than reacting to someone else announcing "we're out of time."
- **The Analyst Frame** — appears when the audience does a "deep drill-down" into cold, technical, numerical detail. It's common with engineers, financial analysts, due-diligence teams. It is described as the most dangerous frame because it kills a pitch's emotional momentum entirely.
  - Underlying mechanic: **hot cognitions and cold cognitions cannot occur simultaneously** in the human brain. The moment you let someone start calculating or analyzing, they go cold, and cold audiences don't buy on emotion.
  - *Counter: the intrigue frame*, delivered as a short, prepared, personal narrative (see Section 6 for the full narrative formula). When drilled on details, answer briefly at the highest level possible, then redirect: "These numbers you can verify later — right now, are we a good fit?"
  - Do not chase detail threads. Answer fast, high-level only, and pivot back to the relationship/decision question.

## 4. The Prize Frame and Prizing

The prize frame is the universal counter-frame — usable against power, time, or analyst frames, and also the core closing posture for the entire pitch.

- **Core flip**: normally the buyer/investor is treated as the prize and the pitcher chases them. Prizing inverts this — you frame *yourself* as the scarce, high-value asset, and the buyer as the one who must earn your attention.
- **Underlying human behaviors this exploits**:
  1. We chase what moves away from us.
  2. We want what we cannot have.
  3. We only value things that are difficult to obtain.
- **Prizing 101 (basics)**:
  - Make the target qualify himself to you: "Tell me about yourself — I'm picky about who I work with."
  - Protect your status: never let the buyer unilaterally change the agenda, meeting time, or attendees. Be willing to withdraw if they try.
- **Prizing 201 (refinements)**:
  - Avoid trial closes ("So, are we in the ballpark?") — they signal eagerness and are seen as needy and manipulative.
  - Instead of a trial close, issue a statement, not a question: "So many buyers, yet only one of me. How are you going to compete for my attention." (Delivered with humor, and deliberately not phrased as a question, since questions seek validation.)
  - Make the target perform a real task to earn the deal (Klaff's example: BMW requires a signed care commitment before you can even buy certain special-edition cars).
  - Reframe money itself: money is a commodity, available in a thousand places — it has no value until it's put to work on your deal. There is only one of you. Internal mantra: *"I am the prize. You are trying to impress me. You are trying to win my approval."*
- **Mechanism for why it works**: once the croc brain is engaged (via novel, non-boring input), it reacts one of two ways — curiosity/desire, or fear/dislike. Prizing engineers the first reaction so the target's croc brain casts you as something to be chased rather than something to fear or dismiss.
- **What to do when the decision-maker doesn't show** (a classic frame-loss moment): don't proceed as if nothing happened. Reframe with power/time/prize immediately — e.g., "I can give you 15 minutes to get organized. If we can't start by then, let's call it a day." This typically triggers the room to scramble to accommodate you, restoring your frame instantly.

## 5. Status, Local Star Power, and Beta Traps

Status is presented as the platform from which your frame either succeeds or fails — the same argument, told from a strong-status position, lands completely differently than from a weak one.

- **Global status vs. situational status**:
  - Global status = fixed social rank (wealth, fame, position) — not something you can fake or borrow in the moment.
  - Situational status = fluid, domain-specific status you can seize temporarily regardless of your global rank (the "golf pro and cardiac surgeon" example: on the golf course, the golf pro outranks the surgeon completely, because domain expertise reverses the usual hierarchy). This is called **local star power**, and it's the lever available to anyone regardless of who they normally are.
- **Beta traps**: engineered rituals designed to keep visitors in a subordinate position — the lobby wait, the "sign the visitor log," the empty conference room, the public café meeting, the trade-show booth. Walmart's Bentonville HQ is cited as the most extreme institutionalized version ("the frame supercollider"): reception desks, tiny meeting cells, forced waiting, all engineered to strip vendor status before price negotiations even start.
- **Six steps to seizing situational status** (in order):
  1. Politely ignore power rituals and beta traps (don't sit where told, don't defer to gatekeeper scripts).
  2. Be unaffected by the target's global status — don't let their wealth/fame/title intimidate you into a subordinate posture.
  3. Look for chances to perform small denials/defiances that elevate your status.
  4. As soon as you take power, pivot the conversation into your domain of expertise, where you're unassailable.
  5. Apply the prize frame — position yourself as the reward for doing business with you.
  6. Get the customer to *verbally qualify* your alpha status (lightly, with humor) — e.g., "Remind me again why I want to do business with you?"
- **Rules for holding status once seized**:
  - Always be on time — lateness gives away power before you start.
  - Move fast: establish status immediately, don't let the target's status compound the longer you wait.
  - Avoid idle social rituals/small talk that reinforce someone else's rank.
  - Enjoy the work visibly — enjoyment itself is attractive and status-building.
  - Situational status resets to zero the moment the interaction ends — you rebuild it fresh every time, even minutes later with the same person.
- **The alpha's structural advantages**: statements are trusted without challenge, emotional tone sets the room's mood, and attention snaps toward the alpha automatically (demonstrated by the research example of well-dressed men jaywalking and low-status pedestrians following them into traffic — but only if the leader is dressed the part).

## 6. The Narrative / Intrigue Story Technique

A specific, repeatable structure for building the intrigue frame described in Section 3 — meant to be pre-written and carried into every pitch, not improvised.

- **Six required elements of an intrigue story**:
  1. Brief, and relevant to the pitch.
  2. You are at the center of it.
  3. Risk, danger, uncertainty present.
  4. Time pressure — a ticking clock with real consequences.
  5. Tension — you're actively blocked by some force.
  6. Serious consequences if it fails.
- **The "man in the jungle" narrative formula** (borrowed from screenwriting): put a man in the jungle → have beasts attack him → will he get to safety? The jungle = the difficult situation; the beasts = the conflict/tension; safety = resolution. Critically: **stop the story just short of resolution** and pivot back into your pitch. The incompleteness is what sustains intrigue — resolving it kills the tension that's holding attention.
- **Why it beats the analyst frame**: narrative and analytical processing cannot run in the target's brain at the same time. A well-placed personal story about real stakes forces the brain out of "detective/paradigmatic mode" (fact-checking, calculating) and into "narrative mode" (following a human actor through real-world obstacles), which is processed by the croc brain as felt truth rather than analyzed fact.
- **Illustrative principle — the Jaws analogy**: Spielberg never shows the shark early; the *unseen* threat creates the suspense that holds the audience. If the shark had a visible GPS tracker, there'd be no tension and no movie. Apply the same logic to your own narrative: don't over-resolve it early.
- **A story should reveal character, not information**: audiences don't remember what happened to you — they remember how you acted under pressure. A flat factual account ("we had a problem, then we solved it") has none of this power; the story must show a person, under real threat, actively fighting for an outcome.

## 7. The STRONG Pitch Structure

Klaff's pitch format compresses everything into roughly 20 minutes, split into four timed phases. The reasoning: human attention for a stranger's pitch degrades hard after about 20 minutes, and the brain begins actively forgetting material already delivered once that ceiling is hit.

- **Opening move — the time-constraint pattern**: state up front that the pitch will take about 20 minutes, signaling competence and putting the croc brain at ease (it now knows it isn't trapped indefinitely).
- **Phase 1 — Introduce yourself and the big idea (~5 min)**:
  - Background: state only your single best track-record win, not a full biography. Research shows impressions are formed from the *average* of information given, not the sum — piling on more credentials (one great thing + one good thing + two mediocre things) actively drags the impression down. State one great thing and stop.
  - **"Why now?" frame / three-market-forces pattern (trendcasting)**: frame the idea against three converging forces — economic, social, technological — to show the opportunity is new and time-sensitive, not a stale leftover deal nobody else wanted. Targets structurally distrust "old" deals that have been sitting around.
  - Movement matters: the brain is wired to notice motion and largely blind to static comparisons (**change blindness** — shown two before/after images, most people miss even large differences unless attention is explicitly drawn to the change itself). So don't present "old way vs. new way" as two static states — narrate the movement from one to the other.
  - **Idea introduction pattern** (1-minute template): *"For [target customers] who are dissatisfied with [current market option]. My idea is a [new category] that provides [key solution]. Unlike [competing option], my idea is [distinguishing feature]."*
- **Phase 2 — Explain the budget and secret sauce (~10 min)**:
  - Simplicity for its own sake is a trap — it can read as naive. The real skill is tuning the message to the *target's* theory of mind, not minimizing information generically.
  - Budgets: present them as evidence of disciplined budgeting skill (a hard, respected executive skill), not optimistic revenue projections (which every investor discounts on sight as inflated "hockey stick" charts).
  - Competition: address directly — how easy is it for new entrants to compete, and how easy is it for customers to switch away from you. Never skip this; ignoring it reads as naive.
  - "Secret sauce": name your specific, defensible competitive advantage briefly — this is what prevents you from reading as a flash-in-the-pan idea.
- **Phase 3 — Offer the deal (~2 min)**: state plainly what the buyer will receive, when, and how, plus their role/responsibilities if any. High-level only — no drill-down. Remember: the most important thing you're delivering, always, is you.
- **Phase 4 — Frame stacking for a hot cognition (~3 min)**: covered fully in Section 9.
- **Attention discipline throughout**: Klaff repeatedly invokes the Jerry Seinfeld comparison — even one of the best-known performers alive gets roughly three minutes of default audience goodwill before he has to actually earn attention on merit, and it takes him a month of work to build three minutes of quality material. If a world-class comedian can't coast past three minutes, no pitch coasts past twenty.

## 8. The Science of Attention: Novelty, Tension, and Push/Pull

Attention itself is modeled as a specific neurochemical cocktail, not a vague state of interest — and the pitch must actively manufacture it.

- **The two ingredients**:
  - **Dopamine** — the chemical of desire/anticipation of reward. Triggered by *novelty*, not by the reward itself (novel, unexpected gains spike dopamine; expected rewards that fail to arrive crash it, producing stress). Sources of novelty: short demos, new ideas, strong metaphors, unusual visuals/shapes/movement.
  - **Norepinephrine** — the chemical of tension/alertness. Triggered by *taking something away* or introducing real consequences (something to be gained or lost). Without stakes, there's no reason to stay alert.
  - Attention = desire (dopamine) + tension (norepinephrine) held in balance. Too much dopamine with no tension reads as an unchallenged "lovefest" that gets boring; too much tension with no reward reads as threat.
- **This cocktail only holds for a few minutes at a time and must be continuously refreshed** — hence the pitch is built as a *series of tension loops* (push, then pull, then resolve, repeat), not one long argument.
- **Push/pull patterns** (increasing intensity, to be used whenever you sense attention dropping):
  - Low intensity: *Push* — "There's a real possibility we're not right for each other." *Pull* — "But if this did work out, our forces could combine to become something great."
  - Medium intensity: *Push* — invoke a hypothetical VC who only cares about the people, not the idea, implying a values mismatch is possible. *Pull* — "But obviously you're not like that — you value people over just numbers."
  - High intensity: *Push* — "This doesn't seem like a good fit — let's just agree to reconnect on the next one," accompanied by visibly packing up to leave. This is the most extreme version and should only be used when you're genuinely willing to follow through on walking away.
- **Why conflict/tension is necessary, not optional**: a pitch with no pushback, all agreement and smiles, produces polite listening but zero emotional investment — the target has no reason to remember or act on it. Interesting human connection requires some friction to resolve.
- **The due-diligence-analyst story** illustrates the failure mode directly: an all-dopamine, no-tension pitch (glossy materials, no stakes) to 25 analysts produced total silence — until Klaff physically began reclaiming the materials mid-pitch (introducing loss/tension), at which point questions started flowing.

## 9. Frame Stacking and Hot Cognitions

The book's model of decision-making: most consequential decisions (personal and financial) are made via fast, felt, pre-conscious "hot cognition," and only rationalized with facts afterward — not the reverse. Brain-scan research is cited showing decisions can be predicted from neural activity several seconds before the subject consciously believes they've decided.

- **Hot cognition** = an instant, felt "wanting" or aversion, formed before or without full understanding of the details (developed in the older brain structures — brainstem, midbrain). **Cold cognition** = the slow, deliberate, fact-based evaluation process of the neocortex.
- **Hot and cold cognition cannot run simultaneously** — this is the load-bearing neuroscience claim behind the entire chapter. If you let a target go cold (into detailed analysis) near the close, you lose the emotional "wanting" that actually drives yes/no decisions.
- **The four-frame hot cognition stack** — deployed near the end of the pitch, in this order, to prevent the target from settling into cold analytical evaluation before you're ready for it:
  1. **Intrigue frame** — dangle something the target wants but can't get yet (e.g., a compelling, not-yet-introduced partner or character; a story cut off before resolution).
  2. **Prize frame** — make the target feel they must qualify themselves and compete for your attention, not the reverse.
  3. **Time frame** — introduce a real, reasonable deadline (not manufactured pressure) so the target feels urgency without feeling coerced ("I can give you until Friday, and I'm genuinely fine with a no").
  4. **Moral authority frame** — appeal to a values-based, non-transactional standard the target must live up to (e.g., "we do clean deals, no games — can you play by the rules?").
- **Why this order/combination works**: each frame independently produces a hot cognition; stacking four in quick succession compounds the effect faster than the target's neocortex can intervene and start rationally evaluating. Klaff describes personally being "stacked" by a Wall Street trader this way and only recognizing it in hindsight — evidence the technique works even on a trained practitioner.
- **The moral authority frame in depth** — argued to be one of the most powerful frames available because it borrows the same psychological wiring that makes us defer automatically to doctors (we're hardwired to obey people who hold power over life/death or high expertise). Illustrated by the Mother Teresa case: her frame (material wealth is worthless, life and death aren't the ultimate concern, help the downtrodden) overrode the doctor frame of an entire hospital of physicians, because she simply never reacted to their usual status signals — and she left having gotten dozens of doctors to pledge free clinical work in Tijuana.
- **Practical guidance on hot vs. cold framing choice**: default instinct is to trust the "smarter" neocortex with your message, but that's backwards — the croc brain is the actual first (and often deciding) recipient. Chasing a cold, rational "like" from the neocortex is a weaker outcome than generating a hot "want" in the croc brain.

## 10. Eradicating Neediness

Neediness (validation-seeking behavior) is named as the single most common deal-killer among experienced dealmakers, and is treated as a distinct, correctable failure mode rather than a personality trait.

- **What causes neediness, mechanically**:
  1. Wanting something only the target can give (money, a job, a signature) sets the stage.
  2. Needing the target's cooperation and not getting it (distraction, interruptions, being cut short) creates frustration.
  3. Believing the target's "yes" is what will make you feel okay traps you — the stronger this belief, the more neediness leaks out, and the less likely the target is to comply. This is a self-reinforcing downward spiral.
  4. The target's visible disinterest or withdrawal triggers a natural fear response, and involuntary neediness signals follow.
- **Why it's fatal**: neediness reads to the other party's croc brain as a threat signal ("this person might do something desperate"), triggering an avoidance/defensive reaction rather than sympathy. It also collapses frame control, status, and hot-cognition stacking simultaneously — it undoes everything else in the method at once.
- **The three-step formula to eliminate it**:
  1. Want nothing (in the moment of the pitch itself — desire is fine before/after, not while pitching).
  2. Focus only on what you do well.
  3. Announce your intention to leave, and mean it.
- **The Tao of Steve framework** (borrowed from the film, applied as a literal operating philosophy): eliminate your desires (let things come to you rather than chasing); be excellent at one specific thing in front of others; withdraw at the exact moment people expect you to keep pursuing them. People chase what pulls away — this is prizing applied to your entire demeanor, not just a scripted line.
- **The classic recovery story**: Klaff's own account of four failed VC pitches down to his last few hundred dollars, and how a mentor diagnosed his failure purely as neediness leaking through in his closing lines ("Do you still think it's a good deal?" / "We can sign right away if you want"). The fix in the fourth and final pitch was purely behavioral: state three prize-frame points, then go completely silent and let the room react — no chasing, no filler. That pitch closed with $2.1M wired within days.
- **Practical marker to self-check for neediness**: notice the post-pitch "awkward two minutes" after you finish talking — this is described as the single easiest moment to leak neediness and undo 20 minutes of good work, because the unspoken pressure ("I need something from you") peaks right there.

## 11. Applying the Method: Progression and Shared Vocabulary

The book closes with an explicit, staged learning path (framed as a skill requiring practice with a partner, not solo reading) and a working vocabulary meant to let two practitioners narrate frame dynamics to each other in real time.

- **Seven-step progression for learning frame control**:
  1. Learn to *recognize* beta traps in daily life without yet acting on them — just build pattern recognition.
  2. Begin actually stepping around beta traps in low-stakes situations, ideally with a practice partner.
  3. Identify and label the frames coming at you (power, time, analyst) using the book's specific vocabulary.
  4. Start initiating small, safe frame collisions — low-risk targets only, not your boss or CEO on day one.
  5. Practice delivering denial/defiance with a light touch — if you're triggering defensiveness instead of playful engagement, you're coming on too hard; reset and try a different context.
  6. Never force it — frame control that isn't fun signals you're doing it wrong; lighten up rather than push harder.
  7. Once you have basic competency, apprentice with others who are better at it than you — mastery compounds faster with partners than solo.
- **Humor as a structural requirement, not decoration**: repeatedly emphasized as the difference between a playful, status-elevating frame disruption and one that reads as rude or aggressive and gets you thrown out of the meeting. The humor signals "I have enough options that I can afford to play," which is itself a status/prize signal.
- **Shared vocabulary to internalize and use with a practice partner**: frame control, power-busting frame, frame collisions, prizing, beta traps, seizing status, local star power, push/pull, alpha, hot cognition, crocodile brain, neocortex. The stated purpose of naming these explicitly is speed — being able to say "they're beta-trapping us, time-frame them and withdraw" in the moment, instead of losing the opportunity while explaining the concept from scratch.
- **Long-run payoff claimed for mastering the method**: frame discipline lets you selectively ignore what doesn't serve your goals, which compounds into faster decision-making and more focus over time — a secondary benefit beyond winning any single pitch.
