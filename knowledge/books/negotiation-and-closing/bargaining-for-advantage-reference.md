# Bargaining for Advantage — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing negotiation/closing/sales processes, or advising on deal strategy.*
*Book: "Bargaining for Advantage: Negotiation Strategies for Reasonable People" — G. Richard Shell*

---

## 1. The Core Approach: Information-Based Bargaining

Shell's whole system is built on one premise: information is the primary currency of negotiation, not aggression or charm. Information-Based Bargaining rests on three moves repeated throughout every deal: prepare thoroughly before you start, listen more than you talk so you learn what the other side actually wants, and read the signals the other party sends through conduct (not just words) once bargaining is under way.

The book rejects one-size-fits-all formulas like "always open high" or "win-win." Every negotiation is a mix of a unique person, a unique situation, and a unique set of stakes, so the right move depends on diagnosis, not a script. Two structural claims anchor the rest of the book:

- Six Foundations determine your leverage and options before you ever say a word: your personal bargaining style, your goals and expectations, the authoritative standards and norms available to you, your relationships, the other party's interests, and leverage itself.
- Negotiation follows a universal four-step process once talks begin: preparing your strategy, exchanging information, opening and making concessions, and closing and gaining commitment. Anthropologically this shows up across cultures (Shell contrasts a corporate merger negotiation with a Tanzanian Arusha land dispute settled the same way: ritual opening, information exchange, concession-trading, and a closing ceremony that cements commitment).

Four effectiveness habits cut across every foundation and every stage, and Shell argues they are learnable regardless of personality: a willingness to prepare, high expectations, the patience to listen, and a commitment to personal integrity. Cooperative people tend to be strong on integrity and listening but weak on expectations; competitive people tend to be strong on expectations but weak on listening. Effectiveness means shoring up your weak habit, not becoming a different personality.

## 2. The First Foundation: Your Bargaining Style

Style is the necessary starting point because you cannot execute a strategy that fights your own instincts under pressure. Shell frames five basic conflict-handling styles, in descending order of aggressiveness, using a thought experiment (a stranger offers $1,000 to the first two people at a table who can get the person across from them to walk around and stand behind their chair):

- Competitor: plays to win, opens with ambitious demands, uses threats and ultimatums, controls the split once it wins.
- Problem solver: looks past the obvious split to find a move where both sides can win simultaneously (the hardest style to execute, most valuable in complex, high-trust negotiations).
- Compromiser: fair-minded, defaults to fast 50/50 splits to preserve the relationship and avoid conflict.
- Accommodator: solves the other person's problem first, trusting reciprocity will follow.
- Avoider: dislikes conflict enough to opt out of the interaction rather than engage.

Underneath these five sit two more basic orientations, cooperative and competitive. Research Shell cites (a study of American lawyers, a nine-year field study of English labor negotiators by Rackham and Carlisle) both found cooperative negotiators are more common and rated more "effective" by peers than competitive ones — contrary to the popular image of the table-pounding dealmaker. Skilled negotiators in the Rackham/Carlisle data used far fewer "irritators" (self-serving remarks, gratuitous insults, direct attacks — 2.3/hour vs. 10.8/hour for average negotiators) and almost never triggered "defend/attack spirals" (1.9% of comments vs. 6.3%).

A key mismatch risk: cooperative and competitive people misread each other. Cooperative negotiators assume good faith and disclose freely; competitive negotiators interpret that openness as weakness to exploit, then stonewall when it's their turn to reciprocate — confirming the cooperative side's suspicion that "hardball" is everywhere. The fix isn't to convert the other side to your style; it's to accurately diagnose which style you're facing early and calibrate.

Practical implication: don't try to become someone you're not. Identify your natural style, understand its blind spot, and consciously compensate for that one blind spot (detailed compensating tools are in Section 13).

## 3. The Second Foundation: Goals and Expectations

Research finding stated bluntly: people who expect more, get more — and the effect is not marginal. In a classic Siegel and Fouraker experiment, negotiators told they needed to hit a $6.10 bonus target averaged $6.25 in profit; negotiators told to hit $2.10 averaged only $3.35, despite identical bottom lines (no losses allowed either way).

Shell draws a hard distinction between a goal and a bottom line:

- A bottom line (reservation price) is the minimum you'll accept — it defines a positive or negative bargaining zone once compared against the other side's bottom line.
- A goal is your highest legitimate, justifiable expectation — not a wish, an expectation you'd feel real disappointment about missing.

The core mechanism: whichever reference point you focus on becomes your psychological anchor. Negotiators who fixate on their bottom line relax the moment an offer clears it — and an alert counterpart senses that relaxation and stops conceding. Negotiators who fixate on their goal keep pushing past that early relief point. Practical rule: know your bottom line so you don't get exploited, but orient your attention and energy toward your goal, and let the bottom line take care of itself later in the process (Akio Morita's approach in the Sony/Bulova story — he had goals firmly set but worked out his real walk-away only as real information arrived).

Five-step goal-setting process:

- Think carefully about what you actually want — remember price/money is usually a means, not the end goal itself (CBS's William Paley made his fortune by treating price as secondary and giving away radio programming for ad access).
- Set an optimistic but justifiable target — high enough to be a real stretch, but backed by some standard or argument you could defend "with a straight face."
- Be specific — vague goals ("negotiate a fair salary," "do the best I can") produce vague, weak effort; specific goals ("10% raise over my last salary") mobilize psychological "striving" mechanisms and focus your search for supporting standards.
- Commit to the goal — write it down, visualize achieving it, and tell someone else about it. Public or social commitment measurably increases follow-through (research on goal-writing, Ben Franklin's youthful written "plan for regulating his future conduct," door-to-door sales scripts that get customers to fill out their own order forms).
- Carry the goal with you into the negotiation itself — it's easy to get swept off target mid-negotiation (Barry Diller's $3.3M Poseidon Adventure overbid is Shell's cautionary tale of "escalation of commitment" — getting caught up in the fight and forgetting the original goal was profit, not winning the auction).

Watch for the "winner's curse": in competitive bidding situations, the winning bidder has, almost by definition, been the person most susceptible to escalating past their own goal.

## 4. The Third Foundation: Authoritative Standards and Norms

People have a deep psychological need to appear consistent and reasonable to themselves and others — Cialdini's "consistency principle." Negotiators who can frame their position in terms of a standard, norm, or precedent the other side already accepts get what Shell calls normative leverage: their position looks legitimate rather than self-serving.

Core rule for using standards: anticipate the other side's preferred standards and frame your proposal inside them, rather than asserting only your own criteria. If your case can't fit their standard, argue for a special exception grounded in the specific facts, and attack the standard itself only as a last resort (illustrated with a hospital nurse-executive framing a budget request around the hospital's own stated "quality patient care" priority rather than her department's needs).

Two categories of market standards:

- Preemptive standards act almost like fixed formulas that eliminate negotiation entirely (the Ifugao "natural rate of increase" for borrowed pigs; the 6% U.S. real-estate commission; the 15% literary-agent fee). Deviating from an institutionalized standard risks looking greedy or being punished socially — "Pigs get fat, but hogs get eaten."
- Range-finding standards bracket a bargaining zone without dictating the outcome (e.g., "fair market value," comparable sales, industry benchmarks). These reward whichever side comes better prepared with supporting data.

Positioning themes are crisp, memorable framings of what the negotiation is "about" — they organize your own arguments and help the other side understand what you need (the 1997 Teamsters/UPS strike theme "Part-time America won't work" beat UPS's "we must stay competitive" theme and delivered a wage increase and 10,000 part-time-to-full-time conversions).

Consistency traps are a manipulative use of the same principle: get someone to agree to an innocuous-sounding standard before revealing why it matters, then hold them to the standard's logical consequence (classic telemarketing script: "Would you like to save money?" ... "Then switch to our service"). Defense: slow down when someone asks leading questions before revealing their purpose; if you must agree to a standard, phrase your agreement broadly and vaguely to preserve room to maneuver later.

Deference to authority is a second, related lever: people obey authoritative-looking packaging (dense contracts, "company policy," expert jargon) even when it's unjustified (Cialdini's "Candid Camera" DELAWARE CLOSED sign story). This cuts both ways — the Air Florida cockpit transcript (copilot deferring to the captain's mistaken judgment before a fatal crash) shows how deference to authority can suppress a subordinate's own correct information, a risk in any hierarchical negotiation team.

Third-party audiences let you invoke normative leverage even when the immediate counterpart won't budge: find someone your counterpart is answerable to who accepts your standard, and negotiate in that person's presence (Gandhi's first-class train ticket in South Africa — he couldn't out-argue the conductor directly, but an English fellow passenger who accepted "well-dressed, well-behaved people can travel first-class" as the operative standard shifted the outcome).

## 5. The Fourth Foundation: Relationships

Trust is the foundation that makes deals possible to close and durable once made. Shell grounds relationship-building in the norm of reciprocity: a deep, cross-cultural obligation to repay what others have done for us, tracked both over long horizons (Ethiopia's 1985 aid to Mexico repaying 1935 Ethiopian-war aid) and turn-by-turn within a single negotiation ("I told you a bit about my needs, now it's your turn").

The three-part reciprocity code of conduct: be trustworthy and reliable yourself; be fair to those who are fair to you; and when someone treats you unfairly, tell them — unaddressed unfairness breeds exploitation and eventual relationship collapse.

The "ultimatum game" (a stranger splits $100 with you by one-shot offer; reject it and both get nothing) is Shell's evidence that fairness is not just nice-to-have — most people will reject offers below roughly 20-30% of the total purely to punish unfairness, even at a cost to themselves. Behavior in an early round also sets the norm for future rounds: reward stinginess and you'll get more stinginess; push back on an unfair split and future offers improve.

Personal friendships and working relationships behave differently at the table, and confusing them is a common error:

- Close personal relationships (friends, couples) trigger equal-split norms almost automatically. Research on dating couples found they made bigger concessions, argued less, and disclosed more truthfully than strangers negotiating the same problem — but they left far more value on the table because they defaulted to simple 50/50 splits instead of problem-solving for hidden trade-offs.
- Working relationships sit between friendship and stranger-to-stranger dealing: enough trust for reciprocity to operate, but enough professional distance to sustain real conflict over stakes without threatening the relationship (Shell's model example is J.P. Morgan and Andrew Carnegie).
- When stakes get very high, close personal relationships become risky bargaining partners — some people will "cash in" a friendship at a high enough price. Shell's advice: at high stakes, bring in professional advisers or a mutually trusted mediator rather than negotiating a big-money split directly with someone you love.

Building trust deliberately, three mechanisms:

- Similarity principle: people trust people who seem familiar — shared group membership, background, communication style (Steve Ross researching a counterpart's horse-racing hobby before a negotiation; Armand Hammer bidding on sheepskin parchment for a Libyan oil concession).
- Gifts and favors as costly signals of intent to invest in the relationship (Jovanovich's engraved watch to Dick Smith; J.P. Morgan handing Carnegie a second $10,000 check rather than pocketing an accounting error in Morgan's favor).
- Relationship networks: shared acquaintances who can vouch for you lower the trust threshold (Japanese meishi business-card exchange rituals; Chinese guanxi; Shell's own point that Western "professional networking" and alumni lists function the same way, just without the ceremony).

Relationship traps to watch for: trusting too quickly (test small commitments before large ones); reciprocity traps (accepting a trivial "gift" that's designed to manufacture obligation — the airport-flower-donation con); and negotiating high-stakes splits with friends or family without a neutral process, which risks either an exploitative outcome or the relationship itself.

## 6. The Fifth Foundation: The Other Party's Interests

The single most under-used lever in negotiation, according to the Rackham/Carlisle planning-session research: skilled negotiators spent roughly 40% of their planning time exploring shared or complementary interests versus about 10% for average negotiators (both groups spent most remaining time on their own goals and conflict areas). Skilled negotiators also generated roughly twice as many possible settlement options.

Three reasons this is hard, per Shell:

- Partisan perception: we see the world through our own interests and assume the other side's needs conflict with ours by default.
- Competitive framing: negotiation naturally draws attention to divergent interests, not shared ones (a hospital's dispute with a foreign drug firm over a consent form nearly collapsed until someone asked "why would this specific physician say no?" and found several fixable, non-financial causes).
- Process dynamics: even attentive parties miss shared priorities almost half the time in controlled studies (Leigh Thompson's review of 32 studies, 5,000+ participants) because both sides bluff about their real priorities during exchange.

Four-step process for exploiting this Foundation:

- Identify the actual decision maker — not the HR contact or general intermediary, but the person with real authority and a personal stake in the outcome (illustrated with MBA students negotiating salary/signing terms directly with a founder rather than a recruiting coordinator).
- Look for common ground: role-reversal is Shell's recommended technique — literally sit in the other party's chair (with a colleague playing you) and ask "how would it serve my interests to give this person what they want?" Consumer example: companies often value customer satisfaction more than a marginal price point, which is why simply asking for a discount works far more often than people expect ("Ask and It Shall Be Discounted").
- Identify why the other side might say no — often for reasons that have nothing to do with the obvious financial conflict (First Union's CEO Ed Crutchfield discovered CoreStates' CEO was blocking a $16B bank merger over concern for the target region's charitable commitments, not price — a $100M independent community foundation, 0.5% of deal value, unlocked the whole transaction).
- Search for low-cost options that solve their problem while advancing your goal (Kelly Sarber won a premium-priced Oceanside garbage-hauling contract by proposing her trucks return with clean sand for the city's eroding beaches — solving a problem the city hadn't put out to bid).

## 7. The Sixth Foundation: Leverage

Leverage is the power to reach agreement on your own terms, not merely the power to reach agreement at all. Shell's single-sentence test: at any given moment, ask who has the most to lose if no deal happens. The party with the most to lose has the least leverage; the party with the least to lose has the most.

Three types of leverage, illustrated through a 1977 hostage crisis (the Hanafi Muslim takeover of three Washington, D.C. buildings) that Shell uses as an extended case study in how leverage shifts moment to moment:

- Positive leverage: your ability to supply something the other party wants or needs. ("Every time the other party says 'I want' in a negotiation, you should hear the pleasant sound of a weight dropping on your side of the scales" — Bob Woolf.) Authorities built positive leverage with the hostage-taker by delivering small, deliverable wins (halting a film screening, returning a $750 fine) before tackling his real underlying need — recognition as a Muslim leader.
- Negative leverage: your credible power to make the other side worse off. Threats only work if they're credible — the threatener must appear willing and able to carry them out, and must not be equally harmed by executing the threat (police could not use force without endangering hostages, which crippled their negative leverage early on; Donald Trump's two competing architectural models presented to Tiffany & Co.'s Walter Hoving is a "civilized" commercial version of the same tactic).
- Normative leverage: leverage drawn from the consistency principle (Chapter on standards) — getting someone committed to a value or standard whose logical implication favors you (the Pakistani ambassador invoking Koranic compassion to move Khaalis toward releasing hostages).

Three common misconceptions Shell explicitly debunks:

- Leverage is not the same as objective power or wealth. A five-year-old refusing broccoli, or an elderly widow (Vera Coking) refusing to sell her house to Donald Trump for a casino expansion, can hold real leverage against a vastly more powerful counterpart because they control something the other party needs and have little to lose by saying no.
- Leverage is not fixed — it is dynamic and changes as a negotiation unfolds (a job candidate's peak leverage moment is the "golden moment" after an offer is extended but before it's accepted).
- Leverage is not simply a function of the objective facts — it is a function of the other party's perception of the facts (Joshua's trumpets and torches at Jericho; police prominently displaying but never using weapons around the Hanafi Muslim buildings).

Coalitions multiply leverage in three ways: group dynamics favor whoever assembles majority support first (jury deliberation research shows the first position to reach a majority tends to become the unanimous verdict); "social proof" causes ambiguous-situation observers to follow the crowd; and coalitions can directly improve your BATNA or worsen the other side's alternatives (North Dakota cattle ranchers forming their own meatpacking cooperative to escape dependence on a few large slaughterhouses).

Leverage inverts inside families, firms, and other ongoing-relationship contexts: displaying a good BATNA or announcing you don't need the other side (which helps in market Transactions) reads as a threat and damages trust internally. Instead, passionate, uncharacteristic intensity from someone who is usually calm signals genuine priority and moves people (General George Marshall's uncharacteristic three-minute outburst that reoriented Roosevelt's WWII mobilization posture).

## 8. The Situational Matrix: Diagnosing What Kind of Negotiation You're In

Before choosing tactics, diagnose the situation along two axes: how much the parties' stakes conflict, and how much the parties' future relationship matters. This produces four quadrants, each calling for a different default strategy:

- Quadrant I — Balanced Concerns (high stakes conflict AND high relationship importance): business partnerships, joint ventures, mergers where existing management stays on, family businesses, long-term supplier relationships. Best strategies: problem solving or compromise. Example: Ben Franklin's teenage "meal deal" with his brother James — Franklin kept his vegetarian principles, split the boarding-fee savings, and both sides came out ahead rather than settling for an unhappy compromise.
- Quadrant II — Relationships (low stakes conflict, high relationship importance): marriage, friendship, cohesive work teams. Best strategies: accommodation, problem solving, or compromise. Example: the Institute for Advanced Study's recruitment of Einstein — Flexner tripled Einstein's own modest salary request because the relationship (landing a "crown jewel" scholar) mattered far more than the specific number.
- Quadrant III — Transactions (high stakes conflict, low relationship importance): divorce, one-off house sales, most market transactions between strangers. Best strategies: competition, problem solving, or compromise. Example: J.P. Morgan's acquisition of Rockefeller's Mesabi ore fields — neither side would name a price first, and the deal only closed once both sides agreed on a mutually trusted go-between (Henry Clay Frick) to set the number.
- Quadrant IV — Tacit Coordination (low stakes conflict, low relationship importance): a highway intersection, airplane boarding, situations that barely require "negotiation" at all. Best strategies: avoidance, accommodation, or compromise — solve the coordination problem and move on.

Compromise appears as a usable strategy in all four quadrants but is rarely the best choice anywhere — Shell frames it as a fallback for when time is short, not a default philosophy, because reaching for a 50/50 split before exploring trade-offs leaves value on the table (the video-store-sale example throughout Chapter 9 shows how a party that moves straight to an even compromise gets no credit and no reciprocity for the concession).

Perception matters as much as the "objective" situation: the two sides may see the same event differently (a department-store return clerk may see a pure Transaction while the customer sees an ongoing Relationship worth invoking) — and professional agents (union negotiators, brokers) often perceive more Balanced-Concerns nuance than the constituents they represent, who may see a pure Transaction and pressure their agent toward hardball theatrics.

## 9. Step 1 in Practice: Preparing Your Strategy

Preparation is the single most consistently validated effectiveness factor in the negotiation research Shell cites — a computer-mediated negotiation experiment he ran found that a structured 30-40 minute preparation process improved outcomes for both sides regardless of whether the actual bargaining happened face-to-face or electronically; the delivery channel barely mattered, but the preparation process did.

Three-part preparation sequence:

- Assess the situation using the Situational Matrix (Section 8) to identify which quadrant you're in and which strategy family fits.
- Match the situation to your own style — a naturally cooperative person will struggle in a pure Transaction against a competitive counterpart; a naturally competitive person will damage a Relationship-quadrant negotiation with unnecessary hardball. If the mismatch is severe, get help (an agent, adviser, or teammate whose style fits the situation better than yours does).
- Examine the situation from the other party's point of view — situations are perceptual, not objective, and the two sides may be operating from different quadrant assumptions; part of the early information-exchange stage is testing and, if needed, correcting that mismatch.

The chapter closes by pointing toward Appendix B's Information-Based Bargaining Plan (Section 14) as the concrete tool for consolidating all Six Foundations plus the situational analysis into a single pre-negotiation document, along with a list of specific questions to ask early in the discussion phase to test your assumptions against reality.

## 10. Step 2 in Practice: Exchanging Information

Information exchange is the ritualized opening phase before explicit bargaining starts (Shell's Arusha "talking to the mountain" example: extravagant opening demands nobody takes literally, whose real function is to set the agenda and test the waters). It serves three distinct purposes:

- Establishing rapport. Even a minimal, seemingly automatic exchange (the McDonald's counter greeting) opens a communication channel and reduces ambiguity; genuine, non-manipulative rapport-building (finding real shared background, as Steve Ross did researching a counterpart's horse-racing hobby) triggers Cialdini's "liking rule" — we say yes more readily to people we know and like. An e-mail negotiation experiment (Northwestern/Stanford MBAs) found a "strictly business" group deadlocked 30% of the time versus 6% for a group required to exchange personal background first. Rapport-building fails when it's transparently manipulative (extracting concessions on the basis of forced rapport) or when a careless remark poisons the atmosphere (Intel's "negotiating with the Japanese is like negotiating with the Devil" quote leaking to the press before sensitive talks).
- Determining interests, issues, and perceptions. The core discipline here is "probe first, disclose later" — skilled negotiators in the Rackham/Carlisle data spent 38.5% of their time asking questions, testing understanding, and summarizing, versus 17.9% for average negotiators, and asked roughly twice as many clarifying questions. Sony's Akio Morita and MCA's Sidney Sheinberg (the Betamax lawsuit story) illustrate the failure mode on both sides: Sheinberg ambushed Morita with a legal ultimatum during what Morita believed was a relationship-building dinner, and Morita failed to recognize that his Japanese "friends don't sue" framework didn't apply to an American counterpart operating under U.S. litigation norms. Bluffing about priorities is common and costly — one study found 20% of negotiators, including experienced professionals, agreed to options neither side actually wanted because of bluffs that backfired.
- Signaling expectations and leverage. How you signal depends on whether your actual hand is strong or weak, and on how flexible you intend to be:
  - Weak hand, firm stance: bluff (act strong when you're not) — high-risk; experienced counterparts often see through the classic "let me get back to you" stall (Victor Kiam's Aroma Disk Player buyback from Charles of the Ritz is Shell's example of a bluff that worked).
  - Weak hand, flexible stance: emphasize uncertainty about the future, acknowledge the other side's power directly, or appeal to their sympathy ("What would it take for you to say yes?").
  - Strong hand, firm stance: make confident demands and credible threats, or display your alternatives and let the other side decide — but do this cleanly and directly (Sheinberg's mistake was hedging an otherwise legitimate threat inside an informal dinner setting).
  - Strong hand, flexible stance: show you're investing in the relationship and be generous about it, matter-of-factly rather than as a display of magnanimity (J.P. Morgan handing Carnegie the extra $10,000 check).
  - Special case: cooperative people signaling strength to a competitive counterpart who won't listen sometimes need to open with visible assertiveness to earn attention and respect before the counterpart will engage constructively (Anthony Robbins's stubborn-supplier story; Trump's observation that competitive people respond to matched passion).

## 11. Step 3 in Practice: Opening and Making Concessions

**Should you open first?** No universal rule. Naming a price first ("anchor and adjustment" effect) can pull the final number toward your figure, but only works if you have solid information about market value — Raymond Chandler and Beatles manager Brian Epstein both opened first without knowing industry norms and left large amounts of money on the table because the other side simply accepted their unknowingly-low opening figure. When neither side has confident information and the relationship matters, get a neutral appraiser instead (Rockefeller and Morgan's Mesabi ore fields deal, settled through go-between Henry Clay Frick).

**Optimistic vs. reasonable opening** depends entirely on the Situational Matrix quadrant:

- Transactions: open optimistically — the highest or lowest figure you can defend "with a straight face." This works through two mechanisms: the contrast principle (a large final concession off a high anchor feels more satisfying than a small concession off a modest one) and reciprocity (rejecting an aggressive opening and receiving a moderated one triggers a felt obligation to respond in kind). Exceptions: don't open optimistically if you visibly lack leverage, if the other side has signaled it won't haggle at all (roughly 15% of people hate bargaining and price to sell, per Shell's cited research), or as you move toward Balanced Concerns territory where an aggressive opening reads as an insult to the relationship.
- Balanced Concerns / Relationships: open fairly or generously — Wayne Huizenga's acquisition strategy (open within 5-10% of his real ceiling, negotiate mainly on non-price terms) reflects respect for the seller's investment in the business and the desire to retain them post-acquisition.

**Concession strategy** by quadrant:

- Transactions: firmness, concede slowly in diminishing amounts, converging toward (not below) your expectation level rather than your bottom line. Experimental comparison of three concession patterns (start high/never move; start moderate/never move; start high/concede gradually) found the gradual-concession approach produced both more closed deals and higher satisfaction on both sides, even when the conceding party ended up paying more.
- Balanced Concerns: make big moves on your minor issues and small moves on your major ones ("issue trading" or integrative bargaining), package multiple issues together, and use the conditional formula "IF you give us X, THEN we can consider Y" so no concession is ever made without a reciprocal link.
- Relationships: accommodate and give the other party what they want generously.
- Tacit Coordination: accommodate or do whatever solves the immediate problem; don't overthink tactics.

Never give away a concession casually — "concession devaluation" (also called reactive devaluation) means the other side discounts the value of anything conceded too easily, and may even lower their own valuation of the whole deal as a result ("I guess we valued this wrong").

**Good guy/bad guy** is the signature Transaction-quadrant ploy: the bad guy opens outrageously or attacks your proposal, creating a sense of threatened loss; the good guy then "champions" a concession from the bad guy, earning your trust and reciprocal concessions through contrast and the liking rule simultaneously. Counter: name the tactic openly and demand to know who actually holds decision authority.

## 12. Step 4 in Practice: Closing and Gaining Commitment

Two psychological levers dominate the closing stage — Shell walks through both using the 1988 RJR Nabisco leveraged buyout auction (Kravis/KKR vs. Ross Johnson's management-led bid, chronicled in Barbarians at the Gate) as the extended case:

**The scarcity effect** — people want things more when the supply appears to be running out. Four ways negotiators manufacture or exploit it:

- Real or bluffed competition ("we have another buyer").
- Deadlines, most credible when tied to an external event the parties don't control (a regulatory filing date, a competing bid's own expiration).
- "Exploding" terms that disappear after a stated time (signing bonuses that vanish if you don't decide immediately).
- Walkouts — sometimes genuine, often theater (Wayne Huizenga's habitual, calculated walkouts during the Blockbuster/Viacom sale) — whose real message depends entirely on the underlying leverage: the party with more to lose either chases the walker-out, or the walker-out returns on their own once they've made the point.

**Overcommitment / loss aversion** — the more time and resources already invested in reaching a deal, the harder it becomes to walk away, even when walking away is now the better choice (the amusement-park-line thought experiment: waiting 45 minutes already sunk makes people more likely to wait an additional 45 than people who've only waited 3). Manipulative counterparts exploit this by introducing late "essential" demands ("we've come so far, don't let this be wasted") or by "nibbling" — small, additional asks right before close that feel too trivial to fight over but that professional negotiators use to add 3-5% in value over time. Antidote: track whether the other side is as invested as you are, and require a reciprocal concession for any late request.

**Softer, relationship-preserving closing tactics:**

- Splitting the difference — fast, simple, and feels self-evidently fair, but only sound if your opening was itself fair (if you opened reasonably while the other side opened aggressively, splitting rewards their aggression) and risky to reach for too early in a Balanced Concerns negotiation before genuine trade-offs have been explored.
- Neutral appraisal — bring in a mutually trusted expert or split the difference between two independent appraisals (the Mesabi ore fields precedent again).
- Post-settlement settlement (Howard Raiffa's idea) — after reaching an agreement, keep looking for improvements that make one or both sides better off without making anyone worse off. Shell's own field research found people rarely want to do this in practice: they're fatigued, their priorities have shifted mid-negotiation, and they worry the other side might use the extra round to renege on the original deal.

**Breaking an impasse:** leave yourself a rhetorical "back door" ("we are unable to continue at this time" — note "at this time"), build the other side a face-saving "golden bridge" back to the table, change negotiators or remove a poisoned intermediary if personal chemistry has broken down, or use the "one small step" / GRIT technique (Graduated and Reciprocated Initiatives in Tension Reduction) — one side makes a small, visible concession and waits for reciprocation before repeating the cycle (Anwar Sadat's 1977 flight to Jerusalem; a negotiator's M&M-counting ritual to restart mutual concessions in a stalled business deal).

**Commitment beats mere agreement.** An agreement signals present willingness; a commitment makes backing out costly. Devices that convert agreement into commitment, roughly in ascending order of formality: a handshake or equivalent social ritual; public announcement (which the parties themselves become accountable to, as in Trump's press conference for the Doug Flutie contract, or the Arusha public feast that seals the two-pigs land dispute); individualized accountability (Shell's "bring the hamburger" volunteer story — assigning each person a specific, missable contribution made a diffuse group of no-shows into reliable participants); a written, legally enforceable contract; and, where trust is thinnest, simultaneous exchange (title and payment change hands at the same instant).

## 13. Ethics in Negotiation

Deception is a structural feature of bargaining, not an aberration — Shell frames the negotiator's core ethical tension via Professor James White's line: "The negotiator's role is at least passively to mislead his opponent about his settling point while at the same time to engage in ethical behavior."

**The legal floor: six elements of fraud.** A misrepresentation is legally fraudulent only when it is a (1) knowing (2) misrepresentation of a (3) material (4) fact, on which the victim (5) reasonably relies, (6) causing damages. Key nuances Shell draws out:

- Bluffs about your own bottom line, demands, or alternatives are generally not "material" facts as a matter of law (the ABA's Model Rules explicitly exclude "estimates of price or value" and "a party's intention as to settlement" from the false-statement prohibition for lawyers) — but lying about a concrete, checkable fact like a rival bid that doesn't exist has produced real fraud verdicts against a landlord and a real-estate broker, particularly when the victim was an unsophisticated consumer or small business dealing with a professional.
- Silence is generally legal ("be silent and be safe") except in four situations: a partial disclosure that becomes misleading without the rest of the picture; a fiduciary relationship (partners, trustees, family-business members); one side holding vital information the other side has no access to (a seller's duty to disclose a defect, versus no duty for a buyer to disclose "hidden treasure"); or a specific statutory disclosure duty (insurance, securities, some state home-sale disclosure laws).
- Statements of opinion or intention can still be fraud if they flatly contradict facts the speaker already knows (a seller telling a buyer they'll have "no trouble" with a key customer while secretly owing that customer money and having lost their business already).

**Three schools of bargaining ethics** Shell offers as a self-diagnostic, not a verdict:

- The Poker School (associated with Albert Z. Carr's "Is Business Bluffing Ethical?"): negotiation is a game with legal rules; anything short of actionable fraud is fair play, and a skilled opponent should expect and even admire a well-executed bluff. Weaknesses Shell flags: it assumes everyone agrees bargaining is "a game" (idealists and pragmatists don't), assumes universal, well-known rules that don't actually exist across industries and jurisdictions, and leans on legal boundaries that are themselves uncertain.
- The Idealist School: the same ethics that govern ordinary social life should govern the bargaining table — if lying is wrong at the dinner table, it's wrong at the negotiating table. Idealists can decline to answer, deflect, or stay silent, but won't affirmatively lie, even about something as "trivial" as a comparison-shopping claim. Weakness: strict honesty can be exploited by less scrupulous counterparts, especially when representing someone else's interests.
- The Pragmatist School ("what goes around comes around"): deception is sometimes necessary, but the calculus is prudential rather than moral — lying is bad chiefly because it risks your reputation and future relationships, not because it's inherently wrong. Pragmatists draw a sharper line between lying about hard facts (never acceptable) versus lying about rationales or justifications (more tolerable, because harder to detect and less central to the deal), and rely heavily on "blocking" techniques (deflecting a question without an outright lie) as their preferred middle path.

**Self-defense against unethical tactics** (Shell's "rogue's gallery," useful as a checklist when building a negotiation-training skill): lies about bottom lines/alternatives; lowballing (get the other party committed, then move price against them by adding terms — used against a car buyer in Shell's own "Dale Singer" story); phony issues / decoys (push hard on several fake priorities, then trade them away for concessions on the one issue that actually matters — Guber-Peters used this against Sony); fake authority claims (either falsely claiming authority you don't have, or falsely denying authority you do have to avoid conceding); overcommitment exploitation; good guy/bad guy; consistency traps; reciprocity ploys (fake small concessions designed to manufacture obligation); and the nibble.

General countermeasures: Transactions (low relationship stakes, price-dominant) are the highest-risk setting for unethical tactics — be more alert there than in Relationship or Balanced Concerns settings. Use your relationship network to vet counterparts before you're exposed. Probe persistently rather than accepting explanations at face value ("in dealing with cunning people, we must ever consider their needs to interpret their speeches" — Francis Bacon). Be assertive when you sense manipulation. And never match unethical tactics with your own — doing so forfeits both your moral standing and, often, your legal recourse.

## 14. Style Diagnostics and Role-Based Effectiveness Tools

**Assessing your own style formally:** Shell recommends the Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument (developed 1970s, University of Pittsburgh/UCLA) as an inexpensive, quick way to get an outside read on your natural conflict-handling style; its five categories map directly onto the five bargaining styles in Section 2 (it uses "collaborate" where Shell uses "problem solve").

**Gender and cultural variables:** Shell is deliberately cautious about overreading these. Findings he cites as genuinely interesting rather than decisive: in a 762-person Harvard MBA study, men endorsed unethical bargaining tactics significantly more often than women did, though both genders endorsed standard competitive tactics like bluffing about bottom lines equally; separately, women were found more likely to tell "other-directed" lies (protecting someone's feelings) while men told more "self-centered" lies (personal advantage) — a pattern that could help explain a rapport-building advantage but doesn't reliably predict assertiveness or closing behavior. Any gender-based style gap in one Thomas-Kilmann study (female students preferring compromise, male students preferring competition) disappeared entirely once the same instrument was given to men and women already in the workforce. Shell's overall guidance: don't negotiate against a stereotype — negotiate against the specific person and evidence in front of you, updating your read as you go.

**Seven tools for highly cooperative people** (compensating for the weak habit of under-asserting):

- Spend deliberately more prep time on goals and expectations, not your bottom line — your bottom line is exactly what you'll get if that's your anchor.
- Develop a concrete fallback alternative before you sit down — "if you can't walk away, you can't say no" (Janie Mitcham's own railroad).
- Delegate to a more competitively-styled agent or teammate when facing a hardball counterpart — this is prudent, not a failure.
- Bargain as an agent for someone else (your family, your team, your future self) rather than "for yourself" — research shows people push harder on others' behalf than their own.
- Create an audience — tell someone your goals in advance and promise to report back; people negotiate more assertively when they know they're accountable to a watcher.
- Practice saying "you'll have to do better than that, because ___" — a stated reason (any truthful reason) sharply raises compliance in documented "because" experiments, and works the same way in reverse when you use it to push back.
- Insist on commitments, not just agreements, when dealing with people whose reliability is unproven.

**Seven tools for highly competitive people** (compensating for the weak habit of under-listening and over-exploiting):

- Reframe "win-win" as a genuine goal, not a soft concept — aim for both sides doing better, with you doing best.
- Ask more questions than feels natural before pouncing on an opening.
- Lean on standards and norms rather than raw leverage when the future relationship matters — reasoned arguments outperform power plays for durability.
- Delegate relationship management to someone with stronger people skills when the relationship carries real weight.
- Be scrupulously reliable — broken small promises are remembered longer than competitive people expect.
- Use integrative bargaining (issue trading) instead of haggling over every single point — haggling everything leaves money on the table in complex, multi-issue deals.
- Actively acknowledge the other party's self-esteem and (real or perceived) leverage, even when you hold the stronger hand — it costs little and is remembered when positions eventually reverse.

**The Information-Based Bargaining Plan** (Appendix B's working template — the single-document tool that operationalizes the whole book) walks through, in order: the problem statement (who you're negotiating with, to solve what); your specific goal, target decision-maker, bottom line, and relationship background; the interests at stake (shared / conflicting / ancillary); a leverage assessment (your best alternative, their best alternative, whether either can be improved, and who currently has the most to lose); possible proposals built from shared interests or creative bridges across conflicting ones; applicable authoritative standards and norms; potential third-party moves (as leverage, excuse, audience, or coalition partner); a side-by-side situational and style analysis (how you see the situation vs. how they likely see it, and what strategy adjustment that implies); and an overall positioning theme to hold the whole negotiation together under pressure.
