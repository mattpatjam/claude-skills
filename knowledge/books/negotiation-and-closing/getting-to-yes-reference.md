# Getting to Yes — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, designing negotiation/closing/sales processes, or advising on deal strategy.*
*Book: "Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In" — Roger Fisher & William Ury*

---

## 1. The Problem with Positional Bargaining, and the Alternative

Every negotiation can be judged by three criteria: does it produce a wise agreement (one that meets legitimate interests, resolves conflict fairly, is durable, and accounts for community interests), is it efficient, and does it protect or improve the relationship between the parties. Standard "positional bargaining" (each side stakes out a position, argues for it, then makes concessions toward a compromise) fails all three tests:

- **It produces unwise agreements.** Negotiators lock themselves into positions. The more you defend a position, the more your ego identifies with it, and the harder it becomes to reconcile the outcome with the parties' actual underlying interests. Example: US-Soviet nuclear test-ban talks collapsed over "how many on-site inspections" (3 vs. 10) when nobody had even defined what an "inspection" meant — the fight was over positions, not the substance an inspection was meant to serve.
- **It is inefficient.** Extreme opening positions, small concessions, stalling, and stonewalling are rational moves *within* positional bargaining, but they burn enormous time before either agreement or breakdown is reached.
- **It endangers the relationship.** Positional bargaining becomes a contest of will. Whichever side "bends" feels it lost, generating resentment that can outlast the deal itself.
- **It gets worse with more parties.** Coalitions form, positions calcify further because more people have to agree to change them, and reciprocal concessions become unworkable (150-party negotiations can't trade concessions bilaterally).

**"Soft" bargaining is not the fix.** Soft negotiators (friendly, generous, quick to concede) produce fast agreements but sloppy ones, and a soft player is systematically exploited by a hard player using the identical positional framework. Neither style solves the underlying problem — the choice between hard and soft is a false choice.

**The alternative: principled negotiation (negotiation on the merits).** Developed at the Harvard Negotiation Project, it is hard on the merits, soft on the people, and reduces to four points, one per chapter of the book's Method section:

1. **People** — Separate the people from the problem.
2. **Interests** — Focus on interests, not positions.
3. **Options** — Generate a variety of possibilities before deciding what to do (invent before deciding).
4. **Criteria** — Insist that the result be based on objective standards independent of either side's will.

These four points apply across three stages of any negotiation: **analysis** (diagnose the situation — perceptions, emotions, interests, existing options and criteria), **planning** (decide how to handle people issues, rank your interests, generate more options and criteria), and **discussion** (communicate back and forth using the same four elements to reach agreement).

Principled negotiation is a **dominant strategy**: if the other side also uses it, it becomes easier, not harder, to reach a wise outcome — unlike hard positional tactics, which become more costly to use the better the other side understands them.

## 2. Separate the People from the Problem

Negotiators are people first: they have emotions, values, backgrounds, egos, and blind spots — and so do you. Every negotiator has two distinct interests: in the *substance* of the deal and in the *relationship* with the other side. Positional bargaining forces these into conflict (if you give in on substance you seem to value the relationship; if you hold firm you seem not to). The fix is to separate the relationship from the substance and treat each on its own merits, dealing with people problems using psychological techniques rather than substantive concessions. The three baskets of people problems: **perception, emotion, and communication.**

**Perception**
- Conflict lives in people's heads, not in objective reality. Established facts often fail to resolve a dispute because the parties still disagree about what to do with them.
- **Put yourself in their shoes.** Understanding their point of view is not agreement — it's a tool. You have to feel the emotional force of their view, not just intellectually register it.
- **Don't deduce their intentions from your own fears** — a suspicious reading of ambiguous behavior forecloses real openings toward agreement.
- **Don't blame them for your problem** — blame is usually justified but always counterproductive; it triggers defensiveness. Separate the symptom from the person: describe the problem ("the generator broke down a third time this month"), not the person's character.
- **Discuss each other's perceptions explicitly and frankly**, without either side blaming the other. Investing time in things the other side wants to hear (even if you think the point is minor) can be one of the best-value moves in a negotiation — the Law of the Sea technology-transfer example shows how treating a request as "unimportant" wastes a low-cost opportunity to build credibility.
- **Look for chances to act inconsistently with their expectations** — a single surprising, disarming gesture (Sadat flying to Jerusalem in 1977) can do more to shift perception than any argument.
- **Give them a stake in the outcome by including them in the process.** People reject solutions they had no hand in shaping, regardless of the substantive merits — get them involved early, ask their advice, and give credit generously. "The process is the product."
- **Face-saving** is not a cosmetic trick — it's the real need to reconcile a negotiating position with one's principles, past statements, and self-image. Frame proposals so accepting them is consistent with the other side's values and past commitments.

**Emotion**
- Recognize and name emotions, yours and theirs, explicitly: "We feel we've been mistreated and we're afraid an agreement won't be honored — rational or not, that's our concern." Naming a feeling defuses it and moves the negotiation from reactive to purposive.
- **Let the other side vent.** Do not interrupt or walk out on an angry speech; listening quietly (without responding to the substance of the attack) provides psychological release and can free a negotiator to be more flexible afterward, especially in front of a constituency that needs to see them being "tough."
- **Don't react to emotional outbursts.** The steel-industry Human Relations Committee rule — only one person may be angry at a time — legitimizes venting while blocking escalation.
- **Use symbolic gestures.** A note of sympathy, an apology (which needn't concede fault), a small gift, a shared meal — low-cost acts that can produce outsized goodwill.

**Communication**
- Three chronic failure modes: (1) parties aren't really talking to each other, just performing for constituents/spectators; (2) even when talking directly, the other side isn't really listening (each side is busy composing its next point); (3) even clear statements get misunderstood, especially across language/cultural lines (the Persian mistranslation of "mediator" as "meddler" torpedoed U.N. Secretary General Waldheim's 1980 Iran trip).
- **Listen actively and acknowledge.** Interrupt to check understanding ("Did I understand correctly that...?"). The cheapest concession you can make is letting someone know they've been heard. Repeat their case back to them *positively*, in their own strongest terms, before you respond to it — if you can state their case better than they can, you maximize the odds of a constructive dialogue.
- **Speak to be understood, not to score points for an audience.** Negotiation is not a trial; limit group size, use private/confidential channels, and remember that important decisions are typically made when no more than two people are in the room.
- **Speak about yourself, not about them.** "I feel let down" instead of "You broke your word." Statements about your own feelings are hard to challenge and don't trigger defensiveness the way accusations do.
- **Speak for a purpose.** Full disclosure of your flexibility can hurt you (revealing you'd pay more than the seller asked kills the deal). Before saying something significant, know what purpose it serves.

**Prevention beats cure.** Build a personal relationship with the other side *before* you need it — arrive early, chat informally, find shared ground. And structurally, **face the problem, not the people**: sit side by side, literally if possible, with the shared documents/materials in front of you both, so the negotiation reads as two people jointly attacking a problem rather than attacking each other (the "two shipwrecked sailors" frame — disentangle needs and shared logistics from personal antagonism).

## 3. Focus on Interests, Not Positions

**The librarian story:** two men fight over whether a window should be open or closed. Instead of negotiating a compromise position (half open), the librarian asks *why* each man wants what he wants — one wants fresh air, the other wants no draft — and opens a different window entirely, satisfying both. This is the core move: **positions are what someone has decided; interests are what caused the decision.**

**Interests define the problem.** The stated dispute ("I won't pay a penny more than $95,000") sits on top of the real interests ("he needs $100,000 to settle a divorce; I told my family I wouldn't pay more than $95,000"). Reconciling interests, not positions, is why the Camp David Sinai negotiation worked: Israel's position (keep part of the Sinai) and Egypt's position (return every inch) were irreconcilable, but Israel's *interest* was security and Egypt's *interest* was sovereignty — met simultaneously by a demilitarized Sinai under full Egyptian flag.

**Why this works:**
- For any interest, multiple positions could satisfy it — looking past the first position that comes to mind opens alternatives nobody considered.
- Behind opposed positions there are usually more *shared* and *differing-but-compatible* interests than truly conflicting ones (a tenant and landlord both want stability, a well-kept building, and a good relationship — the differing interests, like allergy to paint fumes vs. cost of repainting, are the ones that actually need reconciling, and only the bare rent figure remains genuinely opposed).
- Agreement is frequently made possible **because** interests differ, not despite it (a buyer values shoes more than $30, a seller values $30 more than shoes — hence the sale).

**How to identify interests:**
- **Ask "Why?"** — Put yourself in their shoes and ask why they hold the position they hold; ask them directly, framed as "what's your basic concern," not "justify yourself."
- **Ask "Why not?" — analyze their presently perceived choice.** Map what they believe will happen if they say yes and if they say no (the book's example: a 1980 Iranian student leader's balance sheet on releasing US hostages shows why an apparently irrational holdout was actually a rational read of the incentives in front of him). Useful checklist of consequences to map: impact on their political support, their group's short/long-term interests, precedent set, consistency with principle, and reversibility.
- **Realize each side has multiple interests, not one**, and that "the other side" usually isn't a single person with a single interest — it's a constituency (a negotiator answers to a boss, a board, a spouse, a membership).
- **Look for basic human needs underneath stated demands** — security, economic well-being, a sense of belonging, recognition, control over one's own life. A dispute framed as "just money" (e.g., alimony) is often driven by needs for security and recognition that a bigger check alone won't satisfy if delivered without respect.
- **Write interests down** — a running list, ranked and updated as you learn more, both clarifies your own priorities and surfaces options.

**Talking about interests constructively:**
- **Make your interests come alive** with specifics ("a truck nearly hit seven-year-old Loretta Johnson at 8:30 Tuesday") rather than vague claims — concreteness builds credibility and impact.
- **Acknowledge their interests as part of the problem** before asserting your own — people listen better to those who've shown they understand them first.
- **Put the problem before your answer.** State your reasoning and interests first, your proposal last — if you lead with the proposal, they'll spend your entire explanation composing a rebuttal instead of listening.
- **Look forward, not back.** Ask "who should do what tomorrow," not "why did you do that yesterday" — arguing about causes is often a ritual with no negotiating purpose at all.
- **Be concrete but flexible — "illustrative specificity."** Go in with one or more specific options that would satisfy your interests, but hold them as illustrations, not commitments, so you stay open to better ones.
- **Be hard on the problem, soft on the people.** Advocate your interests aggressively — this is where negotiators should spend their energy — while simultaneously giving the human beings on the other side full personal respect and support. This combination creates useful cognitive dissonance: attacking the problem while supporting the person makes it easier for them to dissociate from the problem and join you in solving it. Either half alone (aggression without support, or support without pressure) is much less effective than the combination.

## 4. Invent Options for Mutual Gain

**The two-sisters-and-the-orange problem:** two sisters split an orange down the middle; one eats her half and discards the peel, the other bakes with the peel and discards the fruit. Neither asked what the other actually wanted. Negotiators routinely "leave money on the table" this way — reaching agreements that could have been better for *both* sides, or failing to reach agreement at all when a good deal was available. This happens because of four diagnosable obstacles:

1. **Premature judgment** — criticism kills invention before it starts; a critical sense "waiting to pounce" makes people afraid to float half-formed ideas, especially in front of the other side (an idea might be read as a binding offer, or as a naive concession).
2. **Searching for the single answer** — treating the goal as narrowing to one decision short-circuits generating the range of options needed to find a *good* decision.
3. **The assumption of a fixed pie** — treating the negotiation as zero-sum kills the motive to invent, since any gain for them looks like a loss for you.
4. **Thinking "solving their problem is their problem"** — partisan self-concern produces one-sided proposals that the other side has no reason to accept.

**The fix — four countermoves:**

**Separate inventing from deciding.** Run an actual brainstorming session (with colleagues, or even with the other side under explicit "off the record" ground rules) with one hard rule: no criticism during the generation phase. Practical mechanics:
- Before: define your purpose, keep the group to 5–8 people, change the physical setting from normal meetings, design an informal atmosphere, appoint a facilitator.
- During: seat people side by side facing the problem (not facing each other), state the no-criticism rule explicitly, generate a long list fast, record every idea in full view (blackboard/newsprint).
- After: relax the no-criticism rule to star the most promising ideas, improve them ("what I like best about that is... might it be better if..."), then schedule a separate time to actually decide.
- With the other side present: distinguish the brainstorming session explicitly from an on-the-record negotiating session, and float at least two options simultaneously (or deliberately absurd bracketing options) so nothing you say can be mistaken for a committed offer.

**Broaden the options before narrowing.** Use the **Circle Chart** — four types of thinking in sequence: (1) the concrete problem you observe → (2) diagnose it in general/theoretical terms → (3) generate general theoretical approaches/prescriptions → (4) translate back into specific, feasible actions. One good specific action can be reverse-engineered to find the general theory behind it, which then generates *more* specific actions (worked example: a joint Catholic/Protestant history workbook for Northern Ireland schools generates, once you name the underlying theory — "promote understanding in young children," "shared small projects" — further ideas like teacher exchanges and joint film projects).
- **Look through the eyes of different experts/disciplines** (banker, psychiatrist, nutritionist, tax expert) to surface options a single frame would miss.
- **Invent agreements of different strength**: substantive↔procedural, permanent↔provisional, comprehensive↔partial, final↔in-principle, unconditional↔contingent, binding↔nonbinding, first-order↔second-order (agreeing on where you disagree still counts as an agreement).
- **Change the scope** — fractionate a big problem into smaller, more tractable pieces ("edit the first two chapters for $120 and see how it goes"), or enlarge it to sweeten the pot (the India-Pakistan Indus River dispute became solvable once the World Bank's funding for new joint infrastructure entered the picture).

**Look for mutual gain.**
- **Identify shared interests** — they're rarely obvious mid-negotiation over price, but they're always latent (shared interest in the relationship, in avoiding the costs of no-deal, in a fair-price principle both respect). Make a shared interest concrete and future-facing by turning it into a joint goal ("bring five new industries to Pageville in three years" reframes a tax break as pursuit of a shared goal, not a concession).
- **Dovetail differing interests** — the deepest source of creative deals is that the parties want *different* things. Checklist of difference-types worth probing: differing interests (form vs. substance, symbolic vs. practical, short vs. long term, precedent vs. this case), differing beliefs (each side confident they're right → let a neutral arbitrator or vote settle it), differing time-value of money (installment plans), differing forecasts (incentive-based baseball contracts betting on performance), and differing risk aversion (deep-seabed mining royalties structured low while risk is high, higher once risk drops — risk traded for revenue).
- **Ask for their preferences among options you'd find equally acceptable**, then iterate — without anyone conceding anything, you can converge on the option that maximizes joint value ("Vive la différence!" — the negotiator's motto for dovetailing).

**Make their decision easy.**
- **Identify whose decision you're actually trying to influence** — negotiate with a specific person's actual choice, not an abstraction like "the insurance company."
- **Know whether you want words or performance** — don't inflate a price "for negotiating room" if what you actually want is the sale itself; if you want a horse to jump the fence, don't raise the fence.
- **Draft possible agreements early**, in the simplest form possible, and check: could the number of approvals required be reduced? Would it be easy for them to implement (it's easier to agree not to start something than to stop something already in motion)?
- **Shape proposals to look legitimate** — grounded in precedent, fairness, and consistency with the other side's own past statements or decisions.
- **Offers persuade better than threats.** Think through what the other side's harshest internal critic would say about the deal, and what their defense would be — this pressure-tests whether the proposal actually meets their interests.
- **Draft a "yesable proposition"** — a proposal specific and complete enough that a single word, "yes," would be sufficient, realistic, and operational.

## 5. Insist on Objective Criteria

Even with perfect handling of people and full understanding of interests, some interests genuinely conflict (you want lower rent, the landlord wants higher). Resolving that residual conflict through **will** (who is more stubborn, or more generous) is costly regardless of which side wins the contest — it produces mechanical splits instead of wise outcomes and it damages the relationship. The fix: **negotiate the substance based on standards independent of either party's will** — objective criteria.

**Why it works:**
- It reliably produces wiser, more durable agreements, since standards tied to precedent or community practice are less likely to later feel unfair to either side (and thus less likely to be repudiated).
- It's efficient — arguing over which standard applies wastes less time than arguing over positions, especially as the number of parties grows (100+-party talks cannot function on reciprocal position concessions at all).
- **Worked example — Law of the Sea deep-seabed mining fee dispute**: India proposed $60M/site, the US proposed zero, both dug in. Once an MIT economic model of deep-seabed mining economics was accepted as a neutral reference, both sides revised their positions based on what the model showed — neither side "lost," both looked reasonable, and the resulting fee schedule was durable because it was seen as fair rather than extracted.

**Developing objective criteria — types of fair standards:** market value, precedent, scientific judgment, professional standards, efficiency, costs, what a court would decide, moral standards, equal treatment, tradition, reciprocity. At minimum a criterion must be independent of either side's will; ideally it's also legitimate and practical. **Test: reciprocal application** — would you accept the same standard applied against you? (If a real-estate agency's "standard contract" wouldn't be the same one they use when *they* buy, it's not really an objective standard.)

**Fair procedures as an alternative to fair substantive standards:**
- "One cuts, the other chooses" (the child-splitting-cake rule) — applied at the Law of the Sea talks by having a private mining company nominate two sites, letting the UN's Enterprise choose one for itself and license the company the other, which gave the company incentive to make both sites equally attractive.
- Taking turns, drawing lots, letting a third party decide, or submitting to arbitration (including "last-best-offer" arbitration, used in professional baseball, where the arbitrator must pick one side's final number wholesale — which pressures both sides toward reasonable numbers in advance).

**Negotiating with objective criteria — three rules:**
1. **Frame each issue as a joint search for objective criteria** — "let's figure out what a fair price would be" reframes the negotiation from adversarial to a shared technical problem.
2. **Reason and remain open to reason** about which standard is most appropriate and how it applies — a principled negotiator, unlike a positional one, is genuinely persuadable on this question, not just deploying "principle" as a rhetorical weapon.
3. **Never yield to pressure, only to principle** — bribes, threats, appeals to trust, or flat refusals to budge are all pressure; the correct response in every case is the same: ask for the reasoning behind the position, propose objective criteria, and hold firm absent a principled reason to move.
- **Ask "what's your theory?"** whenever the other side states a bare position or number.
- If two standards both seem legitimate but produce different numbers, **splitting the difference between the two principled results is still principled** — the outcome remains independent of will.
- If you can't agree on which standard applies, **submit the choice-of-standard itself to a mutually trusted third party** — not to settle the substance, just to advise on which criterion fits.
- **Worked example — Tom's insurance claim.** The adjuster opens with a bare number ("$3,300 — that's the policy"). Tom repeatedly asks "how did you arrive at that?" and "what standard did you use?" instead of countering with his own number, eventually walks the adjuster through comparable listings (mileage, radio, air conditioning) as objective inputs, and leaves with $8,024 instead of the initial $3,300 — not by out-stubborning the adjuster, but by refusing to let the conversation rest on anything but a defensible number.

## 6. BATNA — Your Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement

No method of negotiation can manufacture leverage that doesn't exist — if you have $100 and the item costs thousands, no technique changes that. What principled negotiation *can* do in the face of a power gap: (1) protect you from accepting a deal you should reject, and (2) help you extract the most from whatever assets you do have.

**Why a fixed "bottom line" is the wrong protective tool.** Setting a walk-away number in advance (highest price you'd pay / lowest you'd accept) does guard against caving under pressure, but at high cost:
- It's rigid by design — you've pre-committed to ignore anything you might learn during the negotiation that should change your assessment.
- It suppresses creative, multi-variable solutions (a $160,000 bottom line on a house sale blinds you to a $135,000 deal with a resale right-of-first-refusal, a delayed closing, storage rights, and a buy-back option — which might serve your interests better than the "higher" number).
- It's usually set arbitrarily too high (through wishful group thinking) or too low (missing a better non-sale alternative like renting).

**The correct standard: know your BATNA.** The right question isn't "what should I be able to get" — it's **"what will I do if this negotiation fails?"** Generate and evaluate your actual alternatives (keep the house on the market, rent it, tear it down), and measure every offer against the best of those alternatives, not against an arbitrary number. This standard is flexible enough to accommodate an imaginative multi-variable deal that a bottom line would have rejected outright.

**Developing a BATNA — three steps:** (1) invent a list of things you could do if no agreement is reached, (2) improve the most promising ideas into real, executable alternatives (turn "I could look for work in Chicago" into an actual Chicago job offer in hand), (3) select, even tentatively, the single best option. A **trip wire** — a deliberately imperfect fallback package better than your BATNA — gives early warning that a deal is drifting toward unacceptable territory, while leaving room to still negotiate further (e.g., before bringing in a mediator).

**BATNA is the real source of negotiating power**, not wealth, size, or political connections. The wealthy tourist negotiating for a pot at the Bombay train station is *weak* if he doesn't know what a comparable pot costs elsewhere — his wealth doesn't convert into power until applied to learning his alternatives. Worked example: a small town negotiated a factory's "goodwill payment" from $300K/year to $2.3M/year purely because the town had developed a credible BATNA (annexing the factory into town limits to tax it at full residential rate), while the much larger corporation had no alternative to keeping the plant where it was.

**Practical BATNA moves:**
- Disclosing your BATNA is a judgment call: disclose it if it's genuinely strong and the other side underestimates it; keep it hidden if it's weaker than they assume.
- **Actively probe the other side's BATNA** — they're often overly optimistic about their own alternatives (the aggregation fallacy: totaling up several mutually exclusive options as if you could have them all at once). If their BATNA looks unrealistically strong to them, look for ways to legitimately show them otherwise.
- If both sides truly have excellent BATNAs, the best outcome for both may be **not reaching agreement at all** and each going elsewhere.
- The stronger the other side's raw power (money, muscle, size), the more you benefit from insisting on principle and investing in your own BATNA — principle is the asset that scales against brute leverage.

## 7. When They Won't Play: Negotiation Jujitsu and the One-Text Procedure

Three escalating responses when the other side keeps bargaining positionally despite your efforts:

**1. Just keep playing principled negotiation yourself** — it's "contagious": focusing on interests, options, and criteria tends to pull the other side toward the same frame over time, since positional moves stop getting traction.

**2. Negotiation jujitsu** — don't push back against their pressure; redirect it toward the problem, the way judo uses an opponent's own force against them. Their attack typically has three forms, each countered the same basic way:

- **Don't attack their position — look behind it.** Treat their stated position as one option, not a claim to accept or reject, and mine it for the interest underneath: "what exactly are the trade-offs that make you need X?" *Assume* their position is a genuine (if partial) attempt to solve the shared problem, and ask how it does so — this invites them to examine and often soften their own position without ever being told to. (Nasser's insistence that Israel "withdraw with no commitment from us whatsoever" fell apart the moment an American lawyer walked him through what would actually happen if Golda Meir agreed to exactly that.)
- **Don't defend your ideas — invite criticism.** Ask "what's wrong with this?" instead of asking for acceptance; use their criticism to find their real constraints and improve your proposal. Also ask directly for their advice ("if you were leading this, what would you do?") — it forces them to confront your half of the problem.
- **Recast a personal attack as an attack on the problem.** Let them vent, show you've understood, then redirect: "when you say we don't care about the kids, I hear your concern for the kids — we share it; what can we do now to get the strike resolved so we can get back to educating them?"
- **Two key tools: questions, and silence.** Questions invite answers instead of triggering resistance the way statements do, and they give the other side no position to attack. Silence — simply not filling an uncomfortable pause after an honest question goes weakly answered — creates pressure the other side often breaks by offering more information or a better idea. "Some of the most effective negotiating you will ever do is when you are not talking."

**3. Bring in a third party using the one-text procedure.** Rather than shuttling firm proposals back and forth (which locks each side in further with every round), a neutral party (mediator, or even a self-appointed participant whose real interest is getting *any* deal done) interviews both sides about *interests*, not positions, drafts a single non-binding proposal, circulates it for criticism only (not counter-offers), revises repeatedly, and eventually presents a final recommendation that each side can accept or reject with a single yes/no. This:
- Removes the psychological trap of each side's ego attaching to its own draft (no one, not even the mediator, is committed to any interim draft).
- Collapses what would be many-sided haggling into one decision per party.
- Scales especially well to multilateral negotiations, where 150 parties literally cannot trade bilateral concessions with each other. (This is exactly what the US did at Camp David in 1978 — 23 drafts over 13 days, ending in a text both Egypt and Israel accepted.)
- Can be self-administered: **"mediate your own dispute."** If your own stake is more in reaching *an* agreement than in the specific terms (a salesperson closing a deal between a customer and their own production team, a manager arbitrating between two subordinates), you can run this exact process yourself.

**Worked case: Frank Turnbull vs. Jones Realty (landlord/tenant rent-control dispute)** illustrates the technique end to end: open by inviting correction ("please correct me if I'm wrong"), acknowledge the other side's past kindness explicitly, state your standard as fairness rather than self-interest, deflect a "you just want money" attack back onto principle without losing composure, decouple "trust" from the substantive question when it's raised as a manipulation, ask questions instead of asserting facts, restate their position back to them positively before responding, take time before committing ("let me get back to you"), present all your reasoning before your proposal, offer a solution as *one* fair option rather than a demand, disclose the consequences of non-agreement as someone else's neutral recommendation (not your own threat) while keeping your own BATNA — which was actually weaker than the landlord assumed — undisclosed, and close by explicitly incorporating the other side's interest (timing of move-out) into the final deal so they can save face internally.

## 8. Countering Dirty Tricks (Taming the Hard Bargainer)

Tricky tactics — deception, psychological pressure, and positional-pressure plays — fail the test of reciprocity: they're designed for one-sided use. The standard bad responses are to **tolerate** them (Chamberlain at Munich: give in hoping it satisfies the other side, which often just invites escalation — Hitler raised his demands and later invaded the rest of Czechoslovakia) or to **mirror** them (an arms-race dynamic that usually ends in breakdown). Neither works. The correct response: **negotiate about the negotiating process itself**, using the same principled method applied one level up.

**Three-step process:** (1) recognize the tactic for what it is, (2) raise it with the other side explicitly ("I'm getting the sense you two are running good-cop/bad-cop — want to take a break to sort it out?"), (3) negotiate over its legitimacy and desirability using the same four elements — separate the people from the tactic (attack the tactic, not their integrity), focus on the interest the tactic is serving, invent an alternative "rule" for the game, and insist on reciprocity as the objective criterion ("shall we alternate who sits facing the sun?"). As a last resort, invoke your BATNA and be willing to walk.

**Deliberate deception:**
- *Phony facts* — don't extend unearned trust; verify factual claims the way you'd expect them to check your credit.
- *Ambiguous authority* — ask directly how much authority the other side actually has before any give-and-take starts; if they later claim they need higher approval ("second bite at the apple"), insist on reciprocity — you also reserve the right to reconsider until their approval comes through.
- *Dubious intentions about future compliance* — build compliance mechanisms into the agreement itself (the divorce example: convert "trust me, I'll pay child support" into a contingent forfeiture of house equity if two payments are missed — hard to refuse if they claim 100% certainty of compliance).
- Note: **withholding your full negotiating position is not the same as deception.** You're not obligated to disclose your reservation price; if pressed, redirect to a neutral third party who can confirm whether a zone of agreement exists without either side revealing numbers.

**Psychological warfare:**
- *Stressful physical setups* (uncomfortable seating, noise, no room to caucus) — name it and ask to fix it (change seats, take a break, relocate).
- *Personal attacks* (comments on appearance, forced waiting, refusing eye contact) — recognizing the tactic usually neutralizes it; naming it explicitly usually stops its recurrence.
- *Good-guy/bad-guy routine* — ask the "good guy" the exact same question you'd ask the "bad guy": what's the principled basis for this number?
- *Threats* — threats invite counter-threats and escalation, and often backfire by making the other side "dig in" against being seen to cave to coercion. Prefer **warnings** (consequences that will occur independent of your will) over threats (consequences you'd have to choose to inflict) — warnings are more legitimate and don't invite counter-threats. Make clear threats costly or hard to communicate credibly, and consider stating publicly that you only negotiate on the merits so a reputation, not a bluff, is doing the work.

**Positional pressure tactics:**
- *Refusal to negotiate* — probe their interest in refusing (fear of conferring status, fear of looking soft, disbelief that agreement is possible), and suggest indirect channels (third parties, letters, back-channel intermediaries).
- *Extreme opening demands* — call for the theory behind the number until it looks unreasonable even to them.
- *Escalating demands* (reopening settled issues, adding new ones with every concession) — name the pattern, consider a break to decide whether to continue at all, and insist on principle when you return.
- *Lock-in commitments* (the "two dynamite trucks" — publicly throwing away your steering wheel so the other side must swerve) — these depend on credible communication reaching you; you can defuse them by reframing the stated commitment as a mere "aspiration," refusing to treat it as serious, or simply declaring your practice of never yielding to pressure.
- *"Hardhearted partner"* ("I'd love to, but my partner won't allow it") — get agreement on the underlying principle (ideally in writing) and, where possible, go directly to the actual decision-maker.
- *Calculated delay* — negotiate the delay explicitly, and where possible create a fading opportunity of your own (parallel talks with a third party) or anchor to an objective deadline.
- *"Take it or leave it"* — not illegitimate by itself (most retail commerce works this way), but when used to end substantive negotiation it can be ignored at first, or met by naming what they lose if no deal happens and offering a face-saving frame for them to back off it.

**A personal check on your own conduct:** would this approach be acceptable used against a friend or family member? Would you be comfortable if it were reported in the newspaper? Answering honestly clarifies your own standard for what tactics you're willing to use — but whatever standard you set for yourself, be equally prepared to name and resist these tactics when used against you. Don't be a victim.

## 9. Sources of Negotiating Power

Negotiating power is the ability to persuade someone to do something — it is **not** the same as raw resources (wealth, military strength, staff size); whether a resource converts to power depends entirely on context (US wealth and nuclear weapons did nothing to free hostages in Beirut). Trying to answer "who's more powerful?" is actively counterproductive — concluding you're stronger breeds under-preparation, concluding you're weaker breeds discouragement, and neither answer tells you what to actually do. The useful move is to build power along six specific, developable channels:

1. **A good working relationship.** Understanding, being understood, and communicating well are not zero-sum — a better relationship increases each side's ability to influence the *other* simultaneously. Trust compounds: two trustworthy parties can each influence the other more than two distrustful ones can. Good listening surfaces the actual interests and options you need (the hospitalized patient who refused to transfer wasn't irrational — he was reacting to a lifetime of feeling abandoned, surfaced only once someone actually asked "why not?" instead of repeating the medical case).
2. **Understanding interests.** The deeper you understand what the other side actually wants, the more cheaply you can satisfy it (the radio-station buyer who discovered the "unreasonable" minority owner wanted to keep her management job, not maximize price, saved roughly a million dollars once he restructured the offer around that interest).
3. **Inventing elegant options.** A cleverly designed process (e.g. the sealed-bid stamp auction, where the winner pays the *second*-highest bid, so every bidder can safely bid their true maximum) can dovetail incentives so well that both sides do better than under a naive process.
4. **External standards of legitimacy.** Precedent and objective criteria work as both sword (persuading them) and shield (resisting pressure to concede arbitrarily) — "this is what GM paid for the same item last week" is far harder to argue against than a bare assertion.
5. **A developed BATNA**, including a "micro-BATNA" for what happens if just *this meeting* fails to reach agreement — worth drafting a good exit line in advance for that scenario. It's also sometimes legitimate to work on *worsening the other side's BATNA* (the father who stopped leaving his wallet out once he learned that was his son's alternative to earning lawn-mowing money).
6. **Carefully crafted commitment** — three distinct moves: (a) commit clearly to what you *will* do (a firm, concrete, ideally written or time-limited offer is far more persuasive and easier for the other side to say yes to than an open-ended one); (b) commit, with real caution, to what you *won't* do (locking in early damages the relationship and risks looking coercive — it's far less risky once interests are understood and options explored, especially if the rigidity is justified by something other than raw will); (c) clarify precisely what you want *them* to commit to (a vague or overbroad ask like "never interrupt me again" is a bad commitment — ambiguity about the actual demand undercut US pressure on Iraq in 1990, when it was never fully clear whether troop withdrawal alone, or also destruction of military capacity, would satisfy the coalition).

**Use these sources in combination, not isolation.** Leading with your strongest single card (e.g., flatly threatening to walk away using your BATNA) undercuts the persuasive power of your other arguments about fairness. The elements reinforce each other far more than any one deployed alone — and you will negotiate more effectively, over the long run, if you actually believe the case you're making rather than performing someone else's script.

## 10. Applying the Method: Fairness, People, Practical Tactics, and Limits

**On fairness and when positional bargaining is acceptable.** Principled negotiation takes more upfront work than simple haggling, so weigh: how much does an arbitrary/unprincipled outcome actually cost here (a one-of-a-kind low-stakes item vs. a load-bearing foundation or a precedent-setting deal)? How complex are the issues (complexity rewards joint problem-solving)? How much does the relationship matter going forward? How entrenched are the other side's expectations (adversarial habits built over years of ritualized bargaining, as in some labor-management relationships, may take several full negotiation cycles to shift — GM and the UAW took four contracts). Positional haggling does the least damage when it comes only *after* interests, options, and standards have already been explored.

Agreement on which fairness standard is "best" is not required for progress — using *any* external standard, even contested ones, still narrows the range of disagreement versus pure will-based haggling, and multiple legitimate-but-different standards can themselves be reconciled by splitting the difference between their results, or by a fair procedure (arbitration, a coin flip) once no persuasive basis remains to prefer one over another.

Getting more than you can justify as fair is possible but should be weighed against real costs: **durability** (unfair agreements invite later repudiation or non-performance, and courts can void "unconscionable" terms), **relationship/reputation damage** (a hard-won reputation for fair dealing unlocks creative agreements that trust alone makes possible — it's far easier to destroy than build), and **your own conscience** (the tourist who paid a Kashmiri rug-weaving family in worthless pre-WWII currency found the "win" soured every time he looked at the rug).

**On difficult people and irrationality.** "Separate the people from the problem" does not mean ignore people issues — it means disentangle *substantive* issues (terms, prices, dates, numbers) from *relationship* issues (trust, communication quality, emotional balance, degree of mutual understanding) and negotiate each on its own merits, never trading one for the other (don't concede substance to "improve" a relationship, and don't threaten the relationship to extract a substantive concession). If relationship problems persist despite good-faith effort, **negotiate the relationship itself**, directly and on its own merits, using the same tools (external standards, forward focus, no pressure tactics). When someone appears irrational, first question whether they're actually reacting rationally to a *different perception* of reality (the person who fears flying is behaving rationally against their belief that the plane will crash) — tracing the belief to its root, taking it seriously rather than dismissing it, is more likely to produce change than argument or punishment.

**On adapting style across people and culture.** Get in step with the specific person's pacing, formality, directness, time horizon, and preferred communication mode, but avoid stereotyping — individual variation within any group (nationality, gender, profession) is typically as large as variation between groups. Treat every assumption about "how this type of person negotiates" as a hypothesis to test, not a rule to apply.

**On practical tactics.**
- *Where to meet*: driven by what you're actually worried about — need for privacy, need for staff/reference materials on hand, or wanting the freedom to walk out easily (sometimes easier from their turf than yours).
- *Who opens*: less important than being well prepared with external standards of value; opening too early (before interests and standards are explored) risks feeling like a railroad job to the other side.
- *How high to open*: the highest figure you could justify to a neutral third party without embarrassment, presented with the reasoning first (an unjustified number heard before the reasoning gets tuned out) — and never advanced as a rigid position, since retreating from a "firm" number costs more credibility than retreating from a stated standard.
- Strategy is mostly a function of preparation, not cleverness — a scripted step-by-step plan collapses the moment the other side doesn't follow the expected sequence; know the terrain, not one fixed path through it.

**On moving from ideas to closure.**
- Envision what a completed, workable agreement would look like from the start, and work backward from what each side would need to be able to say to their own constituents to defend it.
- Draft a **framework agreement** early — even just a skeleton of headings to be filled in — to keep the conversation from missing key terms and to track progress.
- Treat all commitments as tentative until the whole package is done ("Tentative Draft — No Commitments" at the top of a working draft) — this frees people to explore options without the fear that every idea floated becomes a binding offer.
- Stay persistent about interests, flexible about the specific solution — when a proposal is challenged, defend the *interest* behind it, not the proposal itself, and ask if there's a better way to meet it.
- Make an offer once diminishing returns set in on further option-generation — it should read as a natural, non-surprising outgrowth of the discussion, not a first position and not an ultimatum.
- Where a few issues remain stuck, split the difference **between figures each backed by a legitimate standard** (not between arbitrary positions), or bring in a third party for a final recommendation.
- **Be generous at the very end** — a final, clearly-labeled concluding gesture of real value to the other side, consistent with your own logic, can dissolve last-minute hesitation and leaves them walking away feeling fairly treated, which pays off in both implementation and future dealings.

**On testing the method with limited risk.** Start on low-stakes negotiations where your BATNA is already solid and objective standards are readily available; build new techniques one at a time onto skills you already have; expect a temporary dip in performance while learning, the way any athlete does when overhauling a technique; keep a negotiation journal reviewing what worked after each significant negotiation; and prepare deliberately every time — preparation, not native talent or a clever script, is what negotiating power actually runs on. Role-playing the other side (with a friend, or by literally arguing your own case from their seat) is a specific, effective rehearsal technique the book recommends.

**On the limits of the method.** No amount of skill manufactures leverage that isn't there, and negotiation only makes sense when there's a realistic chance of beating your BATNA. Even negotiating with terrorists or adversaries as extreme as Hitler is a question of *how*, not *whether* — you are influencing their decisions (and they yours) whether or not you're formally talking, and direct dialogue, where personal safety allows, generally produces better outcomes than refusing contact altogether. Negotiating does not mean giving in: the 1981 Algeria-mediated resolution of the Iran hostage crisis worked because each side received only what it was actually entitled to, not because either side capitulated. War itself, when it happens, is often best understood as a continuation of negotiation by other means — intended to alter the other side's BATNA or their perception of it — which means principled-negotiation thinking about framing and communicating terms remains relevant even in that extreme case.
