# Customer Success — Framework Reference

*Pure framework reference distilled from the book. No commentary, no application notes — just the frameworks, models, and mental tools as the author presents them.*

*Source: Customer Success by Nick Mehta, Dan Steinman & Lincoln Murphy*

---

## 1. The Origin Story — Why Customer Success Exists

The book opens with the event the authors treat as the birth of the discipline. In spring 2005, Salesforce.com was riding an 88 percent bookings growth rate and a soaring market cap. At a company offsite, David Dempsey (head of renewals, nicknamed "Dr. Doom") presented data showing Salesforce's churn rate was 8 percent *per month* — meaning almost every customer was exiting the business every year. The insight: a recurring revenue business can show glowing new-customer-acquisition growth and still be in a death spiral if customers leak out the bottom faster than they're added at the top. That presentation is credited as the moment that triggered the customer success movement.

The underlying mechanic that makes churn existential in a subscription business: in the old "perpetual license" software model, a vendor collected as much as 80–90 percent of a customer's total lifetime value (LTV) in the initial sale. In the SaaS/subscription model, the initial deal is often less than 10 percent of total LTV (in a monthly-subscription business, it can be less than 1 percent). This inverts the economics: the vendor no longer has the customer's money up front, so unless the customer keeps renewing and expanding, the vendor never recovers its acquisition and onboarding costs (typically 24+ months of subscription revenue). This financial inversion — not goodwill, not a marketing trend — is presented as the actual cause of the customer success movement.

**A healthy recurring revenue business engine has three gears that all must be turning:**
- New customer acquisition
- High retention rates
- Positive upsell results (selling more to existing customers)

Only when all three are working does a business have the growth investors reward. This is why "successful customers do two things: (1) they remain your customers, and (2) they buy more stuff from you" is repeated throughout the book as the single test of whether customer success is working.

**Customer success is defined as three distinct but related things simultaneously:**
- An **organization** — focused on customer experience with the goal of maximizing retention and LTV.
- A **discipline** — like sales or product management, with its own best practices, forums, and practitioners (Customer Success Managers, or CSMs — also titled account managers, customer relationship managers, customer advocates, or client specialists).
- A **philosophy** — one that must pervade the entire company, not live in a single department.

**Key financial vocabulary used throughout:**
- **ARR / ACV** (annual recurring revenue / annual contract value) — the annualized amount customers pay on a recurring basis. Also tracked monthly as **MRR**.
- **LTV** (lifetime value) — total dollars a customer spends, or is expected to spend, over the life of the relationship. Often 10x or more the value of the initial deal in a healthy subscription business.
- **Churn** — the percentage of subscribers who discontinue, or the dollar value of ARR lost. "A customer who churned" refers to the lost customer; "churned dollars" refers to lost ARR.
- **Partial churn** — the customer doesn't leave, but you lose contract dollars: unused licenses returned, a product line dropped, or a deeper discount negotiated at renewal.
- **Second-order revenue** (coined by Jason Lemkin) — revenue generated indirectly by loyal customers: a happy user who changes jobs and buys your product again at their new company, or who refers friends. Estimated to add as much as 50–100 percent to a customer's effective LTV.
- **"Death and marriage" churn** — the industry euphemism for unavoidable churn: a customer goes out of business or is acquired. Distinguished from *avoidable* churn in reporting so it doesn't distort the picture of controllable risk.

## 2. Attitudinal versus Behavioral Loyalty

The book frames all of customer success as ultimately being in service of one goal: loyalty. It draws a hard distinction between two kinds:

- **Behavioral (intellectual) loyalty** — a customer stays because they have to, not because they want to. Example given: a shopper who buys bread and milk at the only store within 30 miles. They have no options, so their loyalty proves nothing about the vendor's quality.
- **Attitudinal (emotional) loyalty** — a customer stays because they love the brand or product. This is the loyalty every vendor actually wants: it survives price increases, resists competitive raids, and turns customers into active advocates.

Attitudinal loyalty is expensive and hard to build — "it's expensive to build products that customers love instead of products that they simply own." Two contrasting case studies the book uses to establish that customer success (under any name) is what creates it:

- **Marc Benioff / Salesforce** — built customer success out of necessity (to reduce churn).
- **Steve Jobs / Apple** — built customer success out of intuition, calling it the "Genius Bar": individuals whose job is to help customers get maximum value out of what they already bought, not to sell them anything new. Apple invested in this well before "customer success" was a term, driven by the belief that attitudinal loyalty carried the company through periods when its products weren't very good.

The larger claim: "in traditional businesses, the customer relationship ends with the purchase. In a subscription business, the customer relationship begins with the purchase" (credited to Tien Tzuo, Salesforce's 11th employee and later CEO of Zuora, who also coined the term *the subscription economy*).

## 3. Why Customer Success Matters — The Three Financial Justifications

The book identifies three basic, distinct benefits that justify investing in customer success, and stresses that the reason a company invests will shape how it should organize:

1. **Reduce/manage churn.** Investing in customer success cannot fix a fundamentally broken product, a bad implementation process, or a sales team that oversells — customer success can only optimize around a foundation that's already sound. Churn also has second-order damage beyond the lost revenue: the ex-customer often becomes a reference for a competitor, a "double-whammy" in competitive markets.
2. **Drive increased contract value for existing customers (upsell/cross-sell).** Even a company with zero churn can be leaving enormous money on the table. Example given: a company whose average customer expands 30 percent a year has a customer growing at only 10 percent (nominally "healthy" at 110 percent net retention) who should actually be treated as if the 20-point gap *were* churn, because moving underperforming accounts up toward the average delivers revenue more cheaply than acquiring new logos (no marketing spend, minimal sales spend).
3. **Improve the customer experience and customer satisfaction.** Some CEOs (Cornerstone OnDemand's Adam Miller is the example given) don't try to financially justify the investment at all — they build the cost into gross margin because delivering on the value promise is a matter of principle, trusting that financial results will follow.

## 4. Customer Success as an Organizational Change — Three Required Drivers

The book compares the emergence of customer success as a new corporate function to the earlier emergence of IT departments, and argues any real new organizational category requires three things to be simultaneously true:

1. **Business dependency** — the business's survival now actually depends on the function (not just benefits from it).
2. **New skills** — the work requires skills genuinely different from adjacent existing roles (you can't just relabel an existing team).
3. **New activities and metrics** — someone has to decide what will be measured (gross renewals, net retention, adoption, etc.) because the metric doesn't already exist elsewhere in the business.

**The organizational tension model (Joe and Sherrie).** The book illustrates healthy organizational design with a scenario: Joe (VP of Sales) and Sherrie (VP of Customer Success) both report to the CEO, both carry large bookings quotas (new business for Joe, renewals + upsell for Sherrie), and both lobby the VP of Engineering (Bill) for competing product priorities. The prescription: the CEO must ensure both have **equal authority and equal incentive weight** with every other org they depend on (especially product/engineering). This is a deliberate power shift — historically "sales is king," but as the installed base becomes more valuable than new bookings, power must shift toward whoever owns retention.

**Cross-functional impact — how each function must change:**

- **Sales & marketing (demand generation):** market and sell only to customers who can be successful long-term; de-emphasize maximizing the initial deal size at the expense of LTV; build renewal-awareness into the sales process; improve expectation-setting; invest in knowledge transfer to post-sales teams; incentivize reps on renewals/LTV, not just new bookings. At the extreme, some companies give the customer-success leader veto power over risky deals.
- **Product (product management + engineering):** shift thinking from "appeals to prospects" to "delivers to customers" — build ROI measurement into the product, make implementation easier, design for ease of adoption (not just functionality), value stickiness over feature count, value performance over demo polish, build modular upsell paths, and make customer self-sufficiency easier.
- **Services:** the core reframe is "in a recurring revenue business, there's no such thing as post-sales. Every single activity is a pre-sales activity" — because the next renewal decision is always pending. A support rep resolving a ticket should think of that resolution with the same urgency a sales rep brings to closing a deal this quarter, because the customer's decision clock never stops ticking.

## 5. What Customer Success Is Not

The book draws sharp boundaries against several adjacent, easily-confused disciplines:

- **Customer Experience (CX)** — the assessment and management of the overall customer experience across every touchpoint (sales, onboarding, invoicing, support, renewal), usually measured through surveys. Overlaps with customer success mainly in the shared use of survey data as an input to customer health.
- **Customer Relationship Management (CRM)** — the market category for tools like Salesforce, Microsoft Dynamics, and Oracle/Siebel. A market label, not a discipline or role — often mistaken for subsuming customer success because the acronym CRM would otherwise be the most literal name for what CSMs do.
- **Customer Advocacy** — the discipline of turning happy customers into references, case studies, and community participants (tools like Influitive). Advocacy is described as both a *source* of customer-health data and a likely *output* of high customer health — the two can form a virtuous cycle but are not the same thing.

**Customer Success is NOT Customer Support — the defining five-way contrast:**

- **Financial:** Customer Success is a revenue-driver; Customer Support is a cost center.
- **Action:** Customer Success is proactive; Customer Support is reactive.
- **Metrics:** Customer Success is success-oriented; Customer Support is efficiency-oriented.
- **Model:** Customer Success is analytics-focused; Customer Support is people-intensive.
- **Goal:** Customer Success is predictive; Customer Support is responsive.

The two are described as "paradoxical in many of the most important ways," not variations on a theme. Support's core identity is "break/fix" — the phone number, chat window, or email you use when something appears broken; it is judged by efficiency metrics like tickets closed per rep per day. A common organizational mistake is standing up customer success *inside* the support org — this typically degrades into a "premium support" tier (better SLAs, extended hours, a named point of contact) rather than a genuinely proactive, analytics-driven, retention-owning function. Both are 100 percent necessary; the prescription is organizational separation (at least initially), with close collaboration once each discipline is properly formed.

**What Customer Success positively is**, restated as five defining characteristics:

- **A revenue driver** — drives revenue two ways: renewals (avoiding churn, whether an explicit signed renewal or an implicit non-opt-out) and upsells (customers buying more).
- **Proactive** — acts on data-driven risk/opportunity signals rather than waiting for inbound requests; explicitly warns against staffing the function with people who have only ever been reactive.
- **Success-oriented** — driven by top-line metrics (renewal rate, upsell percentage, base growth), contrasted with efficiency metrics that are about cost reduction, not revenue growth.
- **Analytics-focused** — driven by forward-looking, predictive analytics that determine where CSM time is best spent, the same way sales analytics identify the best pipeline opportunities to work.
- **Predictive** — the contrast to mere "responsiveness": the goal is to know who to talk to *before* they call you.

## 6. Customer Success beyond SaaS — The Expanding Subscription Economy

The book argues customer success is not B2B-SaaS-specific; it is the natural response to any business model where the vendor holds ongoing power over whether the customer keeps paying. It traces the expansion of subscription/pay-as-you-go models across categories:

- **Long-established subscriptions:** magazines, fitness centers, cable TV, country clubs, hardware/software maintenance.
- **Newly adopted subscriptions:** movies (Netflix), satellite radio, music streaming, diet programs, HSA plans, grocery delivery.
- **Emerging subscriptions:** razors (Dollar Shave Club), meal kits, health drinks, package delivery (Amazon Prime), prescriptions, fitness-center aggregators (ClassPass).

Non-software examples used to demonstrate the concepts apply universally:

- **United Airlines** — a delayed-flight text message is framed as customer success: expectation management delivered at scale, functionally identical to a CSM emailing a customer that a promised feature has slipped.
- **Volkswagen** — cited as literally becoming a SaaS company via its Car-Net apps, sold as a $199/year subscription on top of standard CarPlay hardware.
- **Bright Horizons** (B2B corporate childcare benefits) and **Nipro Diagnostics** (home glucose meters, with test-strip replenishment functioning as a subscription) — both used to show high-touch and low-touch customer success models operating in non-technology, B2C-adjacent businesses.
- **Frequent-shopper and frequent-flyer programs** are framed as the pay-as-you-go equivalent of a subscription — they create the same lock-in and require the same proactive management even without a formal renewal date.

The universal underlying test proposed: *does repeat business matter to your model?* If yes, customer success concepts apply regardless of whether you call it that, sell software, or issue a renewal contract at all.

## 7. The High Touch / Low Touch / Tech Touch Segmentation Model

This is the book's core operating model for how customer success is actually delivered at scale, and it recurs as a lens applied to every one of the Ten Laws.

**High touch.** The most people-intensive model, justified by high per-customer revenue. Consists of a deliberate mix of scheduled interactions (defined onboarding, coordinated vendor-group handoffs, monthly status meetings, executive business reviews (EBRs) quarterly or biannually, on-site visits, regular health checks, renewal prep) and unscheduled, risk-triggered interactions (multiple outages, elevated support-case volume, declining usage, overdue invoices). The retention target for high-touch customers is effectively 100 percent — anything less is treated as a major failure, because these are also usually the accounts with the biggest expansion upside. An individual CSM in this model typically manages 5–15 accounts.

**Low touch.** The "tweener" tier — not big enough for white-glove treatment, but valuable enough to warrant *some* one-to-one contact. The organizing idea is **just-in-time (JIT) customer success**, borrowed explicitly from the Toyota Production System: deliver exactly what the customer needs at exactly the right time — not a minute before or after, and not more than necessary, because there's no "goodwill inventory" budget for this tier the way there is for high touch. A low-touch CSM typically manages 20–50+ accounts, and the same scheduled-touch list from high touch gets systematically thinned (packaged onboarding instead of a defined bespoke process, EBRs annually instead of quarterly, automated health checks, auto-renewal instead of manually-prepped renewal).

**Tech touch.** All customer touches are technology-driven and one-to-many, because one-to-one contact cannot scale to this volume economically. Channels: email (the most powerful — timely, relevant, essentially free at scale), webinars, podcasts, online communities, user groups, and customer summits. Effective tech-touch email depends on the volume of data a vendor already holds about a customer (original contract date, tenure, industry, contract value/growth, support history, invoice history, satisfaction/health scores, every product click) — the same data that fuels a health score can trigger highly personalized, seemingly one-to-one automated outreach at zero marginal cost.

**Headcount modeling implication:** once the three tiers and their expected task loads are defined, you can estimate CSM time-per-customer and back into a headcount model. Two caveats the book flags explicitly: (1) your first-pass headcount ask will almost always be larger than finance will approve — treat the model as a tool for informed trade-off conversations, not a mandate; (2) CSM-to-account ratios should be set by **contract value (ARR)**, not raw customer count, because a $2M customer and a $20K customer are not equivalent — though a hundred $20K customers can, in aggregate, be harder to manage than one $2M customer.

A guideline for finding the tier boundaries: apply the Pareto principle to your ARR distribution (where does 80 percent of revenue come from?) to decide how many accounts warrant high-touch versus low-touch coverage.

## 8. The Ten Laws of Customer Success — Framing

Part II of the book adapts and extends *The Ten Laws of Cloud Computing* (a 2010 Bessemer Venture Partners guide) into *The Ten Laws of Customer Success*, commissioned by Bessemer in 2015 and authored by ten different named experts across SaaS, on-premise software, finance, education, collaboration, project management, sales enablement, CSM, and compensation management. Each law is tagged for relevance across five business types (B2B SaaS, subscription-based, pay-as-you-go, B2C, traditional), and each chapter closes with a High Touch / Low Touch / Tech Touch breakdown of how the law applies differently by tier. The Ten Laws answer one question: *what does my company need to do to be great at customer success and build a thriving recurring revenue business?*

---

## 9. Law 1 — Sell to the Right Customer

The foundational law: churn prevention starts before the sale, not after it. A frequently cited industry maxim in the chapter: "ninety percent of all churn happens at the time of sale" — i.e., most churn is actually a downstream symptom of having sold to a customer who was never going to succeed with the product.

**The true cost of selling to the wrong customer** is not just the eventual churned-dollar loss. It compounds through the whole post-sale chain: harder onboarding, heavier product-team burden accommodating an outside-the-box use case, an eventual pre-renewal "SWAT team" rescue effort (which the book estimates saves the renewal only about half the time, usually only by discounting or extending free months — which just kicks the same unresolved problem into the next contract term), and finally negative word-of-mouth from a customer who blames the vendor rather than the mismatch.

**Defining the right customer** requires alignment, starting with the CEO, on product-market fit (PMF) and an explicit target customer profile — by use case, vertical, size, or some blend — and then operationalizing that definition through the whole funnel: marketing targets the right profile, sales disqualifies poor fits early (going beyond standard qualification criteria like budget/timeframe/sponsorship), and a scalable system captures fit data so it can be tracked against CAC ratio, net churn, and LTV.

**Mitigation tactics offered for selling to the wrong customer:**

- **Use data, not anecdotes.** Replace "I think we sold to a customer like that before and it didn't work" with a specific cohort analysis (e.g., "31 customers matching this profile; 14 churned at first renewal, 4 more at second; remaining 5 renewed at a 14 percent average contract decline; average NPS 5.2, average health score 38.7").
- **Give the VP of customer success veto power over pipeline deals.**
- **Put customer success under the sales VP** (the book explicitly does *not* recommend this generally, but notes it forces the same leader who makes the sell decision to live with its consequences).
- **Incent the VP of sales — and ideally the whole sales org — on retention, not just new bookings.**
- **Escalate to Law 10** — ultimately this has to be enforced by a CEO willing to say no to bookings-accretive-but-unsustainable deals.

**Churn-reason discipline:** track and regularly analyze the *reasons* behind churn across your base — what percentage was poor adoption, product-fit gaps, or truly unavoidable factors (M&A, restructuring, bankruptcy) — because this data should feed back into refining who "the right customer" actually is.

**By touch tier:** high-touch customers suffer proportionally more from a mis-sell because more resources are already staffed against them (a 10–20 percent burden increase is 10–20 percent of a much bigger number, and their brand value amplifies negative word of mouth). Low-touch mis-sells are individually less painful but numerically more frequent and harder to rescue because fewer resources exist per account. Tech-touch mis-sells are essentially unsalvageable — you cannot rescue a bad-fit customer through email and webinars alone; the multiplier effect (10x worse moving down each tier) is explicit.

## 10. Law 2 — The Natural Tendency for Customers and Vendors Is to Drift Apart

**The core metaphor:** a customer and vendor are like two boats side by side in the middle of a lake with no one rowing either one. Left alone, constant background change (in both organizations) will drift them apart. Customer success organizations exist specifically to "get into one of the boats and start rowing" — intervention against drift is not optional maintenance, it is the entire job.

**Nine named churn triggers**, each given with telltale warning signs and a countermeasure:

- **Financial return/business value not realized** — *signs:* declining usage or inactivity post-signup. *Countermeasure:* CSM reviews client goals, guides through adoption phases for early value, continuously looks for expansion of use to generate more ROI.
- **Stalled or prolonged implementation** — *signs:* product never reaches operational/production status. *Countermeasure:* define smaller implementation phases that get partial value live faster instead of one long monolithic rollout.
- **Loss of project sponsor or power user** — *signs:* the customer "goes dark," the champion is unreachable. *Countermeasure:* train multiple users (not just one), and maintain executive-level relationships as a fallback when a champion leaves.
- **Low rate of product adoption** — *signs:* little to no usage, or a declining trend. *Countermeasure:* map a customer journey of functionality to adopt, build a library of ROI stories/testimonials to reignite interest.
- **Acquisition by a company using another solution** — *signs:* customer contact reports an acquisition or new leadership mandating a different tool. *Countermeasure:* attempt to present value to new leadership directly; often unrecoverable ("the die has been cast").
- **Lack of product features** — *signs:* customer actively requests features/enhancements or more aggressive pricing. *Countermeasure:* keep CSMs current on the roadmap, involve customers in feedback loops so they feel like part of the process.
- **New leadership driving a shift in direction/strategy** — *signs:* being pulled into an RFP or a formal solution evaluation. *Countermeasure:* proactively reach the new leader before the comparison happens — "the comparison of your existing installed product, with all its warts, does not usually stand up well against PowerPoint slides and demos from your competition."
- **Poor product quality or performance issues** — *signs:* rising support-ticket or escalation volume. *Countermeasure:* set an automatic alert threshold (e.g., 3+ tickets in a week triggers an action plan), escalate internally, communicate progress proactively.
- **Product is not the right solution** — *signs:* customer's understanding of core capabilities doesn't match reality, or requests fall outside your wheelhouse. *Countermeasure:* trains back into Law 1 — educate sales on ideal-use-case parameters and teach professional services to spot mismatches early.
- **The human factor** — *signs:* negative feedback (direct or secondhand) about a specific team member's fit with a specific customer. *Countermeasure:* don't ignore it — proactively confirm whether the relationship is salvageable or whether the resource needs to be swapped; delaying that swap compounds damage.

**Maintenance touchpoints recommended to counteract drift generally:** proactive CSM/executive outreach, timely relevant email content, high-quality customer webinars, an active customer community, regular user-group meetings, customer advisory boards, and user conferences.

**By touch tier:** high-touch drift is both easier (tight relationship, frequent contact) and harder (higher stakes, more organizational complexity and politics on the customer's side, champions can vanish overnight in a reorg). Low-touch and tech-touch customers force a vendor to invest in the most scalable levers — process and product — rather than any individual relationship, since retention/churn targets naturally run lower the further down the tier pyramid you go. Three specific tools recommended for detecting drift in tech-touch relationships you never personally talk to: **surveys**, **community** monitoring, and **rigorous post-churn analysis** (understanding *why* a customer left is treated as more valuable and more tractable than trying to understand why customers who stayed did so, because churn is a discrete, considered event).

## 11. Law 3 — Customers Expect You to Make Them Wildly Successful

**The reframe:** customers today don't just expect a working product — they expect the vendor to actively guide them to a *specific, defined outcome*, and they hold the vendor responsible for closing that gap. A CIO quote used to crystallize this: "None of them challenge us. They come in, install the software, and then move on... We're not just paying for a product — we want expertise as well."

**Three things you must always know about a customer to deliver "wild success":**

1. **How are they measuring success?** — their specific unit of currency (time saved, revenue gained, cost reduced) and the threshold that would let them declare victory.
2. **Are they achieving that value** (or credibly on track to)?
3. **What has their experience been along the way?** — the qualitative dimension; even a customer who technically hits their metric can still churn if the path there was painful and effortful beyond what they consider reasonable.

**"ROI isn't a concept, it's an equation."** The discipline is to quantify expected outcomes as concretely as the sales cycle already did — reduce churn *by how much*, identify *how many* upsell opportunities of *what size*, what productivity gain is expected and how it will be measured — and then set those numbers as explicit tracked targets, not implicit assumptions.

**Cadence discipline:** use regular strategic/quarterly business reviews (SBRs/QBRs) built around jointly-defined success criteria, not just roadmap updates and open-ticket reviews — a QBR that only covers unresolved features and bugs is "extremely defensive" and nobody wins that conversation. Every QBR should end by setting the measurable objective for the next one.

**"Success isn't a destination, it's a journey."** Once a customer hits an initial goal (e.g., moved renewal rate from 85 to 88 percent), the vendor's ongoing value is to show what best-in-class customers achieve next (e.g., 90+ percent) and help chart the path there — via content marketing for lower-touch tiers, via executive alignment conversations for higher-touch tiers.

**The Challenger dynamic:** the book explicitly invokes the *Challenger Sale* methodology, arguing the CSM (and the whole customer-facing team) has to be willing to challenge the customer's plan when it's heading somewhere unproductive — "allowing your customers to go down the wrong path will bring potentially disastrous results, so challenging customers to do it the right way is mandatory." Demanding customers are explicitly reframed as a *positive* signal, not a red flag: "do not mistake demanding for unsuccessful... the traits that make a customer demanding are also likely to ensure that they are getting maximum value from your product."

**By touch tier:** for high-touch customers, this burden concentrates on one individual CSM who must personally understand the customer's success definition, quantify ROI, and deliver training — a heavy, largely un-automatable job today. For low-touch customers, the danger is a mismatch between the customer's expectation of unlimited human help and the reality of rationed CSM time — the book contrasts two ways of setting that expectation in writing ("your CSM team can be of assistance on a limited basis" vs. no mention of a CSM at all) and stresses being explicit about the limitation rather than implying unlimited access. For tech-touch, in-app guidance tools (the book names WalkMe as an example) and built-in feedback prompts (e.g., detecting a user stalled on a screen) substitute for a human relationship, paired with early-warning triggers if a new customer hasn't hit a defined value milestone.

## 12. Law 4 — Relentlessly Monitor and Manage Customer Health

The chapter deliberately unpacks its own title word by word, arguing each word carries independent weight and none of the four can be dropped.

**Why customer health matters (the sales-pipeline analogy):** understanding customer health does for a VP of customer success what pipeline visibility does for a VP of sales — it (1) predicts future behavior, (2) predicts the *timing* of that behavior, and (3) enables better team management, without waiting 12 months to find out whether a CSM's book of business is actually healthy.

**Health, as a concept, works exactly like a human physical exam:** you run a set of tests, compare against a definition of "healthy," and arrive at a composite score (the book's illustrative example: normal vitals, slightly high cholesterol, 10 lbs overweight → a score of 84/100).

**Possible components of a customer health score**, offered as a menu (every company must decide which apply and are measurable for them):

- **Product adoption** — frequency and depth of use; use of "stickiest" features; how many/which people use it (does it reach executives, board meetings?). Considered the single highest-value signal if you can get it.
- **Customer support** — call/case frequency and duration; too little or too much activity are both signals worth investigating.
- **Survey scores** — direct verbal feedback, treated as the equivalent of what a customer explicitly tells you in a human relationship.
- **Marketing engagement** — opens, click-throughs, bounces, unsubscribes on outbound campaigns.
- **Community involvement** — asking vs. answering questions, voting on proposals.
- **Marketing participation** — willingness to be a reference, give a case study, speak at a conference.
- **Contract growth** — a stagnant or shrinking contract size over years is a red flag regardless of stated satisfaction.
- **Self-sufficiency** — customers who need less hand-holding to use the product effectively tend to be healthier.
- **Invoice history** — on-time payment as a loyalty signal ("healthy, happy, and loyal customers pay their bills on time. Period.")
- **Executive relationship** — depth and seniority of the vendor's relationship inside the account.

**"Manage" means the score must be actionable, not observational.** The prescription is a **component-based** score, not just one aggregate number — because a CSM handed only an overall number has no lever to move it ("go boil the ocean"), whereas a CSM who can see that *product adoption* specifically is dragging the score down (13 of 100 licenses active) has a concrete, targeted action.

**"Monitor" requires systematized tracking**, not spreadsheet archaeology — though the book notes there's real value in first experiencing the manual pain of tracking health by hand, both to properly motivate automating it and to work the kinks out of a process before locking it into software (automating a broken process just "makes mistakes faster").

**"Relentlessly" is presented as non-negotiable** given how existential retention is to the business model — daily work naturally expands to fill available time (the "gas in a vacuum" framing), so health monitoring has to be an enforced discipline, not something squeezed in when convenient.

**The health "sine wave."** Customer health is explicitly *not* linear or constant — it more closely resembles a sine wave than any steady trend, and can swing from "awesome reference" to "at risk" within a single release cycle (e.g., an upgrade that breaks a key feature) and back again within weeks based on responsiveness. Understanding this shape is what lets a team "take advantage of the peaks and intervene in the valleys" rather than treating any single data point as a permanent verdict.

**By touch tier:** high-touch health management happens mostly one-to-one (a personal call to ask for a reference, a personal call to work through a problem) but can still borrow tech-touch tactics for efficiency (an automated-but-personalized-looking email asking for a review, gated by rules like "health score >85 and zero open tickets"). Low-touch is the natural home of just-in-time intervention — very few regularly scheduled touches, most triggered by a rule (e.g., "below-average CSAT + health score below 70 triggers a call"). Tech-touch is nearly free to execute but easy to overuse; the mitigating factor is that if every automated touch is genuinely personalized, timely, and relevant, it will not read as spam regardless of volume — but deliverability, open rate, and click-through trends must be actively tracked to confirm that's actually true.

## 13. Law 5 — You Can No Longer Build Loyalty through Personal Relationships

**The historical shift:** in the pre-SaaS enterprise-software era, personal relationships built by account managers were themselves a primary driver of loyalty. As SaaS lowered price points and expanded addressable markets (down to $45,000/year deals, then far lower, sometimes free), the economics of maintaining a personal relationship with every customer collapsed — loyalty has to be built through **value delivered by the product and by scalable programs**, not personal rapport, for the majority of a modern customer base.

**A six-step process for building loyalty without one-to-one relationships:**

1. **Segment your customers by a metric that fits your business** (most commonly ARR) — segmentation reveals behavioral differences by slice (e.g., larger customers may renew more readily after a milestone; smaller customers may churn faster but expand more readily once they're past initial success).
2. **Define a customer coverage model based on that segmentation** — decide how many accounts fall into high-touch, low-touch, and tech-touch, often using the Pareto principle against ARR (where does 80 percent of revenue live?) to draw the tier boundaries.
3. **Create customer interaction categories per tier**, e.g.:
   - *High touch:* multiple in-person meetings per quarter, QBRs, a formal success/blueprint plan, one-to-one executive meetings.
   - *Low touch:* one in-person meeting per quarter (or as needed), at least one high-value video/online meeting monthly, periodic executive touch.
   - *Tech touch:* webinar-style one-to-many product-adoption sessions, monthly-to-quarterly newsletters, data-triggered email campaigns, on-demand training, a community portal.
4. **Establish a communication cadence** — weave tier-specific touches into a broader macro-communication calendar (newsletters, regional user groups, an annual conference) so every customer, regardless of tier, hears from you at least monthly through *some* channel.
5. **Build a strong, loyal customer community** — give customers a place to connect with each other (portal, regional meet-ups, conferences) because that connection will happen with or without you; making it easy converts your best customers into what the book calls **"company success agents"** — customers who evangelize and convert other users simply by talking about their own success, a role the book argues can become as valuable as a CSM's.
6. **Develop a customer feedback loop** — surveys, a suggestion box, focus groups, one-to-one meetings, or a customer advisory board, giving customers a structured channel to shape product, support, and company direction.

**Three communication principles that hold across every tier:** communicate often, set clear expectations, and be as transparent as possible.

**By touch tier:** this law barely applies to genuine high-touch customers, where personal relationships remain the norm and function much as they did in the old enterprise-software era (many 1990s account managers transitioned directly into high-touch CSM roles). It becomes decisive starting in low touch, where JIT delivery has to substitute for a strong personal relationship, and is existential in tech touch, where the vendor must "win by building value, not relationships" because one-to-one contact is economically impossible at that customer count.

## 14. Law 6 — Product Is Your Only Scalable Differentiator

**The central claim:** every part of a company can become somewhat more efficient, but only the product itself can be built once and deliver value to millions of users without adding headcount — "make once, ship many." The thought experiment the book poses: if you built a truly perfect product, which internal teams would become unnecessary? Its answer list: provisioning, implementation, training, customer support, customer success, operations, most of professional services, and renewals. In that world there would be no concept of "post-sales" at all — just millions of customers using and loving the product and spreading the word.

**Two structured feedback mechanisms for feeding the voice of the customer back into product:**

- **Product Advisory Councils (PACs)** — a structured, interactive forum where select senior-level clients engage directly with product management. Focus areas: helping define product vision/strategy against real business problems (current and future), discussing how clients view the product's approach to those problems, factoring in market/technology trends clients are seeing, and helping with strategic-level feature prioritization. PACs are run with real commitment structure: members apply, commit to a full calendar year, commit to a defined number of meetings (e.g., three roadmap-review sessions plus two feature-prioritization sessions annually), cannot delegate the roadmap-review meetings, and agree to participate in the reference program. A recommended practice: send a quarterly recap showing which client-requested changes actually shipped, to keep the "voice of the client" credible and visible.
- **Communities of Practice (COPs)** — similar in purpose but organized around business processes and practices rather than product features; typically larger groups than a PAC, and function as peer-to-peer forums across a variety of business sectors.

**Three customer-feedback metrics named explicitly, with their measurement mechanics:**

- **Customer Satisfaction (CSAT)** — usually a 5-point scale; the key requirement is the ability to benchmark against historical data; used for both transactional (post-interaction) and relationship (overall) surveys.
- **Net Promoter Score (NPS)** — measures loyalty via willingness to recommend, not raw satisfaction. Always an 11-point (0–10) scale. Calculated as (% giving 9 or 10) minus (% giving 0–6). Mostly a relationship-level metric.
- **Customer Effort Score (CES)** — measures ease of doing business, on a 1–7 agreement scale. The tracked metric is the percentage of respondents who at least "somewhat agree" that resolving their issue was easy. Mostly a transactional-level metric.

**Design principles the book calls out for product itself as a retention lever:** rich, self-service search functionality (because users expect to look anything up without effort); built-in self-diagnostic tools so users can solve their own problems; and mobile-aware design, since users are no longer tied to a single desktop workstation.

**By touch tier:** in high touch, the critical lever is the communication pipeline between CSMs (who function, in effect, as field product managers) and the product team — without a defined process for surfacing what CSMs are hearing, that intelligence dies with the individual conversation. In low touch, communities, forums, surveys, and a well-composed advisory board (deliberately matched to your actual customer mix — don't fill a board with enterprise voices if 70 percent of the business is SMB) substitute for direct CSM-to-PM relay. In tech touch, the highest-signal feedback often comes directly from in-product usage data rather than any direct conversation, and high customer volume makes lightweight in-product experimentation (adding a feature for a day and observing the effect) cheap and viable.

## 15. Law 7 — Obsessively Improve Time-to-Value

**The core discipline, in three parts:**

1. **Establish concrete success measures before the clock starts.** Capture the business sponsor's specific success metric *during the sales cycle*, while their attention is highest — trying to extract it later, once the sponsor has moved on to other priorities, is far harder. When a customer doesn't have a specific measure ready, the book supplies a menu of common examples to offer them (decreased time to reach quota, increased converted leads, increased deal size, decreased time to onboard reps, decreased time to first close, and similar), and insists on capturing the *baseline* value for whichever metric is chosen. Only once success measures are locked in should the onboarding clock formally start — otherwise a company can reach full implementation with no way to tell whether the customer actually got value.
2. **Implement iteratively for early value** rather than deferring all value to a single "go-live" moment — get the simplest measurable win live first, then layer in the rest. Reconnect with the business sponsor at multiple points during onboarding specifically to re-validate the chosen measures are still the right ones.
3. **Adjust in real time.** As onboarding nears completion, bring the CSM into project status calls and brief her on the agreed success criteria, because customer project teams typically lose focus and drift back to their day jobs the moment onboarding formally ends — the CSM's top priority in the following weeks is being "obsessive" about closing the gap to the stated value, ahead of any other standard CSM activity (feature intros, QBRs, etc.).

**The compounding-days argument for urgency:** the book frames time-to-value improvement in terms of the selling days it frees up — multiply the number of days saved per customer by your total customer count, and the result is the number of *additional selling days* available across your business, because a customer that reaches value faster is also a customer that can be sold expansion faster. Upsell literally cannot begin before the customer is deriving real value; no one buys more of something they haven't yet gotten working.

**By touch tier:** for high-touch customers, the key nuance is that "project complete" is never the same thing as "value received" — measuring implementation duration (kickoff date to sign-off date) is comparatively easy, but *value* requires a proxy (which features matter most, and are they being used?) or a direct conversation, since "value" itself resists clean measurement. For low-touch customers, time-to-value pressure is often *higher* despite smaller budgets, because expectations don't scale down with contract size ("we're a small company — why does this take so long?"); the countermeasure is rigid, prescriptive, pre-defined implementation steps with hard time boxes. For tech-touch, in-app guidance, social login (skipping setup friction), and instrumented onboarding milestones (Dropbox's "upload your first file" prompt is the example used) matter more than anything else, because the entire experience must be self-service.

## 16. Law 8 — Deeply Understand Your Customer Metrics

**Five steps to capture, measure, and understand churn and retention:**

1. **Define what you are measuring and the components of CMRR.** CMRR (**committed monthly recurring revenue**) is the combined value of all recognized recurring subscription revenue plus signed-but-not-yet-live contracts, minus churn. Decide whether measurement happens per-customer, per-contract, or both — this decision cascades into how your order process must be built to capture order type (new/add-on/renewal), upgrade/downgrade amounts, downgrade reasons at the SKU level, and cancellation reasons (ideally both a standardized picklist and a free-text field). Distinguish quantity downgrades from price downgrades — they represent very different problems.
2. **Define the period and frequency of measurement** (weekly/monthly/quarterly/annually), aligned to typical customer commitment length. A common pattern: measure granularly (monthly) but *report* as an annualized rate for executive consumption. Define explicit rules for handling early and late renewals so they don't distort the period's churn/retention numbers — best practice for a late renewal is to shift the expected CMRR and customer count to the next period while keeping the original subscription start/end dates intact.
3. **Determine expected CMRR and categories of churn.** Best-practice formula: expected CMRR = prior-period renewal CMRR + annualized value of in-period add-ons. Decide, and agree with finance, the exact point in the fiscal period when expected CMRR gets "locked." If your base is highly dynamic, build a planned churn rate (e.g., 10 percent) directly into your bookings/revenue plan as an accepted cost of doing business, so mid-term add-ons don't mask a real churn problem.
4. **Determine how to identify suspected/at-risk churn**, via two complementary approaches: (1) human/qualitative assessment (viable at enterprise account density, harder to keep consistent as a CSM team scales, and not fiscally viable at SMB density) and (2) signal/data-driven scoring (define the attributes of your healthiest customers and your at-risk customers — usage patterns, support case volume, NPS, tenure, contract growth trend, departure of a key contact — then track them systematically, ideally via a purpose-built customer success application rather than spreadsheets).
5. **Align with executive leadership on standard definitions and reports.** Report churn/retention by every dimension that matters to the business (industry, size, tenure, geography, sales channel, product line, or individual CSM), from both a CMRR and a customer-count perspective, and make the reporting cadence and trend visibility consistent enough that leadership can see the impact of new programs as they roll out.

**Why granularity matters:** the book contrasts knowing a single top-line number (net retention of 108 percent) with knowing the underlying detail — which segment expanded, which industry churned hardest, retention/growth by product line, average discount change at first renewal, average current-vs-original contract size for tenured customers. The claim: real management competence lives in the granular detail, not the headline number.

**By touch tier:** high-touch churn analysis benefits from direct conversation — a churned customer will tell you far more in an actual conversation than a CRM drop-down field ever will. Low-touch analysis is mostly financial-mechanics work (can you track every transaction with enough granularity?), with the qualitative "why" requiring occasional surveys or hand-picked follow-up calls. Tech-touch analysis leans almost entirely on incentivized surveys of a representative customer subset, since direct conversation with the full base isn't economical.

## 17. Law 9 — Drive Customer Success through Hard Metrics

**The maturity framework borrowed and adapted: the Capability Maturity Model (CMM).** Originally developed by Carnegie Mellon's Software Engineering Institute in the late 1980s to assess software-process maturity, the book repurposes its five levels as a diagnostic for how mature a customer success organization is:

- **Level 1 — Initial.** Work happens through the heroic, ad hoc effort of committed individuals with no defined process. The typical CSM directive at this stage: "do whatever it takes to make your customers successful and make sure they renew." Works for a while on individual talent, but produces overworked people and inconsistent, uneven results.
- **Level 2 — Repeatable.** Enough process discipline exists that earlier successes can be deliberately repeated.
- **Level 3 — Defined.** The process is documented, standardized, and integrated into the organization's standard operating model.
- **Level 4 — Managed.** Active measurement of the now-defined process.
- **Level 5 — Optimizing.** Consistent, ongoing improvement driven by that measurement.

**Three categories of metrics to track once an organization is mature enough to measure and optimize:**

1. **Customer and user behavior** — every login, click, upload, download, and error in a properly instrumented SaaS product; aggregated into higher-level customer health signals (payment behavior, admin engagement, referenceability). The book's explicit caution here, paraphrasing Gainsight CEO Nick Mehta: *"no one buys your software so they can log into it."* Usage is only a proxy for business value — some objectives (e.g., "we eliminated our FTP servers") are invisible to product instrumentation and can only be confirmed by directly asking the customer.
2. **CSM activity** — frequency of interaction types (QBRs, emails, calls), support-ticket volume handled directly by CSMs, timeliness of risk identification, and effectiveness of risk-mitigation efforts. The point of tracking this is to test assumptions (are QBRs actually driving adoption? does a face-to-face visit outperform email for satisfaction?) rather than assuming any given ritual works.
3. **Business outcomes** — gross retention, net retention, expansion, logo retention, customer satisfaction, and NPS. These require cross-functional agreement (with product, marketing, sales, finance) on what "success" actually means, since ownership of these numbers is frequently shared across teams.

**The "fluffy one-on-one" failure mode.** The book contrasts the vague, unmeasurable manager check-in ("How are your customers doing? Any at risk? How can I help?") with the specific, metrics-driven one made possible by mature tooling ("your average health score is six points below the team; the dragging component is executive relationship — let's build a plan starting with your lowest-scoring account"). The claim: active management is only possible when there are clear measurables — this lets a manager "manage the results but coach the process."

**By touch tier:** for high-touch CSMs, the book proposes **net retention** as the single best lagging quality metric (because it captures both retention and upsell), paired with a sales-pipeline-style set of leading indicators — health score across book of business, health score trend, level of direct engagement, number of triggered risk actions (and how many were completed), number of upsell opportunities identified, and number of positive relationship activities (references, case studies). For low-touch CSMs managing far larger books, the same leading indicators matter even more because there's no relationship-based intuition to fall back on — survey scores, email engagement, ticket volume, and invoice punctuality become the necessary substitutes. For tech-touch, the useful comparison is to a marketing team: measure email engagement, webinar attendance, community engagement, and user-group participation, with **health score playing the same role for customer success that pipeline plays for sales** — the single best predictor of future customer behavior.

## 18. Law 10 — It's a Top-Down, Company-Wide Commitment

**The reframe of what customer success actually is at the company level:** for most of business history, companies ran on two core processes — building product and selling product. The book argues for adding a **third core process: driving customer success** — and insists this can only work as a genuine company-wide commitment, not a department's mandate.

**Every function's core question gets re-framed around customer success:**

- **Product:** which feature will truly help clients achieve their goals (versus just being demoware)?
- **Sales:** which prospects are likely to be a good long-term fit (versus quick churns)?
- **Marketing:** which messages authentically reflect delivered value (versus buzzwords)?
- **Finance:** which metrics reflect real customer value (versus just new sales)?

**Why customer success is treated as inevitable, not optional** (a causal chain): globalization and technology lowered barriers to entry → new entrants disrupt nearly every category → those entrants build lower-friction business models → lower friction makes it easy for customers to try and buy (short-term pricing, granular consumption, easy deployment) → the same low friction makes it just as easy for customers to leave → customers end up holding the power and the choice → customers with choice stay only with vendors who deliver the outcomes they want.

**Three named business benefits of embracing this early:** faster growth (less churn headwind, more upsell tailwind, since "the leaky bucket effect of churn cannot be counteracted by new business alone"), higher valuation (the book cites an Altimeter Capital report naming "dollar renewal rate" as the single most important valuation metric for subscription businesses), and competitive differentiation (because not every competitor is doing this well yet).

**A practical checklist for operationalizing top-down commitment:**

- **Define success** — crystallize, cross-functionally, what "wild success" concretely means for your customers' common use cases.
- **Align around success** — make sure every function knows its specific role (e.g., product reviews customer feedback monthly; sales qualification criteria get regularly refined; marketing messaging is reviewed jointly with customer success).
- **Listen to the customer success team** — give the customer success executive a seat and a voice equal to the sales leader's in every executive and board meeting.
- **Prioritize customer success** — when trade-offs must be made (a delight feature vs. a demo feature; CSM training vs. sales training), actually let customer success win sometimes.
- **Empower the customer success team** — give the CSM leader a title on par with the sales leader; loop CSMs in on escalations; let the CSM be the one who delivers good news to the customer; make clear across the org that the CSM speaks for the client's interests.
- **Measure customer success** — agree on bottom-line metrics (gross churn, net retention) and early-warning metrics (health score, adoption score, NPS).
- **Report on customer success** — put the metrics on the walls, in all-hands meetings, and in the board deck with the same prominence as sales numbers.
- **Incent toward customer success** — build net retention, NPS, or health score into company-wide bonus plans.
- **Challenge the company** — push as hard on customer success targets as on quarterly sales targets.
- **Celebrate success** — build the same visible rituals (gongs, recognition) for customer success wins that most companies already build for sales wins.

**A five-question CEO self-test** offered to gauge whether "top-down commitment" is real or rhetorical:

1. Would he say no to an above-average-deal-size prospect in the pipeline because the odds of making them truly successful are too low?
2. Would he delay a planned product release to address a current customer's problem?
3. Is the head of customer success genuinely in his circle of trust?
4. Does the roadmap include items that won't help sell more but will address existing customer needs?
5. Does he personally get involved in critical customer situations as often as he does in key sales deals?

**By touch tier:** this law is explicitly described as not varying by touch model — commitment from the board and CEO, and cross-functional alignment, matter identically regardless of how customers are served. What does vary is the specific *trade-off* being weighed: in high touch, it's hiring another CSM versus another quota-carrying rep; in low touch, it's shrinking a CSM's book of business versus raising sales quotas; in tech touch, it's building a customer portal versus upgrading marketing automation. A story about Marc Benioff scrapping an entire agenda of Salesforce product presentations before the first Dreamforce conference — replacing them with an open microphone specifically inviting customers to say what was *wrong* with the product, the process, and the people — is used as the book's example of what genuine top-down commitment looks like in practice.

## 19. The Chief Customer Officer (CCO)

**The "Before Cloud" CCO** existed at some companies mainly to make a symbolic statement about customer importance, and typically owned overall customer experience (CX) without any direct operational or revenue accountability. The book's illustrative example: a CCO who improves an invoicing process (a genuine customer-experience win) has no clean way to tie that improvement to revenue, profitability, or expansion — making the investment in the role hard to defend to a board focused on measurable outcomes. CX in this era typically lived inside marketing and was measured mainly through satisfaction surveys.

**The New CCO** emerged directly from the rise of subscription/recurring revenue and now typically owns direct, measurable revenue responsibility (retention and expansion), which gives the role real standing at the executive table. The book traces customer success's most common organizational "home" before it consolidates under a CCO:

- Often born inside sales (since sales frequently already owns the early renewal quota).
- Often born inside a broader services organization (shared skills with consulting/support).
- Sometimes born inside marketing.
- Occasionally born inside product (since the product team most needs to hear from CSMs directly).

**Five distinct post-sales organizations, each with a different primary success measurement — the book insists managing all five identically is a mistake:**

- **Professional Services** — measured by **utilization**: of all billable hours available, how many were actually billed.
- **Training** — measured by **number of products/courses delivered** (or, alternatively, number of customers/users trained); run essentially like a product-development team (build, then sell/deliver a training "product").
- **Customer Support** — measured by **efficiency**: tickets closed per rep per day, total calls handled. A cost center, and expected to remain one; the goal is minimizing cost per customer, not generating revenue.
- **Implementation/Onboarding** — measured by **time-to-value** (or time-to-project-completion). Frequently split out from professional services specifically because packaging fixed-price onboarding (versus hourly professional services billing) and rigorously tracking speed-to-completion matter enough to deserve separate management.
- **Customer Success** — measured by **retention** (net retention, renewal rate, or an equivalent umbrella metric of "loyalty"). Described as the organization that "glues the other groups together" — questions too big for support, consulting needs too small to justify an engagement, and post-training reinforcement all land in customer success, which in turn pushes the other four organizations to improve.

**The case for absorbing adjacent functions into the CCO's org, and why it happens (or doesn't):**

- **Renewal/upsell sales** — a strong case for moving this *inside* customer success: it protects the CSM's "trusted adviser" status from being compromised by also being the one negotiating price; it gives the CEO "one throat to choke" for the net retention number; and CSMs are the best-positioned people to arm a renewal/upsell rep with account history and expansion leads.
- **Customer marketing** (email nurturing, reference management, community, webinars, user groups, customer summit) — increasingly drawn toward customer success because the content, timing, and targeting of every one of those programs depends on customer-health data that customer success already owns.
- **Sales consulting** (the function that approves new releases for the sales org to use in a customer context) — the most novel and contested proposed move: give ownership of release-readiness sign-off to the organization that will suffer the most pain from a bad release, i.e., customer success, on the logic that this is directly analogous to giving quality assurance ownership to the team that has to live with production defects.

The overarching argument: as recurring revenue businesses mature, the dollar value of the *existing* customer base's bookings comes to dwarf new-business bookings (the book illustrates this with a quota-comparison chart showing a CCO's effective book-of-business quota overtaking an SVP of Sales's new-business quota within a handful of years) — which is the underlying reason the CCO role's scope, and its claim on adjacent functions, keeps expanding.

## 20. Customer Success Technology — Five Value Propositions

The book frames all customer success technology (generically, a "customer success management" platform) around five distinct value propositions, deliberately drawing a repeated analogy to how CRM systems transformed sales:

1. **Optimize CSM time.** In the absence of good tooling, prioritization defaults to two blunt signals: renewal date and ARR/contract value. Real prioritization requires either product-usage data or aggregated cross-system interaction data (support, billing, survey, marketing engagement) pulled into one place — replacing a vague "we should check on our biggest accounts" instinct with a specific, ranked, reason-coded action list (e.g., "17 customers with usage down 20%+ and a negative last survey score — start with the four renewing this quarter").
2. **Increase the intelligence of every customer touch.** The alternative to a scripted, generic check-in call ("just calling to see how things are going") is a call informed by real context (recent webinar attendance, recent support tickets on a specific topic). This value proposition extends well beyond the CSM team — support reps, product managers, professional services, sales, marketing, and executives all benefit from the same **"360-degree view of the customer,"** a phrase the book notes CRM systems have long claimed but rarely delivered on without heavy manual data entry.
3. **Drive scalability.** With only two real levers to manage a growing customer base — more people, or more technology — a properly deployed customer success solution is framed as capable of a 25–30 percent minimum productivity improvement (measured in accounts or dollars managed per person), enabling levers like better prioritization, more effective calls, easier collaboration, broader accessibility of key data, and cheaper proactivity relative to firefighting.
4. **Improve collaboration, communication, and visibility.** The book explicitly argues the core unsolved problem in customer success (as in CRM/sales) is *not* an analytics problem — plenty of excellent analytics tools already exist — but a **workflow** problem. Direct CRM analogy: Salesforce's core original value wasn't analytics, it was sales-force automation delivering four outcomes — predictability, forecastability, repeatability, and visibility — for the sales funnel. A customer success platform is argued to deliver the identical four outcomes for the *post-sale* funnel (retention, expansion, churn) instead of the pre-sale one.
5. **Better team management.** Just as a CRM system became the primary tool a sales VP uses to coach reps (tracking calls, meetings, pipeline movement, proposals, ASP), a mature customer success platform becomes the primary tool a customer success VP uses to coach CSMs (tracking calls, meetings, triggered actions and their completion, QBRs, renewals/upsell results, health score, satisfaction score, email engagement, account plans). This is also framed as the mechanism by which customer success earns durable executive credibility — headcount requests justified by "everyone's really busy" don't survive scrutiny, but headcount requests backed by quantified account-load and outcome data do.

## 21. The Future of Customer Success and the Customer Economy

**Twenty predicted directions of change**, offered as a forward-looking checklist for how the discipline will mature:

1. Customer success will keep growing beyond SaaS into every industry.
2. The CCO title/role will continue to spread.
3. The discipline itself will become more defined and refined.
4. Customer success orgs will become more operationally focused and less relationship-dependent.
5. Businesses will get better at quantifying customer success's value.
6. Demand for customer success talent will continue to outstrip supply.
7. Universities will start teaching customer success formally.
8. Customer success technology will shift from nice-to-have to must-have.
9. CEOs who came up through customer success will become common.
10. Customer success operations will become as standard a function as sales operations.
11. "One-to-many CSM" will emerge as a distinct, valued job title.
12. Customer success meetups/gatherings will become less novel simply because so many more people will be in the field.
13. More large players (and start-ups) will enter the customer success technology market.
14. A dominant customer success technology vendor will emerge and IPO.
15. VPs of customer success will become regular fixtures at IPO bell-ringings.
16. Public-company earnings calls will routinely foreground retention and customer success.
17. "Customer success" will make a mainstream business-press cover story.
18. Overall churn rates will not necessarily fall, because the ease of switching vendors keeps rising roughly as fast as customer success practice improves.
19. Major management consulting firms will build formal customer success practices.
20. More books will be written on the subject.

**The customer economy.** The book extends Tien Tzuo's term "the subscription economy" into a broader claim: **"the customer economy"** — the argument that customer success is a *secondary* wave riding behind a larger *primary* wave (the shift to recurring-revenue business models generally), and that the primary wave itself is still building, not cresting — comparable to a tsunami that grows in height as it nears shore. The claim extends the disruption logic beyond software to any industry where information asymmetry between vendor and customer collapses (taxis disrupted by Uber/Lyft, hotels disrupted by Airbnb are the named examples) — every such disruption favors the customer, and the customer, in aggregate, always wins that fight.

**An "ideal" modern customer success journey (the Wingtip/Financiality illustration).** The book walks through a detailed fictional 5-month new-customer timeline (a mid-market SaaS training company, Wingtip, onboarding a customer, Financiality) to show what a well-instrumented low-touch experience actually looks like in granular practice: automated CRM-to-CSM-system handoff on contract signature, an automated personalized welcome email from the CEO, an automated small thank-you gift triggered by deal closure, an onboarding SLA that gets flagged "at risk" the moment the kickoff is delayed, milestone-triggered introduction of the assigned CSM partway through onboarding, rule-based health score creation at go-live, an onboarding-satisfaction survey automatically triggered by the go-live milestone, an NPS survey automatically triggered 90 days post-signature, automated "getting started" nudges triggered by low page-view counts in the first week, small automated reward triggers at usage thresholds (an electronic gift card at 50 logins/500 pageviews), an automated alert when support-ticket volume spikes, and a first executive business review at the 90-day mark. The explicit point of walking through this level of granular detail: the first-year experience described is not "future" technology — it is fully achievable today — and the biggest churn risk window is the first renewal or the first months of the relationship if there's no formal renewal event, which is why the earliest weeks deserve this density of instrumented care.

**Starbucks as a non-B2B, "customer success without the name" case study.** Six characteristics the book identifies as customer success in disguise: ubiquity of locations, free Wi-Fi everywhere, consistently comfortable seating, zero pressure to buy or to buy more, consistently friendly staff (sometimes remembering names/orders), and identical products at identical prices in every location (airports excepted). These build **behavioral** loyalty (convenience) which compounds into **attitudinal** loyalty (genuine attachment) — reinforced further by the Starbucks Card/mobile-payment loyalty program (launched November 2001; 4 million cards activated within eight months), which the book highlights for two mechanics: automatic balance reload (removing the "conscious expenditure" moment that might otherwise trigger reconsideration) and a rewards structure ("bonus stars" on every purchase, a free drink every twelve purchases and on your birthday) that functions like an airline frequent-flyer program, making it progressively harder to rationally justify switching to a competitor even though coffee itself is close to a commodity good. The closing distinction drawn from the Starbucks case: customer *service* would only mean the drink was correct and timely; going further — creating a place to linger, free infrastructure, no purchase pressure, personal recognition — is what elevates it into customer *success*, and it is this elevation, not the product category, that the book argues determines whether a business earns attitudinal loyalty at all.
