# Uncommon Service — Framework Reference

*Pure framework reference distilled from the book. No commentary, no application notes — just the frameworks, models, and mental tools as the author presents them.*

*Source: Uncommon Service by Frances Frei & Anne Morriss*

---

## 1. The Core Equation — Service Excellence = Design × Culture

Frei and Morriss's foundational claim: uncommon service is not born from attitude and effort, but from design choices made in the blueprints of a business model. It's easy to put service into a mission statement; what's hard is designing a service model that lets average employees, not just exceptional ones, produce service excellence as an everyday routine. Outstanding service organizations create offerings, funding strategies, systems, and cultures that set their people up to excel *casually*.

The book's master formula:

Service Excellence = Design × Culture

The two factors are weighted equally, which allows for some wiggle room: a stronger culture can partly make up for a weaker design, and vice versa. But if either one is neglected, excellence is definitively out of reach — it's a multiplication, not an addition, so a zero on either side of the equation zeroes out the whole. Design is the operating model itself: the offering, the funding mechanism, the employee management system, and the customer management system. Culture is the invisible layer that animates that design — how people actually behave inside it, day to day, when no one is watching.

The book is organized around four "truths" that define the design half of the equation, followed by a chapter on culture as the multiplier, and a final chapter on how to scale both without losing them:

1. You can't be good at everything.
2. Someone has to pay for it.
3. It's not your employees' fault.
4. You must manage your customers.

The practical implication of framing it as an equation rather than a checklist: getting the service model "right" is only half the challenge. A brilliant service design deployed into an indifferent or misaligned culture wastes the design; a beautiful culture without a coherent design has nothing to reinforce. Both sides have to be built, and both have to point in the same direction.

## 2. Truth Number 1: You Can't Be Good at Everything

The central, most emotionally difficult idea in the book: in services, trying to do everything brilliantly leads almost inevitably to mediocrity. Excellence requires sacrifice. To deliver great service on the dimensions customers value most, an organization must underperform, deliberately, on the dimensions customers value least. This means having the stomach to do some things *badly* — not casually bad, but bad in the service of great.

Commerce Bank is the book's primary illustration. Founder Vernon Hill built a bank that offered the worst deposit rates in the industry, made very few acquisitions, and yet became the fastest-growing retail bank in America (a 2,000 percent stock price rise in the 1990s). It won by deciding very strategically where to excel (hours: open seven days a week, until midnight on Fridays, full service on weekends — "America's Most Convenient Bank") and by being explicit about what would fund that excellence (the worst deposit rates in every local market). Convenient hours and low rates were inextricably linked: Commerce could deliver excellence in hours *because of* its dismal deposit rates, not despite them.

Walmart follows the same pattern: low prices and broad selection at the top of its customers' priority list, funded by deliberately harsh lighting and sparse sales support at the bottom. Walmart's leadership didn't decide *not* to have store ambiance — it decided to let ambiance be *worse* than competitors', on purpose, as a funding source for the attributes that mattered more.

The framework names the core emotional obstacle explicitly: **the number one obstacle to great service is not the ambition to be great, but the stomach to be bad.** Choosing bad feels like an assault on the soul, particularly in mission-driven fields (health care, education) where managers feel a moral duty to at least *try* on every dimension. The book's counter: choosing bad is your only shot at greatness, and resisting it is a recipe for mediocrity. Charismatic leaders sometimes try to avoid the trade-off by sheer force of personality — get everyone fired up and the kinks will work themselves out — but a system can't be built on the assumption that every employee will perform heroically, every day, indefinitely. Heroism is a red flag: an employee whose superhuman effort covers for a broken design lets the organization avoid confronting the real problem, the money it's leaving on the table, and the risk of that one special employee leaving.

A physical-world analogy is used to make the trade-off feel less arbitrary: the **impossible triangle** from construction — a customer can expect two of speed, quality, and price, never all three. Volvo's engineers choose safety over sporty; Zara's designers choose low-cost materials over durable ones. People working within physical constraints accept trade-offs easily and take pride in the discipline of honoring them. Service managers, by contrast, often behave as though the laws of physics don't apply to them, setting a goal of being simultaneously the lowest-cost, highest-quality, fastest-moving provider — which breeds cynicism among frontline employees who confront the trade-offs daily while management denies reality exists.

## 3. The Attribute Map — Diagnosing Where to Excel and Where to Be Bad

The book's core diagnostic tool for turning "you can't be good at everything" into an actual decision. An attribute map has two axes: the vertical axis lists the distinct elements of the service offering, ranked in descending order of importance *according to the customer* (not the company); the horizontal axis charts relative performance (typically 1–5) of your own firm and of competitors on each attribute, again from the customer's perspective.

The exercise is done twice, once internally and once externally:

**Step 1: Create an internal attribute map.** Assemble a group of five to seven people using "diagonal representation" — a deliberate mix across business units and hierarchy levels (operations and sales, frontline and back office, high and low decision rights). Ask the group to channel the target market's most important customers and brainstorm every attribute of the service experience relevant to them (the resulting "cloud" of attributes). Then force the group to rank-order the cloud from most to least important to the customer. Real disagreement at this stage is valuable data, not a problem to smooth over — it reveals how divergent an organization's internal beliefs about its customers really are, and how little empirical evidence usually backs those beliefs. If consensus can't be reached, use a simple vote (top three priorities, checkmark method).

A related diagnostic distinction surfaces here: **marketing segments** (how you identify and communicate with different kinds of customers) versus **operating segments** (how you classify customers by their service priorities — literally, the different orderings of attributes you're serving). The two frequently don't map one-to-one; a single marketing segment (e.g., a preschool's target demographic of connection-and-curiosity-focused families) can contain multiple distinct operating segments with very different service needs (working parents vs. stay-at-home parents), each requiring its own attribute map.

A companion test for defining what counts as one attribute versus several: if you break apart what looks like a single attribute (e.g., "convenience" into "location nearby" and "open 24 hours") and rank the pieces separately, do they land sequentially on the list with consistent relative performance versus competitors? If yes, collapse them into one attribute. If they scatter widely apart in the ranking, or performance diverges across the pieces, keep them separate.

**Step 2: Create an external attribute map.** Bring in actual customers, because they rarely rank their own priorities well if simply asked to list 1 to 15. The recommended method is a lightweight, qualitative version of **conjoint analysis** — the formal technique of presenting customers with paired attribute trade-offs (e.g., satellite radio or four-wheel drive?) to reveal preferences indirectly, since people often can't articulate rankings directly but can reveal them through forced comparisons. Full conjoint analysis is expensive and slow, and it's only as good as the attribute list fed into it; the book recommends open, adaptive conversations with a random sample of customers instead — random specifically because a convergence of opinion across randomly selected people carries far more signal than convergence within a hand-picked group. The organizing question used with Ochsner Health System patients: "Under what circumstances do you use us versus a competitor?" — a question aimed at surfacing what actually drives the customer's choice, not what they say they value in the abstract. This surfaced four distinct Ochsner operating segments, defined by two variables: complexity of care (high risk / low risk) and urgency (planned / unplanned) — a reminder that operating segments are often discovered mid-process, not assumed in advance.

**Step 3: Analyze your performance.** Once attributes are ranked and rated for your firm and competitors, the resulting chart typically produces one of two shapes, each with a name in the book:

- **Option A** — excelling on some attributes, performing poorly on others, in a pattern deliberately aligned with the customer's own priority order.
- **Option B** — trying to be uniformly average-to-good across every attribute, settling for "less across the board."

Option B is emotionally easier and less individually risky — you don't need deep customer understanding to sustain it, and it survives even if customer preferences shift. But it leaves you stuck in mediocrity. Option A requires real courage (choosing exactly where to be great and where to be bad, with real cost if you choose wrong) but is the only route to genuine excellence. The one legitimate escape from this trade-off is a large enough price premium to fund excellence across every dimension simultaneously — the Hotel Cipriani model, which charges roughly 50 percent more than its nearest competitor to be a 5-out-of-5 on everything. Very few organizations operate in a market that will tolerate that premium.

## 4. Redefining Value — Rearranging Customer Preferences

A second, more radical route to service excellence beyond simply meeting customers' existing priority order: don't just respond to customer preferences, actively **rearrange** them. If you can't win within a market's existing rules, change the rules by pushing established attributes down the customer's priority list and pushing their inverse up.

IKEA is the defining case. Before IKEA, physical durability and timeless design topped furniture buyers' priority lists; furniture was a lifetime investment, purchased once and passed to children. IKEA's flat-pack, self-assembled, disposable furniture violated every one of those priorities and, on a conventional attribute map, would have scored a 1 out of 5 on durability, ease of assembly, sales assistance, and location. Rather than accept that scorecard, IKEA rewrote the list of attributes furniture buyers cared about: the ability to change your furniture whenever you want, the fun of the shopping-and-assembly project, independence, and the store as a destination. Durability wasn't just deprioritized — it was reframed as a liability ("commitment-free" furniture, "It's just furniture. Change it.").

The mechanism IKEA used to make this reversal palatable rather than depressing is named **"Low Price with Meaning"**: making the purchase and use of inexpensive goods feel like fun rather than deprivation. Concretely: playful, bright, modern stores; free on-site childcare; a full-service restaurant with real food, not vending machines; tasteful, well-designed products despite the low price point (so customers don't feel cheap, they feel clever). The insight behind why this works, credited to case-writer Youngme Moon: "it's really hard to get mad at someone who gives you an unexpected delight" — the goodwill generated by small surprises carries over and neutralizes resentment about the parts of the experience that are genuinely inferior (a half-day spent assembling a dresser).

The general two-step method for redefining value, as the book frames it: first, push well-established attributes downward on the customer's priority list; second, push the inverse of those same attributes upward, and be willing to broadcast former liabilities as selling points.

## 5. Truth Number 2: Someone Has to Pay for It — Four Funding Mechanisms

Delivering on Truth Number 1 costs money. Without a reliable, designed-in funding mechanism, an organization risks delivering what the book calls **gratuitous service** — service niceties donated to customers with little chance of ever recovering the cost (Celebrity Cruises' "150 Tastes of Luxury" program is the opening cautionary example: customers loved the champagne and sushi cafés, but the cruise-booking channel made it nearly impossible to convert delight into a price premium).

Four funding mechanisms exist, and only one of them requires charging premium prices:

1. **Charge customers extra for it — in a palatable way.**
2. **Make cost reductions that also improve service.**
3. **Make service improvements that also reduce costs.**
4. **Get customers to do the work for you.**

The book's own advice on sequencing: Method 1 is the simplest from a design perspective, but most markets won't tolerate the price premium required. Methods 2 and 3 are the most reliable route for most organizations. Method 4 gets the most attention but is the hardest to execute well. In consulting engagements, the authors say they typically skip past brainstorming higher prices (clients know their own market's tolerance better than any outside advisor) and jump straight to the three creative funding strategies.

## 6. Funding Source 1 — Palatable Pricing, and True Loyalty Programs

Charging more only works if the charge is **palatable** — a term the book uses precisely: the less palatable a price feels, the more irritated customers get over every penny extracted, regardless of whether they're objectively better off. Commerce Bank's funding source (a rate a half-point lower on deposits) was structured so customers pay for the service invisibly, every day, rather than being charged per teller visit — an equivalent-cost alternative that customers would perceive as unfair even if it were cheaper for them overall. Starbucks bakes the expectation that you'll linger for hours into the price of the latte; it is an explicit, transparent value exchange, just one without a visible meter.

**What determines palatability is the contract you originally set with the customer, not the size of the fee.** Southwest's brand promise ("LUV") makes small added fees feel like a betrayal, akin to a parent charging rent for your childhood bedroom; discount carriers like Spirit Air and easyJet set the opposite contract upfront ("we're practically giving you the flight, so suck it up"), which makes even aggressive à la carte fees feel fair, provided they're disclosed before the purchase decision, not after (Spirit Air's high complaint volume in 2008 came specifically from post-purchase fee surprises, not from the fees themselves). The same logic explains why Four Seasons charging $19.99 for hotel Wi-Fi, or US Airways charging for a checked bag on a $1,000 ticket, generates outsized backlash: the underlying price point already implies a higher standard of care, so a fee that would be trivial at a discount brand reads as a violation at a premium one.

A named distinction the book insists on: **true loyalty programs vs. retention programs.** A true loyalty program invests in your best customers in ways that *increase their willingness to pay a premium price* — inviting them into strategic decisions, giving them access to leadership, treating them as co-producers of the offering (Asda's program, which lets loyal shoppers weigh in on product selection and store layout, and shares 5 percent of any customer-suggested savings with the customer who proposed it, is the book's clean example). A retention program, by contrast, simply pays customers, via discounts, to keep transacting with you — most "loyalty cards" (the book names Tesco) are retention programs mislabeled as loyalty programs. The mislabeling matters because it can crowd out the harder, more valuable work of building a real one.

## 7. Funding Sources 2 and 3 — Cost/Service Reinforcing Loops

**Funding Source 2: cost reductions that also improve service.** The insurance industry example: Progressive Insurance dispatches immediate-response claims vans to accident scenes, arriving faster than police or tow trucks, settling many claims on the spot. This is expensive — but Progressive discovered it was fighting the industry's two biggest cost buckets (fraud and litigation) at the same time: adjusters on-scene catch fraud in real time (internally nicknamed "fraud-busters"), and the simple act of an adjuster asking "are you OK?" measurably reduces a customer's likelihood of hiring a lawyer, because it humanizes the insurer and defuses anger. Fraud, disputed claims, and legal fees eat roughly $15 of every $100 in premiums industry-wide; cutting that bucket funds the visible service improvement. The prescribed sequence for this mechanism: **start with your biggest cost buckets, then work backward to a service improvement that attacks them.**

Progressive extended the same logic into a **customer-selection tool disguised as a service feature**: its "comparison quote," which shows the customer Progressive's price next to competitors' prices. Because Progressive is only the cheapest about half the time, the tool actively routes away the highest-risk, most price-sensitive customers (who are undercharged everywhere and would be unprofitable to Progressive) while retaining low-risk customers Progressive can price accurately and profitably. The tool looks like generosity; operationally it's underwriting discipline.

**Funding Source 3: service improvements that also reduce costs.** Intuit's founder-era decision to staff phone support with the product-development engineers who actually built the software (rather than an outsourced, scripted call center) is the clean example: customers got better support, and the company got a direct, high-fidelity feedback loop that made subsequent product versions more intuitive, which in turn reduced total call volume and support cost over time. FedEx applied the same logic to its "where's my package" problem: moving customers to online tracking, then to *proactive* automated status emails/texts, eliminated the need to call in the first place, cutting cost while measurably improving the customer experience — including "exception reporting" that proactively flags late packages before the customer has to ask.

Zappos treats its entire service-cost structure as a marketing line item rather than an operating expense: free two-way shipping, surprise overnight-shipping upgrades, and running its warehouse 24/7 (a deliberately *inefficient* choice, since batching orders would be cheaper) are funded by redirecting money that would otherwise go to paid advertising, on the logic that word-of-mouth from delighted customers is a more efficient acquisition channel than ads. CFO Alfred Lin's framing: "this money is fungible."

## 8. Funding Source 4 — Self-Service That Customers Prefer

The oldest and most commonly misapplied funding mechanism. Self-service saves the company labor cost, but it only counts as *service excellence* — as opposed to a naked cost-shift — if customers genuinely **prefer** the self-service option to a *readily available* full-service alternative. Comparing self-service to a bad full-service alternative (a 20-minute staffed checkout line) is an invalid test; the comparison has to be against a good one.

**The airline kiosk vs. the grocery self-checkout** is the book's central contrast, used as a shorthand: "think airline kiosk check-in, not supermarket checkout." Airline check-in kiosks succeed because they give the customer *more* control and information than the full-service alternative (a visual seat map beats a phone agent's black-box terminal) and because the underlying system was intentionally redesigned to be simple for an untrained customer to operate, not merely exposed as-is. Grocery self-checkout fails the same test on nearly every count: it hands customers the *same or more* work a trained employee used to do (finding barcodes, bagging without breaking eggs, sometimes even weighing items for fraud-detection purposes), with no compensation for the added labor and no improvement in speed or control — which is why customers visibly sweat through it rather than embrace it. The tell that a company hasn't actually improved anything: ask how much extra you charge for self-service. If the answer is "nothing — if anything we discount it," the self-service model hasn't reached excellence yet; it's still a pure cost-shift.

**The real design target: self-service so good that customers would pay *more* for it.** Fire and Ice, a restaurant chain where diners select and watch their own entrée being grilled, competes on the experience of self-direction, not on lower prices — customers are charged for the privilege of meeting their own needs, not compensated for the labor. Ochsner Health System's patient portal (self-service prescription refills, lab results, and appointment scheduling) drove patient satisfaction from roughly 50 percent to the 90-percent range and cut no-show rates in half, entirely without a discount, because it increased the patient's sense of agency and control rather than simply outsourcing work to them.

**Avoiding the free-rider trap when investing in pre-purchase self-service.** Gateway's showroom strategy — let customers get a full, high-touch education from expensive in-store staff, then let them walk out and buy the equivalent Dell online for less — illustrates the risk: when pre-purchase information has high utility and products are easy to substitute, an investment in service excellence can walk across the street to a lower-cost competitor. Amazon avoids the same fate despite carrying no price advantage and fully substitutable products, because it engineered the *purchase* step (one-click ordering, stored payment and shipping information) to be so frictionless that customers who found what they wanted on Amazon rarely bother price-shopping elsewhere before buying — the self-service investment pays for itself because switching at the moment of purchase, not just at the moment of research, has been made inconvenient.

## 9. Truth Number 3: It's Not Your Employees' Fault — The Successful Employee Management System

The reframe: when service is consistently disappointing, or even excellent but only in fits and starts ("episodic excellence"), the instinctive diagnosis — that the company somehow hired an entire staff of people who "just don't get it" — is almost always wrong. The real cause is a **try-harder solution**: since one employee occasionally performs brilliantly (often *despite* the system, not because of it), management concludes the missing ingredient is individual effort, and pushes everyone to try harder. This fails because (1) most companies have a real continuum of talent on payroll, not a roster of superheroes, and few can systematically afford to hire only the best; and (2) the job itself is usually getting harder over time as operational complexity creeps in unnoticed (a quick-service restaurant chain's menu grew from 25 to over 100 items in a few years, and the number of ways to ring up its signature drink hit 55).

The book's structural fix, modeled on Bugs Burger Bug Killers (BBBK), a pest-elimination company that guaranteed complete pest *elimination* (not mere control) and charged 4–10x the industry rate for it: build an **employee management system** with four integrated, mutually reinforcing components.

- **Selection.** Know precisely which traits you're screening for, and build a process that reliably surfaces them. BBBK recruited exactly two profiles — perfectionists and aspiring managers who thrived on excellence — using multi-round interviews that included family members (given the lifestyle demands of night-shift mobile work), with only 2–3 percent of applicants advancing. Southwest's group interview technique (asking one candidate to describe an embarrassing moment while recruiters quietly watch the *other* candidates' reactions) screens for empathy, the trait Southwest considers the actual driver of good service at 30,000 feet.
- **Training.** BBBK put new hires through five months of training (versus an industry norm of days to weeks) at a cost of roughly $15,000 per specialist in 1980s dollars. Training does double duty: building technical skill and imprinting culture. Some companies use training itself as a self-selection filter — Commerce Bank's theatrical first ninety seconds of orientation ("You're all wack jobs!" followed by a group "Wow!" phone-answering drill) deliberately scares off recruits who aren't wired for relentless friendliness, at zero net cost since they haven't been trained yet. Zappos runs every new hire, from C-suite to call-center, through the same four-week program and offers $2,000 at the midpoint to quit with no penalty — a filter for anyone who values quick cash over long-term cultural fit.
- **Job design.** The inverse strategy to heavy training: design the job to be so intuitive that untrained people can do it well on day one. LSQ Funding Group automated the technical, risk-analysis-heavy core of the factoring business into an intuitive IT system, which let it hire almost entirely for attitude and cultural fit rather than technical background, and achieve under-5-percent turnover in an industry known for high churn. Commerce Bank, Southwest (one aircraft type, no interline baggage transfers), and Zappos (call-center reps monitor only three screens, not the industry-standard eight) all simplified job design deliberately to match real, average employees rather than an idealized superstar.
- **Performance management.** Carrots and sticks — including scripts and checklists — that make it easy to do the job well and hard to do it badly. BBBK combined unusually generous incentives (a 20 percent cut of route billings, profit sharing, bonuses) with a demand for total transparency: specialists had to report every detail of every visit, a manager independently called the customer afterward to verify it, and a roving quality-control team made unannounced visits. The explicit cultural rule: "mistakes were forgiven; liars were not." Performance management should be customized, not borrowed wholesale from industry norms — Spence Diamonds pays the highest salaries in its industry but locks even veteran, highly-paid salespeople into a rigid 70-page sales script, because CEO Sean Jones found scripted reps were six times more likely to close, and because the script was engineered around a specific psychological insight (the typical engagement-ring buyer is terrified, not just uncertain). Money is also not assumed to be the most powerful lever — recognition, status, and purpose matter enormously (Commerce Bank's peer-given "Great Job" stickers; the Cleveland Clinic's salaried-not-fee-for-service physician model).

The four components must be **internally consistent with each other and with the rest of the service model** — there is no universally "good" or "bad" selection strategy, only strategies that are or aren't aligned with the rest of the system. Verizon's DSL call-center failure (importing the highly-scripted, repetitive-task hiring profile that worked perfectly for its legacy long-distance call centers) and the fix (hiring instead for comfort with ambiguity, once the company realized DSL support required *learning*, not execution) is the book's case study in what happens when one component of the system is misaligned with the job's actual demands.

## 10. Should You Invest in the Best? — The Competence-Complexity Gap

A companion diagnostic to the employee management system, for organizations wondering whether their service problems are a talent problem or a design problem.

**The attitude/aptitude cost curve.** Employees who are high on both attitude and aptitude are the most desirable hires in any labor market, and therefore the most expensive — often up to twice the cost of employees who excel on only one dimension. Since almost no organization can afford to staff exclusively with this quadrant, most service designs have to be built for a realistic mix of talent, not an idealized one.

**The competence-complexity gap.** Plot two lines over time for any given job: the operational complexity actually experienced by the frontline employee, and the sophistication of the employees you're hiring and training for that job. In many organizations the complexity line has crept steadily upward (new products, new regulations, new systems layered on for years) while the employee-sophistication line has stayed flat or even declined — producing a widening gap that shows up as customer-facing failure, however capable individual employees might be. A telecom company case cited in the book: systems had grown so complex that it took thirteen months for an average employee to reach proficiency, while average tenure was only nine months — customer satisfaction cratered to 30 percent, driven by structural mismatch, not employee quality.

**Two diagnostic tools to size the gap:**

- *Go undercover* (roughly one to two days): physically do an average employee's job, or at minimum sit with employees and ask candidly what makes the work hard, easy, or has changed over time.
- *Chart complexity over time* (roughly fifteen minutes): plot the two lines described above; a widening gap is the signal to act.

**Two remedies, and how to sequence them:** (1) change the people (hire or train differently) or (2) change the job (redesign it to fit the people you actually have). When system complexity itself is the target, the book recommends starting with **high-complexity, low-value-added** processes — the ones adding operational burden without improving the customer's experience at all (the 55 ways to ring up one drink; cut those first, since none of them make the drink better). Only after that cleanup should an organization tackle complexity that *is* adding real customer value, which is harder because internal buy-in is scarcer and customers need to be buffered from the transition. A structural option for closing the gap without simplifying the whole job: break a complex job into smaller, specialized tasks distributed across several employees (the hospital model — one person takes blood pressure, another does anesthesia, another performs surgery), paired with strong hand-off systems and IT that captures shared information centrally, since specialization introduces its own hand-off risk.

## 11. Truth Number 4: You Must Manage Your Customers — The Customer-Operator Concept

The book's most distinctive idea: customers are not passive recipients of a service; they are active participants in *producing* it, a role the book names the **customer-operator**. A customer who takes five minutes to decide on a fry order, blocks a boarding aisle, gives a consulting firm vague requirements, or shows up late for a reservation is not merely consuming service — they are degrading the throughput and quality of the system for themselves and for everyone behind them.

The provocative framing used to make the implication concrete: **customers function like your unpaid, untrained, unmotivated employees.** They work for you in the same operational sense your staff does, but with none of the levers you'd normally use to manage a workforce — no contract, no paycheck, no career-advancement incentive, no rigorous hiring filter (you generally serve whoever shows up), and vastly greater numbers. On the upside, customer-operators require no salary or benefits, arrive exactly when service is demanded, and hold insight into their own individual preferences that no amount of market research could replicate.

**Five types of customer variability**, each requiring a different management response:

- *Arrival* — customers don't want service at the same time (grocery evening rush, Dunkin' Donuts 8 a.m. line).
- *Request* — not everyone orders the same thing (every ad-agency client runs a unique campaign).
- *Capability* — customers differ in knowledge, skill, and resources (a patient's ability to describe symptoms accurately).
- *Effort* — customers choose how much work to put in (auditors' clients handing over disorganized files; shoppers not returning carts).
- *Preference* — even customers wanting "the same" service define quality differently (one diner likes a server's first-name introduction, another resents the presumed intimacy); preference variability multiplies the effect of every other type.

**Two strategic responses to variability: reduction and accommodation**, held in permanent tension. Reduction favors efficiency — menus and reservations are classic reduction tools, limiting customer options in exchange for predictability, at a real cost to the customer who wanted something off-menu or at an inconvenient hour. Accommodation favors service — building slack into the system and keeping a bench of experienced employees able to adapt to whatever variability walks in — but is expensive, and usually requires a customer willing to pay a premium for the flexibility. A third strategic option, illustrated by Dell outsourcing on-site repair calls to third-party service networks (including competitors), is to hand the variability off entirely to an organization better equipped to absorb it.

## 12. The Successful Customer Management System

Mirroring the employee management system almost component for component, applied to customers instead of staff. Shouldice Hospital, a hernia-surgery specialist built on "early ambulation" (getting patients up and moving almost immediately after surgery, with a recurrence rate of roughly 1 percent versus 6 percent industry-wide), is the primary case.

- **Customer selection.** Shouldice narrows its patient population deliberately: candidates must be otherwise healthy, within a defined weight range (with a supervised weight-loss path offered if only moderately over), and willing to embrace the get-up-and-go recovery philosophy — patients who don't meet the criteria by the scheduled date are turned away. The book draws the analogy to elite university admissions: narrowing the input population (rather than accepting anyone and coping with the resulting variability) is what makes an extremely precise, efficient operating model possible in the first place. Progressive Insurance's comparison-quote tool performs the same customer-selection function under the guise of a value-added service (see Section 7).
- **Customer training.** Even well-selected customers often need training to perform their role well, and the training has to be dressed up to be palatable — customers don't like feeling schooled. Shouldice's incoming "class" of roughly thirty patients arrives the day before surgery, receives a formal evening orientation, eats together, and mingles that same night with patients who had surgery earlier that day — peer-to-peer, employee-free knowledge transfer over cookies. Starbucks trains customers to use "barista-speak" ("tall," "venti," modifier order) via a take-home booklet and, more subtly, by repeating every order back in the correct format in front of the whole line — a public, low-stakes correction mechanism dressed up as a translation courtesy rather than a correction.
- **Customer job design.** The same "airline kiosk, not supermarket checkout" principle from Section 8 applies directly: design the customer's task to be genuinely easier or more valuable than the full-service alternative, not merely offloaded onto them. LSQ's customers upload their own receivables data directly into the servers LSQ actually uses for underwriting — extra work, but work that gets them funded faster and exposes them to credit-risk information about their own clients they'd otherwise never see. eBay pushes nearly every retail function (merchandising, fulfillment, even peer support and quality control via feedback stars) onto customer-operators, an approach the book calls **innovating with empathy** — succeeding only because the interface and tasks were deliberately engineered to be simple and forgiving for a non-expert user.
- **Managing customer performance.** Fines and late fees are shown to be weak levers — research cited in the book finds that instrumental penalties (a late fee) often give customers a sense of having "paid for" the transgression, which paradoxically licenses the bad behavior rather than discouraging it. Two better strategies: (1) **redesign the model to eliminate the need for compliance in the first place** — Netflix's mail-based DVD model demoted the customer's operating burden from "drive to the store by a deadline" to "mail it back whenever," compared with Blockbuster's compliance-dependent model; (2) where demotion isn't possible, use **normative methods** (shame, blame, and pride) rather than instrumental punishment — Zipcar, whose entire model depends on the prior renter returning a specific car to a specific spot on time, leans on community identity and belonging ("zipsters," social events, "ambassadors for change" branding) to make letting down a *fellow community member* feel different from stiffing an anonymous corporation, alongside (not instead of) its $50/hour late fee.

The **values lever** ties customer management back to the book's culture chapter directly: culture isn't only an internal, employee-facing asset. Customers who are highly exposed to your operations (which is definitionally true of most service businesses) are also being influenced by your organization's worldview, and a strong, clearly signaled culture is itself a customer-management tool.

## 13. Three Phases of Customer Management Maturity

A staged framework for how far to take customer-operator management, offered as a sequence rather than a binary choice.

**Phase 1: Getting control.** Before optimizing anything, test your own assumptions about how customers are actually affecting cost and quality — the book stresses that this is usually far cheaper and easier than organizations assume, because the relevant data is typically already being collected; the hard part is psychological, not technical (reframing a belief you're certain of as a hypothesis that needs testing). First Union Bank's failed assumption that low ATM adoption reflected low awareness (rather than genuine teller preference) — an assumption that, when acted on by deploying greeters to redirect customers, backfired into public, press-covered customer alienation — is the cautionary example. The diagnosed condition: an "epidemic of overconfidence" in service companies about predicting customer operating behavior.

**Phase 2: Actively involving customers.** The lowest-risk way to extend customer-operator management: bring customers into your *improvement* process, not just your production process. The model is borrowed from Toyota's manufacturing **andon cord** — a cord any assembly-line worker could pull the instant they spotted a defect, which stopped the line and brought a manager over immediately, with the flaw-spotter later celebrated rather than blamed. The customer-facing analog is the customer complaint, reframed as a signal-rich input rather than a fire to extinguish: most companies respond to complaints purely as service-recovery moments (apologize, offer something free, move on) and never mine them for the systemic improvement opportunities buried in the pattern of what customers are actually telling them.

**Phase 3: Going all the way.** Full integration of customers as central producers of the value they consume — Scott Cook's (Intuit) "Contribution Revolution" concept, exemplified by *Wikipedia*, Hyatt's user-and-professional-rated concierge tips, and P&G's BeingGirl.com (a teen community site the company found to be four times as cost-effective as television advertising). The explicit warning attached to this phase: giving customers real operating ownership tends to generate a real, and sometimes unwanted, sense of *decision rights* over the business itself, not just its production process. Threadless's customer-voted T-shirt design model worked beautifully for product design, but when the company considered a corporate merger, that same customer base demanded a say in firm-level strategy and the merger was ultimately abandoned. eBay's decision to change its feedback-star color (made by a new manager who didn't think to check first) triggered a customer uprising over what looked, internally, like an inconsequential styling choice — a reminder that once customers are truly co-producers, no operating decision is small anymore.

## 14. Now Multiply It All by Culture — Schein's Framework and the Three Disciplines

Culture is defined as the element that brings a well-designed service model to life — the way people (both employees and, per the framework above, customers) actually interact within the system. A perfectly designed model deployed into an indifferent or tyrannical culture wastes the design entirely.

**Schein's culture framework** (credited to MIT's Edgar Schein) is adopted as the book's working model, with three layers:

- **Artifacts** — the visible, tangible by-products of a culture: IDEO's no-dress-code, its "Tech Box" salvage-yard of design materials, its lack of permanent job titles.
- **Behaviors** ("espoused values," in the book's shorthand) — the observable norms in action: brainstorming rules that defer judgment and maximize idea quantity, project-based teams that dissolve and reform, peer-driven performance review.
- **Shared basic assumptions** — the underlying beliefs that actually generate the artifacts and behaviors. At IDEO: "failure is part of the culture" ("enlightened trial and error"), and "fail often to succeed sooner." The book's key methodological point: copying the artifacts of a strong culture (removing a dress code, declaring "no idea is bad") does nothing on its own — change has to start at the level of shared assumptions, because assumptions are what drive the thinking that produces the behavior you actually want.

**Three disciplines observed across organizations that build service-excellence cultures successfully:**

- **Clarity** — knowing exactly what culture you need and why it's tied to your performance goals. Zappos's ten explicit core values (Deliver WOW through service; Embrace and drive change; Create fun and a little weirdness; Be adventurous, creative, open-minded; Pursue growth and learning; Build open and honest relationships; Build a positive team and family spirit; Do more with less; Be passionate and determined; Be humble) are the book's clearest example of codified clarity, reinforced by a 348-page, employee-and-vendor-written *Culture Book* used both as an onboarding tool and a living record. Publix's guarantee ("We will never knowingly disappoint you... cheerfully refunded immediately") is printed on every store wall and every employee's business card, giving frontline staff (like the VP who personally scrubbed a customer's filthy oven on his day off) an unambiguous behavioral target even in unprecedented situations.
- **Signaling** — relentlessly communicating core values, especially at moments of maximum receptivity. The book borrows the psychological concept of **imprinting** (first observed in birds bonding with the first moving object they see after hatching) to explain why the first minutes of a new hire's experience matter disproportionately: Commerce Bank's first-ninety-seconds "wack jobs" / "Wow!" phone drill, or Zappos's culture-first, two-interview hiring process (one for cultural fit, one for skills — with the "skills" interview, e.g. a typing test, still doubling as a humility screen), imprint what the organization actually values before any policy manual gets a chance to. Sewell Automotive's onboarding replaces a facilities tour with **storytelling** — new hires' first activity is hearing true stories of service excellence (a technician driving overnight to replace a client's battery; a cashier quietly repurposing end-of-week flowers as customer gifts) — explicitly used to set a standard and *give people permission* to act a certain way, not to demand heroics as the baseline.
- **Consistency** — making sure stated values actually inform decisions, policy, and resource allocation, not just talk. The diagnostic phrase used at the Mayo Clinic, "Is this right for the patients or not?", is offered as the model form: a single sentence that can be invoked in any meeting to re-anchor a decision to the stated culture. Zappos enforces consistency by refusing to let "happiness" mean lowered standards: it still fires the bottom-performing 10 percent of employees annually, ties 50 percent of call-center performance reviews to "contribution to culture and core values" (not just attendance/punctuality), and explicitly penalizes reviews with *no* noted mistakes (read as excessive risk-aversion). Former Mayo CEO Glenn Forbes's formulation: "If you've just communicated a value but you haven't driven it into the operations, into the policy, into the decision making, into the allocation of resources... then it's just words."

## 15. Decalcification — Preventing Cultural Hardening

A named risk specific to sustaining culture over time, not just building it initially. Culture, left unattended, dries out and hardens — partly because of **heuristics**, the mental shortcuts people use under repetitive exposure to similar situations, and partly because employees in constant customer contact get a systematically skewed sample of feedback: customers who are having a great experience rarely call to say so, so frontline staff are disproportionately exposed to complaints, and can start treating complaining customers as a uniform, exasperating category rather than individuals with valid grievances. The book calls this hardening **calcification**.

The illustrative case (drawn from a *Harvard Business Review* article by Dan Ariely): a customer whose car was repeatedly, legitimately defective was gradually relabeled internally as "the customer from hell" by employees worn down by her recurring complaints — when in fact she had been sold, in the book's phrase, "the cars from hell." The moral explicitly drawn out: it is impossible to deliver excellent service once you've dehumanized the customer in your own head, however understandable the fatigue that produced the dehumanization might be.

The prescribed antidote is **regular, scheduled decalcification**, with the required frequency depending on how corrosive the specific job is. Kaiser Permanente's discovery, cited directly: certain high-emotion call-center roles required active decalcification *every four hours*, because staff measurably began shutting down emotionally around the four-hour mark in a shift.

**A general diagnostic method for changing any specific problematic behavior**, offered as a repeatable three-question sequence (built on the Schein layers from Section 14):

1. What's the problematic behavior?
2. What are the shared basic assumptions driving that behavior?
3. What can we do to change those underlying assumptions?

Applied to a consulting firm case in the book: the problematic behavior was low analytical rigor with clients; the underlying assumption, once surfaced, was that presentation polish was valued internally more than getting to the truth (status flowed to the best communicators, not the most rigorous analysts). The fix required changing formal and informal incentives and status cues, and the firm's own founder modeling a change in his personal work habits (doing analytical prep visibly, in the office, instead of privately at 4 a.m.) to demonstrate the new priority was real. A final, blunt distinction offered alongside this framework: most performance problems are **good people behaving badly** (a cultural/assumption problem, fixable) rather than **bad people behaving badly** (a selection problem, not fixable by culture work — those individuals need to be removed, since keeping them is an implicit endorsement of the behavior).

## 16. Getting Bigger — Two Paths to Growth and the Quality-Customization Matrix

Once the design-times-culture equation is under control, organizations face a structural choice about how to grow, and the book insists there are exactly two options: **do more of what you're already doing** (growing the existing service model) or **do different things** (building new service models).

**Growing the existing model requires standardization, and standardization does not have to sacrifice quality — but it does require deliberately reducing customization.** The tension is mapped on a **quality-customization matrix**: quality on the vertical axis, customization on the horizontal. Orient Express Hotels (the Cipriani) sits in the high-quality, high-customization quadrant — extraordinary, but built on decades-long staff tenure at a single property, structurally incapable of scaling. Four Seasons occupies high-quality, high-standardization: reliably excellent and fully scalable, because the *feeling* of exclusivity is consistent even though the operational delivery is highly standardized (the same water glasses, the same doorman protocol, city to city). The reframe: a loyal Cipriani guest checking into a Four Seasons wouldn't feel shortchanged — standardization done well is invisible to the customer, not merely tolerated.

**Mass customization and point-of-sale customization** are named as the mechanism that reconciles standardization with a felt sense of personalization: let the customer make a small number of choices at the very end of a highly standardized process, managed easily by frontline staff, while the back office runs identically regardless of what's ordered. Burger King's "Have it your way" (hold the pickles, not "build me a veggie Whopper") and Chipotle's mix-and-match assembly line over a genuinely narrow set of core ingredients are the book's examples. The explicit warning attached to this section: **don't run your business by the comment box.** Customers who ask for a specific accommodation (Lululemon's request for rain jackets) rarely understand, or need to understand, the operational trade-offs their request implies — evaluating and contextualizing those trade-offs is management's job, not the customer's.

Increasing customization purely to please individual customers, without strategic filtering, produces the matrix's worst quadrant: **high customization, low quality** — the book's telecom-company case, where each region kept its own acquired systems and procedures, producing 8 million customer calls a month from a 12-million-customer base, driven by internal process chaos rather than any actual customer benefit.

## 17. The Five Stages of Strategic Grief, the Multifocused Firm, and Shared Services

**The five stages of strategic grief** — an explicit borrowing of Kübler-Ross's five stages of grief, mapped onto how incumbents typically respond to a focused new entrant that's chosen to be strategically bad at things the incumbent has always tried to be good at:

1. **Denial** — dismissing the entrant by focusing on its obvious weaknesses ("Commerce's rates are pathetic," "Southwest doesn't even feed you").
2. **Anger** — triggered by realizing your *most profitable* customers (the least price-sensitive ones) are often the first to defect, because they're the ones willing to trade a weak attribute (rate) for a strong one (hours/attitude).
3. **Rationalization** — minimizing the threat's scope ("we're only losing one small segment"), often reinforced by outside consultants brought in to validate the existing worldview.
4. **Despair** — setting in once it's clear the first entrant wasn't a one-off; the gap between "zero new entrants" and "one" is typically years, but the gap between subsequent entrants keeps shrinking, producing a pile-up.
5. **Acceptance** — the point at which management stops relitigating the threat's legitimacy and starts responding to it strategically. The book frames its own job, in client work, explicitly as accelerating organizations to stage 5 as fast as possible.

**The multifocused firm** is the book's answer to surviving focused competition without simply acquiring your way to false comfort (acquisitions "mask many sins" — bigger, but often weaker). A multifocused firm runs **multiple service models under one corporate structure**, each individually optimized for a distinct operating segment, each deliberately good at some things and bad at others (Best Buy's price-sensitive core store paired with Magnolia, its ultra-premium store-within-a-store; Giorgio Armani's couture boutiques paired with the younger, standardized Armani Exchange). The two brands feel completely different to the end customer while sharing significant back-end infrastructure — in Best Buy's case, literally the same building.

**Shared services** is the structural mechanism that makes the multifocused firm work: centralizing back-office functions (finance, HR, IT, facilities, procurement) across multiple customer-facing brands so each brand benefits from scale or expertise it couldn't afford alone, without diluting what makes each brand distinct at the front end. The CDM Group's restructuring (pulling agency-level HR/finance/IT into a shared holding-company layer while leaving all client-facing creative, strategic, and scientific decisions with the individual agencies) is the extended case, alongside Yum Brands (Taco Bell, KFC, Pizza Hut and others sharing purchasing scale, e.g. becoming one of the world's largest cheese buyers) and Zappos (6pm.com sharing training and culture infrastructure with Zappos.com while running a deliberately lower-service, discount-focused customer experience).

Two named benefits distinguish successful shared-services models from 1980s-style conglomerates (which the book treats as a cautionary failure mode — bundled units that shared no real mutual benefit and often made each other worse off):

- **Economies of scale** — pooled purchasing and infrastructure cost advantages (Yum Brands' cheese buying).
- **Economies of experience** — deliberately engineered cross-unit knowledge transfer, exemplified by Omnicom University (a multi-week executive training program that turns top performers' real work into the following year's teaching cases) and General Electric's Crotonville.

**Two named failure modes that block shared services from working:**

- **Hard lines** — the political difficulty of deciding which functions get centralized and which stay with the business unit. Business-unit leaders (who own revenue and tend to be the more visible, persuasive, "above the line" voices) usually have more organizational clout than shared-services leaders (who own costs and are "below the line"), making the negotiation an unfair fight by default; the book's diagnostic test is to check whether any credible CEO-succession candidates come from the shared-services side — if not, the fight probably isn't fair in your organization either. Firm, sometimes unilateral leadership (the book cites Yum Brands CEO David Novak personally overruling a business-unit head's push for a dedicated IT system) is presented as the observed, if not fully satisfying, solution; the authors note they have not yet seen this achieved purely through consensus-building.
- **Low quality** — forcing business units to use centralized services that aren't genuinely world-class, which gives shared services a deservedly bad reputation ("forced mediocrity") and recreates the 1980s conglomerate failure pattern.
