# Venture Deals — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, advising on fundraising, or structuring equity/deal terms.*
*Book: "Venture Deals: Be Smarter Than Your Lawyer and Venture Capitalist" (4th ed.) — Brad Feld & Jason Mendelson*

---

## 1. The Players and the Financing Landscape

Every venture deal has more participants than "entrepreneur and VC." Understanding each player's incentives is the first negotiating skill.

**The entrepreneur/founder.** The center of the ecosystem. Founders cannot outsource involvement in a financing to their lawyers — only the founder can resolve many of the issues that come up. Cofounder relationships almost always start well and often fray over time (stress, competence gaps, personality, life changes). This is exactly why vesting, drag-along, and co-sale terms exist: to predefine what happens when a founder leaves, protecting founders from each other.

**The venture capitalist and firm hierarchy** (know who has actual authority):
- **Managing Director (MD) / General Partner (GP)** — most senior, makes final investment decisions, sits on boards. Sometimes titled "executive managing director" or "founding general partner" for extra seniority.
- **Partner** — a title that is often diluted; many "partners" are not actual decision-makers (an old investment-banking trick to blur seniority levels).
- **Principal / Director** — junior deal professional, has some responsibility but usually needs MD support to close a deal.
- **Associate** — works for a deal partner; scouts deals, does diligence, builds the cap table model. Associate programs typically last ~2 years.
- **Analyst** — most junior, crunches numbers and writes memos.
- **Venture partner / operating partner** — experienced entrepreneurs in a part-time firm role; can advocate for deals but usually need MD support.
- **Entrepreneur in Residence (EIR)** — parks at a firm (often up to a year) while planning their next company; helps with intros and diligence.

Rule of thumb: insist on a direct relationship with an MD/GP. Anyone else is unlikely to be at the firm for the long haul or to have final say.

**Financing round nomenclature.** There is no legal definition of round letters — it's just convention. Historical drift: what used to be "Series A" (first institutional round) is increasingly preceded by "Series Seed" and even "Pre-Seed." Numbered extensions (Series B-1, B-2) happen when the same investors add money at a different price without a new lead; a new lead investor typically triggers the next letter. Generally: Pre-Seed/Seed/Series A = early stage; Series B/C/D = mid stage; Series E+ = late stage — though the lines blur.

**Types of VC firms by check size and stage:**
- **Micro VC fund** — often one GP, usually under $15M total capital, invests almost exclusively seed/early stage.
- **Seed stage fund** — up to ~$150M, focused on being the first institutional money in, rarely invests past Series A. Often provides your first outside board member.
- **Early stage fund** — $100M–$300M, invests seed through Series A, occasionally leads Series B, often takes pro rata in later rounds.
- **Mid stage / growth fund** — $200M–$1B, invests Series B and later, once the company is clearly working and needs acceleration capital.
- **Late stage fund** — invests near IPO; includes dedicated late-stage VC funds, hedge funds, crossover investors, bank-affiliated funds, sovereign wealth funds.

**Angel investors.** Individual investors, active mostly in early rounds, often don't participate in later rounds — which is exactly why pay-to-play and drag-along terms exist (to manage non-participating investors in tough financings). Must generally be **accredited investors** under SEC rules (net worth >$1M, or income >$200K individually / $300K joint for the prior two years and expected to continue). "Super angels" are prolific angels who, once they raise outside money, effectively become micro VCs with full fiduciary duties.

**The syndicate.** A collection of investors in a round. Most syndicates have a **lead investor** (occasionally two or three co-leads) who negotiates on behalf of the group — but the entrepreneur is still responsible for communicating with every investor in the syndicate, not just the lead. Watch out for **party rounds** (many investors, each investing a small amount) — nobody feels committed, and the company can end up with a syndicate that has "the worst of all possible worlds": lots of names, no real backing.

**Lawyers.** An experienced VC-financing lawyer is invaluable; a bad or inexperienced one can get you out-negotiated, run up legal bills on both sides, and damage your reputation with future investors (your lawyer reflects on you). As of the book's writing (2019), typical legal costs: $5,000–$20,000 for a very early stage financing, $20,000–$40,000 for a typical financing — costs that have stayed roughly flat over a decade even as hourly rates doubled, largely due to standardized documents.

**Accountants and bankers.** Rarely needed at the earliest stages except for cross-border or entity-conversion issues (accountants), or rounds of $50M+ (bankers). An early stage company arriving with a banker is often read by VCs as a signal that the company already failed to raise money on its own, or is getting bad advice.

**Mentors vs. advisers.** Mentors typically help without a fee arrangement (may take a small equity grant for board/advisory service); "advisers" who ask for a cut of the deal or a retainer up front should be treated with suspicion, especially by early stage companies.

## 2. Preparing for and Running the Fundraising Process

**Structural readiness.** Before approaching investors: incorporate as a Delaware C Corp (VCs won't invest in pass-through entities like LLCs/S Corps and Delaware is essentially universally accepted), register as a foreign corporation in every state where you operate, and organize a clean data room (cap table, IP assignments, financials, major contracts, employment agreements). Every person who has touched the company's IP — employees, contractors, advisers — must have signed a proprietary information and inventions agreement; a single missing IP assignment has killed deals.

**Mindset:** "Do. Or do not. There is no try." Treat fundraising as a full commitment, not an exploration — investors sense uncertainty in founders who describe themselves as "testing the waters."

**How much to raise.** Don't over-engineer a financial model (it will be wrong regardless) — instead work backward from runway to the next meaningful milestone, and add a cushion (e.g., a 6-month plan gets funded for ~12 months). Never quote a *range* ("$5–7M") — it signals you haven't done the work and confuses which VCs can lead (a VC whose max first check is $3M can lead a $5M round but not a $7M one). State a specific number; you can always upsize if oversubscribed.

**Fundraising materials, roughly in order of use:**
- **Elevator pitch** — 1–3 paragraphs, usually embedded directly in an intro email, not a separate attachment.
- **Executive summary** — up to 3 pages: problem, product, team, why-now, basic financial trajectory. This is often the actual first impression and gets forwarded internally at a fund — invest real effort here.
- **Presentation ("the deck")** — typically 10–20 pages; good decks can be done in 10 slides or fewer. Focus on: problem, market size, team, competitive landscape, plan of attack, current status, summary financials, use of proceeds, milestones.
- **Business plan / PPM** — rarely requested at early stage; largely a late-stage or investment-banker artifact.
- **Demo/prototype** — undervalued by founders, prized by investors; shows vision and execution capability better than any document.

**Finding the right VC and creating competition.** Categorize prospective investors as leaders, followers ("maybe"), or "slow no" (never says no, just goes quiet — treat as a no and move on). Target 3–6 months to run a competitive process: shorter signals desperation, longer kills urgency on both sides. Never disclose which other firms you're talking to (prevents them from comparing notes and neutralizing the competitive dynamic) — but do signal, in general terms, that there is real interest elsewhere.

**How VCs decide.** Early conversations are "informal"; once a firm starts formal due diligence, insist a partner-level person is directly engaged, not just an associate doing a data-collection exercise. Ask for references — specifically ask for founders where things went badly (fired CEO, failed company), since that reveals how the VC behaves under stress.

**Closing the deal has two phases:** (1) negotiating and signing the term sheet, and (2) drafting and negotiating the 100+ pages of definitive documents that implement it. Most signed term sheets do lead to a closed deal — the exception is later-stage deals where the VC still needs internal investment committee approval that hasn't happened yet (a major risk if the term sheet doesn't disclose this).

## 3. Term Sheet Overview: Economics and Control

There are only two categories of terms that actually matter in a venture financing: **economics** (the ultimate financial return to each party in a liquidity event) and **control** (who can make or veto which decisions). Everything else is largely negotiating noise, distraction, or ego. When a VC digs in hard on a term that touches neither economics nor control, that's a signal about how that VC will behave as a board member and business partner — not a substantive issue worth much of your time.

Founders receive **common stock**; investors buying into a preferred round receive **preferred stock**, which carries special rights (liquidation preference, protective provisions, etc.) that a holder can always unilaterally give up by converting to common — but the conversion is one-directional; you can't convert back.

**Pre-money vs. post-money valuation.** Pre-money = value ascribed to the company before the investment; post-money = pre-money + amount invested. Example: $2M raised at $6M pre-money = $8M post-money, investor owns 25% ($2M / $8M). **The classic trap:** when a VC says "I'll invest $5M at a $20M valuation," they usually mean *post-money* — meaning the real pre-money offer is $15M, not $20M. A well-prepared founder immediately says, "I assume you mean $20M pre-money" to force clarification without cost.

## 4. Valuation, Price per Share, and the Option Pool Shuffle

**Fully diluted** means every share and every right to acquire a share — outstanding stock, options, warrants, and the *entire* option pool (issued and unissued) — is counted when calculating price per share and ownership percentages.

**The option pool shuffle.** The size of the employee option pool has more impact on effective valuation than founders often realize, because VCs typically require the pool to be sized and funded *before* the money comes in — diluting only the existing shareholders (founders/earlier investors), not the new investors. Typical early stage option pools run **10%–20%** of the post-financing cap table, shrinking at later stages.

Worked example: $2M raised at $10M post-money (20% to new investors). If the term sheet requires a 20% post-financing unallocated option pool, the split becomes: 20% new investors / 60% old shareholders / 20% option pool. If instead the pre-existing 10% pool simply rolls over unchanged, the split is 20% / 70% / 10% — the old shareholders keep an extra 10 points. Same investor ownership (20%) in both cases, but the option pool size determines who actually bears the dilution.

Negotiating levers for the option pool: (1) fight the pool size itself (e.g., 15% instead of 20%); (2) accept the pool size but raise the pre-money valuation to compensate; (3) push to have the option pool increase come out post-money rather than pre-money. All three are really just negotiating price under a different label. Come prepared with an **option budget** — a list of planned hires and their expected grants through the next fundraising milestone — to justify your proposed pool size.

**Warrants** are another lever for a VC to effectively lower valuation without changing the stated price — a right to buy shares later at a fixed price. Rare in early equity rounds (they add accounting complexity) but standard in **bridge loans**, where the existing investor typically gets a discount (up to ~20%) or equivalent warrant coverage for taking early risk before a full round closes.

**How valuation actually gets set** (it's judgment, not science): stage of the company (later stage = more weight on financial performance), competitive tension among funding sources (more interest = higher price, but never bluff competition you don't have), experience of the founding team, size/trendiness of market, the specific VC's stated "natural entry point" (some VCs cap themselves at a max post-money valuation), historical/projected numbers (though early stage projections are reliably wrong), and the macro economic climate.

**Price per share formula (from the term sheet):** Purchase Price = Pre-Money Valuation ÷ Fully Diluted Capitalization (including the expanded employee pool), calculated immediately prior to closing.

## 5. Liquidation Preference: Preference and Participation

The liquidation preference determines proceed allocation on any **liquidity event** (sale, merger, change of control, asset sale — but *not* an IPO, since preferred typically converts to common as part of an IPO). It has two separable components that are often conflated under one name:

**1. The preference (the multiple).** A per-share amount, typically **1×** the original purchase price plus any declared/unpaid dividends, paid to preferred *before* common receives anything. 1× is the long-standing norm; multiples spiked as high as 10× after the 2001 dot-com crash before reverting, though higher multiples still appear in later-stage or distressed deals. Anything beyond a straight 1× non-participating (or reasonably capped participating) structure should be treated as a red flag about the investor.

**2. Participation.** Three flavors:
- **Non-participating ("simple preferred")** — investor takes *either* the preference *or* converts to common and takes their pro rata share of proceeds, whichever is greater. They never get both.
- **Full participation** — investor takes the preference *and then also* shares pro rata (as-converted) in the remaining proceeds. "Have your cake and eat it too" — a fully participating holder essentially never has a reason to convert.
- **Capped participation** — like full participation, but participation stops once total proceeds per share reach a specified multiple (e.g., 3×) of the original purchase price; the holder then either takes the capped amount or converts to common, whichever is greater.

**Worked numeric example** (Series A: $5M invested at $10M pre-money, $15M post-money, investor owns 33% / founders own 67%, rounding for simplicity):

| Sale Price | 1× non-participating | 2× non-participating | 1× participating (no cap) | 1× participating, 3× cap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $5M | Investor takes all $5M (liquidation preference overhang — common gets nothing) | Same | Same | Same |
| $15M | Investor: $5M (indifferent to convert); Common: $10M | Investor: $10M; Common: $5M (half of prior case) | Investor: $5M + 33% of $10M = $8.3M; Common: $6.7M | Same as full participation (cap not reached) |
| $30M | Investor converts: $10M; Common: $20M | Investor: $10M (same either way) | Investor: $5M + 33% of $25M = $13.3M; Common: $16.7M | Same as full participation (cap not reached) |
| $100M | Investor converts: $33M; Common: $67M | Investor converts: $33M; Common: $67M | Investor: $5M + 33% of $95M = $36.35M; Common: $63.65M | Cap ($15M) exceeded — investor converts instead: same as non-participating case ($33M) |

Key takeaway: participation matters a lot at low-to-moderate exit values and matters progressively less at high exit values (because conversion to common eventually becomes better for the investor than preference-plus-participation). It also matters more in aggregate as more total capital is raised with participating terms attached (a $50M-raised company selling for $100M with 2× non-participating preferred can wipe out common entirely).

**Multiple rounds — stacked vs. blended (pari passu) preferences.** When a company has raised multiple preferred rounds, on a below-preference-threshold sale: **stacked preferences** pay the most recent (senior) round first, in full, before earlier rounds see anything. **Blended/pari passu preferences** pay all preferred pro rata according to relative amounts invested. Which structure applies is a negotiated, situational outcome — not a legal default.

**Liquidation preference overhang** = the total amount that must be returned to preferred holders before common holders see a dollar. **Management carve-out** = a negotiated diversion of some sale proceeds to management/employees even when the sale price is below the liquidation preference overhang (a norm reputable investors generally honor even though the legal documents don't require it).

**Recommendation for early rounds:** keep the liquidation preference simple — 1×, non-participating. Participation terms set in a seed round tend to get inherited by every subsequent round (investors love precedent), and a seed investor with participation rights in a later, larger round can end up hurting the common holders disproportionately relative to the small check they wrote.

## 6. Pay-to-Play

A **pay-to-play** provision forces existing preferred investors to keep investing pro rata in future (usually "down") rounds or have their preferred stock automatically converted to common — losing preferential rights. Became common after the 2001 downturn; largely absent before it. Two intensities: aggressive (converts if you don't participate *at all*) vs. softer (partial conversion proportional to the shortfall in your pro rata contribution).

Rationale: it forces every investor to commit at the time of investment to the "rules of engagement" for supporting the company through hard times, rather than free-riding on other investors' willingness to fund a down round. It affects both economics (nonparticipating investors lose their liquidation preference stack) and control (reshuffles who retains preferred voting rights). Companies should watch for angel/friends-and-family investors caught by pay-to-play provisions who structurally can't follow on — and the very bad "adverse change redemption" variant that forces recapitalization at a near-zero valuation if some investors don't participate; that specific version should never be accepted.

## 7. Vesting and Exercise Period

**Standard vesting:** 4 years with a 1-year cliff, monthly thereafter. Nothing vests before the 1-year mark; at 1 year, 25% vests; the remaining 75% vests monthly (or quarterly) over the next 3 years. Example: leave after 18 months with a 1-year cliff → 18/48 = 37.5% vested.

Founders often get credit for time already served pre-financing (e.g., 1 year of vesting credited at closing, remaining vests monthly over the next 3 years) if the company was founded materially before the VC round.

Unvested stock, when someone departs, disappears back into the company (**reverse dilution**) — everyone's percentage ownership increases proportionally. For founders it typically vanishes entirely; for departed employees it usually returns to the option pool for reissuance.

**Single- vs. double-trigger acceleration.** Single-trigger = full/partial vesting acceleration automatically on a change of control. Double-trigger = acceleration requires *both* a change of control *and* the employee being terminated (without cause) within a defined window (often 1 year) after the deal. Double-trigger is now the strong industry norm — it avoids handing an acquirer a fully-vested-and-unmotivated team on day one, while still protecting employees from being pushed out right after close with nothing.

**Exercise period.** Historically, once you vest but leave the company, you have 90 days to exercise (pay the strike price) before forfeiting unexercised vested options. Some companies have extended this to the legal maximum (10 years), which is more employee-friendly but can undermine the retention incentive built into the option structure and creates equity misalignment between long-tenured employees and short-tenured ones who hold options for a decade.

## 8. Antidilution Provisions

Antidilution protects existing preferred holders when the company later issues stock at a *lower* price than they paid (a **down round**), by adjusting their **conversion ratio** (via a **conversion price adjustment**) so they effectively convert into more common shares.

**Full ratchet** — the harshest form. If the company issues even one new share below the protected round's price, that entire round's conversion price is repriced down to match the new (lower) issuance price, regardless of how many shares were sold at the lower price. Came into vogue 2001–2003; rare today except in distressed financings.

**Weighted average antidilution** (the modern standard) — accounts for *how many* shares were issued at the lower price, not just the price itself, using the formula (variables as defined in the term sheet): NCP (new conversion price) is calculated from OCP (old conversion price), CSO (common stock outstanding), CSP (what should have been purchased at the old price for the amount invested), and CSAP (what was actually purchased at the lower price). The larger the down-round issuance relative to the existing capitalization, the bigger the adjustment.

**Broad-based vs. narrow-based** — refers to what's counted in "common stock outstanding" (CSO) in the weighted-average formula. Broad-based includes all common, all as-converted preferred, *and* all outstanding options/warrants/rights (a bigger denominator, so a gentler adjustment). Narrow-based counts only currently outstanding securities (smaller denominator, harsher adjustment for the company).

**Standard carve-outs** (issuances that do *not* trigger antidilution): employee option pool shares, shares issued for non-cash consideration in an approved M&A deal, shares tied to equipment/real-property leases or bank debt, and — a useful modern addition — shares where a majority of the affected preferred class waives antidilution rights (lets a majority of a class bring in new money at a lower price without diluting themselves further via holdout minority investors gaming the antidilution mechanism).

Antidilution is rarely a major negotiating battle on its own — the advice is to accept its near-universal presence and instead focus energy on building enough value that it never triggers.

## 9. Board of Directors and Board Composition

The board is the single most powerful governance mechanism — it can fire the CEO and must approve budgets, financings, M&A, option plans, and executive hires.

**Typical early stage board (3 or 5 seats):**
- 3-seat: Founder/CEO, VC, one outside/independent member (or second founder).
- 5-seat: Founder, CEO, lead VC, second VC, one outside/independent member.

**Mature/pre-IPO boards** grow to 7–9 seats, with the majority being independent outside directors (often domain-expert former CEOs/executives), plus CEO, one founder, and a few VC seats depending on capital raised.

Board negotiation focus points: total board size (more members ≠ better function), how the "remaining"/independent seat(s) get chosen (mutual consent vs. majority vote of common+preferred as one class), and whether VCs get a formal seat vs. a non-voting **observer** role (quality varies enormously — sometimes a junior associate just there to learn, sometimes more valuable than the actual board member).

Outside/independent board members are typically compensated with options for **0.25%–0.5%** of the company, vesting over 2–4 years, plus reimbursed expenses — rarely cash.

VCs generally prefer *not* to seek outright board control; instead they rely on **protective provisions** (veto rights) to protect their position, which lets board decision-making stay focused on the good of the company as a whole rather than turning every vote into a shareholder-class contest.

## 10. Protective Provisions (Veto Rights)

Protective provisions require a majority (or defined threshold) of preferred stockholders' consent before the company can take certain major actions — effectively a veto right, separate from board control. Standard items requiring preferred consent typically include: altering rights/preferences of the preferred; changing authorized share counts; creating a new senior/parity class of stock; redeeming/repurchasing common stock; any change-of-control or liquidation event; amending the certificate of incorporation or bylaws; changing board size; declaring dividends; incurring debt above a threshold (often the first number renegotiated upward as the company matures); voluntary bankruptcy; exclusive licensing/sale of IP; and (a modern addition) issuing crypto tokens or launching an ICO.

Negotiating notes: avoid inserting the word "material"/"materially" as a qualifier — it invites endless disputes over an undefined standard; specificity beats vague reasonableness language. When a new round (e.g., Series B) is added, push hard for **a single combined vote across all preferred series** rather than separate class votes — separate class votes create multiple veto constituencies that can each independently block company action, which becomes unmanageable as more rounds accumulate. Watch for inflated consent thresholds (e.g., requiring 90% instead of a simple majority) that let a small investor block action disproportionate to their stake.

## 11. Drag-Along Rights

Two distinct flavors, often confused:

**1. Preferred-drags-common.** If a majority of preferred agrees to sell the company, all other holders (remaining preferred and common, including founders) must consent and not object. This emerged after the dot-com bust, when common holders would sometimes hold a sale hostage to force preferred to waive part of their liquidation preference. A common compromise: tie the drag threshold to a majority of *common* stock (not preferred) consenting, so small common holders are only bound when their own peer group agrees.

**2. Departed-founder drag** (the authors' preferred version). When a founder leaves, their remaining shares are automatically voted in the same proportion as all other shares cast in any vote — preventing an embittered ex-founder from holding out on votes indefinitely.

Practical note: most jurisdictions require majority-of-class (California) or majority of all shares as-converted (Delaware) to approve a sale, though acquirers commonly want 85–90% shareholder consent in practice — so drag-along rights matter most when there's a large, fragmented minority (e.g., thirty 1%-holders), not when one or two parties hold most of the cap table.

## 12. Convertible Debt, Valuation Caps, and SAFEs

**Mechanics.** Convertible debt is a loan that converts into equity at a future priced round, usually at a **discount** to that round's price (typically **10%–30%, most commonly 20%**) as compensation for the investor's early risk — plus a modest **interest rate** (commonly **5%–12%, averaging ~8%**, often set near the IRS Applicable Federal Rate). Worked example: $500K raised at a 20% discount; six months later a VC leads a $1M Series A at $1.00/share. The angels convert at $0.80/share → 625,000 shares; the VC gets 1,000,000 shares at $1.00 — total effective round size $1.5M.

**Valuation cap.** Sets a ceiling on the price at which the note converts, protecting early investors from a scenario where the next round prices dramatically higher than what they would have accepted (e.g., a $4M cap with a 20% discount means the investor converts at the lower of "80% of the next round's price" or the cap-implied price). Caps can, in turn, act as an anchor that later-round VCs use to negotiate the priced round *down*, so entrepreneurs are advised to avoid disclosing the cap until price is otherwise agreed with the new lead — though in practice VCs increasingly ask for note terms before issuing a term sheet.

**Warrants as an alternative discount mechanism** exist but add legal/accounting complexity (including the Original Issue Discount tax problem, solved by paying a small separate amount for the warrant) and are less common for seed deals than the simple discounted-price approach. Never let an investor get *both* a discount and warrants — pick one.

**Conversion mechanics and triggers.** Standard notes convert automatically when the company raises a qualifying new equity round above a stated minimum size (e.g., $1M) within a stated term (commonly up to 180 days, though VC-affiliated funds are often capped at 1 year by their own LP agreements). If the qualifying financing doesn't happen, the debt simply stays outstanding, governed by amendment provisions requiring majority (sometimes supermajority) noteholder consent to change terms.

**Conversion on a sale of the company before conversion** has three typical outcomes: (a) lender just gets principal + interest back; (b) lender gets principal + interest **plus a multiple** (commonly 2×–3×) of principal; (c) the note actually converts into acquirer stock/cash at a capped valuation. **Always specify this in the documents** — ambiguity here is a common source of disputes.

**Legal danger of convertible debt:** while a company is carrying outstanding convertible debt, it is technically **insolvent** (assets < liabilities on the balance sheet), which in many states shifts board/officer fiduciary duties toward creditors, not just shareholders — a real, if rarely litigated, exposure that doesn't exist under a straight equity raise.

**SAFEs (Simple Agreement for Future Equity, created by Y Combinator) and KISS (500 Startups)** are alternatives that function like an unpriced warrant rather than debt — eliminating interest, maturity dates, and some of the insolvency/fiduciary-duty risk of true debt, but also eliminating explicit pro rata rights (which can be added back, undermining the "simple" premise) and the natural forcing-function a maturity date provides for founder-investor communication. SAFEs/KISS can include a cap, a discount, and an **MFN (most favored nation)** clause guaranteeing the investor inherits any better terms given to later investors.

## 13. The Capitalization Table

The cap table shows who owns what, before and after a financing. Founders must be able to build/verify one themselves — don't blindly outsource this to a lawyer, as errors are common.

**Basic mechanics** (2M founder shares, $10M pre-money, $5M investment, target 20% post-money option pool): Post-money = $15M; VC ownership = 33.33%; founders' resulting share = 100% − 33.33% − 20% = 46.67%. From there, solve backward for total fully diluted shares, option pool share count, VC share count, and price per share. Always sanity-check: (founder shares + pool shares) × price per share should equal the agreed pre-money valuation.

**Convertible notes converting alongside a new round** create a genuine reopening of the valuation conversation, resolved by one of three common methods:
- **Pre-money method** — pre-money valuation stays fixed; dilution from note conversion is shared proportionally by *both* founders and new investors. Most common, but new investors sometimes dispute it since their resulting ownership % ends up above what they thought they "bargained for."
- **Percentage-ownership method** — the new investor's target ownership % is fixed; all dilution from the note conversion falls on founders alone.
- **Dollars-invested method** — post-money valuation is fixed at (pre-money + new investment + converting note principal/interest); a middle-ground compromise between the other two, with founders absorbing only the extra dilution attributable to the noteholders' discount, not the full note conversion.

## 14. Crowdfunding (Product, Equity, and Token)

**Product crowdfunding** (Kickstarter, Indiegogo) — nondilutive: backers pre-pay for a not-yet-built product; a successful campaign both validates demand (an MVP test) and raises cash. Downside: campaigns can succeed financially but the company never ships, or ships partially — a known and largely accepted risk in that culture.

**Equity crowdfunding** (AngelList and similar platforms) — governed by the 2012 JOBS Act, with three tiers of differing regulatory burden:

| | Rule 506(b) | Rule 506(c)/Title II | Title III |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aggregate raise cap | No | No | Yes ($1M / 12 months) |
| General solicitation allowed | No | Yes | Only via a single funding portal/broker |
| Who can invest | Accredited only | Accredited only | Accredited and non-accredited |
| Broker/intermediary required | No | No | Yes |
| Regulatory burden | Light | Medium | Heavy |

Most AngelList-style raises actually happen under 506(b) — functionally similar to a traditional private placement, just discovered online. True "equity crowdfunding" in the retail sense is only Title III, and it comes with meaningful disclosure costs (tens of thousands of dollars) and small per-investor caps for non-accredited participants (as little as $2,000 depending on net worth).

**General solicitation** is the key legal tripwire: advertising your raise (mass email, public pitch without a preexisting relationship) can blow your exemption from SEC registration unless you're specifically operating under 506(c) or Title III.

**Differences from a traditional VC raise:** the founder usually sets the deal terms rather than negotiating them; board seats are rarely part of the deal; you'll have many small, often less-engaged investors rather than one or two committed leads — meaning the company bears more of the ongoing communication burden; and diligence on your investor base becomes much harder (watch for a small number of difficult, entitled, or under-sophisticated participants).

**Token/ICO crowdfunding** — the book treats this cautiously: hype-driven, legally uncertain (many ICOs likely run afoul of securities law), several outright frauds documented, and still immature as a real financing mechanism relative to equity or debt.

## 15. Venture Debt

**First rule: venture debt follows equity, it doesn't replace it.** Lenders use the presence and quality of VC backing as their primary underwriting signal — a company's **primary source of repayment (PSOR)** for venture debt is really "access to future equity capital," not cash flow or collateral (the PSOR for ordinary commercial loans). Loan sizes typically run **25%–50%** of the most recent equity round raised.

**Loan types:** most venture debt is a **growth capital term loan** with a **6–12 month interest-only (I/O) period** followed by amortization over the remaining 3–4 year term (a common structure: 12 months I/O + 36 months straight-line amortization). **Recurring revenue loans** are underwritten off SaaS-style metrics (ARR/MRR, unit economics) instead of relying purely on the equity-backing signal.

**Pricing components:**
- **Interest rate** — almost always floating, quoted as a spread over Prime (rarely LIBOR at this loan size).
- **Loan fees** — typically 0.25%–0.75% of loan amount, usually tied to closing or utilization.
- **Warrant coverage** — the main lender-compensation lever, typically expressed as a % of the loan amount's economic value (e.g., 2% coverage on a $2M loan = $40,000 of warrant value). Bank warrant coverage: roughly 10–15 basis points of fully diluted ownership for early stage, 2–5 bps for later stage; debt funds price higher given their higher cost of capital and yield targets. Strike price and duration (typically 7–10 years) are both negotiable — entrepreneurs prefer next-round-based strikes and shorter duration; lenders prefer last-round strikes and longer duration.
- **Final payments** — a fee (e.g., 8% of commitment) paid at loan maturity, common in life sciences/healthcare and later-stage deals; defers cost but must still be modeled into total loan price.
- **Prepayment fees** — commonly a "3-2-1" structure: 3% of balance if repaid in year one, 2% in year two, 1% in year three.

**Control terms:** early stage growth-capital loans typically carry **no financial covenants** (offset by warrant coverage); later stage/no-warrant loans typically trade covenants back in. Watch closely for a **Material Adverse Change (MAC) clause** — broad, lender-discretion language that can trigger a default and a "cash sweep" even without a defined covenant breach. MAC clauses are hard to eliminate outright (many lenders require them as policy) but their real-world danger depends heavily on the specific lender's track record and reputation — reference-check the lender the same way you'd reference-check a VC.

Negative covenants commonly restrict: asset disposition, change of control/location, M&A activity, additional indebtedness, liens/encumbrances, dividends/distributions, and subordinated debt payments — all generally requiring lender consent above a materiality threshold.

**Restructuring/soft landings:** communicate early and often the moment performance goes off plan — surprise is the enemy of a good outcome with a lender. A meaningful share of any venture debt portfolio is technically in default at any given time; how the company and lender handle that (not whether it happens) is what determines the outcome. The progression from Plan A (raise more equity) → Plan B (sell) → Plan C (wind down) should be actively managed with both investors and the lender at the table.

## 16. How Venture Capital Funds Work

**Three-entity structure:** the **management company** (employs the team, pays overhead, survives across fund vintages), the **limited partnership (LP)** vehicle (the actual "fund," containing the outside investors/LPs), and the **general partnership (GP)** entity (the legal general-partner-of-record for a given fund).

**Management fees:** typically **1.5%–2.5% annually** of committed capital during the **commitment/investment period** (usually the first 5 years), stepping down afterward; over a fund's roughly 10-year life, total management fees run around **~15% of committed capital** in aggregate. Fees are earned independent of investment performance — the only long-run consequence of poor returns is the firm's ability to raise its *next* fund (which is why "it takes a decade to kill a venture firm").

**Carried interest ("carry"):** the VC's share of profit after returning invested capital to LPs, typically **20%** (up to 30% at some elite/legacy firms). Worked example: $100M fund returns $300M total → first $100M returns to LPs, remaining $200M profit split 80/20 → GP carry = $40M. Management fees can often be "recycled" (reinvested) so the fund still effectively deploys the full committed amount, benefiting LP cash-on-cash returns. **Clawback risk:** if a fund distributes carry early (from a strong initial exit) but the fund overall fails to return capital, GPs may be contractually obligated to return that carry to LPs — a real and sometimes contentious mechanic, especially years later when partners have moved on, divorced, or spent the money.

**GP commitment:** GPs typically invest 1% (occasionally up to 5%) of the fund alongside LPs, to align incentives.

**Commitment/investment period vs. investment term:** the commitment period (~5 years) is the window for *new* investments; the investment term (typically 10 years + two 1-year extensions) is how long the fund can remain active managing existing investments (follow-ons, exits). A fund whose commitment period has lapsed without a new fund raised is a **"zombie" fund** — the entrepreneur should ask directly: "When was your last new investment?" and "Are you raising a new fund?" to detect this.

**Reserves.** VCs mentally (and often formally) reserve future capital for follow-on rounds in each portfolio company at the time of first investment — commonly more reserves for earlier-stage investments. Underreserving forces a VC to pick favorites in future rounds, or push the company toward a sale; ask your prospective investor directly how much dry powder is reserved for your company specifically.

**Cross-fund investing** happens when a firm's original fund is underreserved and a *different*, later fund from the same firm fills the gap — this can create real conflicts of interest among a firm's own LPs across fund vintages, especially in a down round.

**Corporate Venture Capital (CVC) and strategic investors** answer to a parent company (or corporate department) rather than purely to financial LPs — motivations can include technology insight, channel access, or competitive lockout, not just return. CVCs may offer a higher valuation for these non-financial reasons, but never grant a strategic or CVC investor a **first right of refusal on an acquisition** — it's a serious, avoidable mistake. Consider a **performance warrant** (extra equity that vests only if the strategic partner hits defined performance milestones) instead of granting unconditional bonus equity to a helpful strategic investor.

## 17. Negotiation Tactics and Game Theory

**Only three things matter in a negotiation:** achieving a fair result, preserving the relationship, and genuinely understanding the deal you're striking. A venture financing is fundamentally a **multiplay, win-win game** (unlike the single-play, adversarial prisoner's dilemma) — both parties will work together for years afterward, so reputational and tit-for-tat dynamics reward cooperative, transparent behavior far more than they reward "winning" any single term.

**Contrast with other negotiation "games":** acquisitions are a hybrid — winner-take-all on the specific deal terms, but win-win on the post-close working relationship (most management teams stay on with the acquirer). Litigation and a founder's involuntary departure are closer to single-round or purely adversarial games where different tactics apply.

**Preparation.** Always have a plan: what you want, what you're willing to concede, and your **walk-away point** (your **BATNA — best alternative to a negotiated agreement**) decided *before* the negotiation starts, not improvised under pressure. Research the other side; know their incentives, insecurities, and constraints. As an inexperienced founder facing an experienced VC, your biggest asymmetric advantage is often simply **time** — you have one deal to focus on; they have many.

**Negotiating personality archetypes to recognize and adapt to:** the Bully (force and volume, no real substance — either match intensity or calmly absorb it); the Nice Guy (endless "let me get back to you," never a firm answer — stay clear and direct, don't get worn down); the Technocrat (obsesses over every minor point — engage selectively, don't lose sight of what actually matters, but don't let them exhaust you either); the Wimp (easy to extract concessions from, but you're then stuck negotiating both sides, and it can backfire once they're on your board); the Curmudgeon (nothing is ever good enough — patience and consistency eventually work); Smooth/Steady/Smart (well-prepared, calm, genuinely dangerous if you're not equally prepared).

**Tactical rules:**
- **Never make the first offer** if you don't have to — you cap your own upside.
- **Listen more than you talk**; you can't lose a point you never volunteered.
- **Don't negotiate point-by-point in isolation** if the other side is steering that way — insist on evaluating the whole package before conceding pieces, since "reasonable on each point" can still add up to an unfair whole.
- Distrust the phrase **"that's just market"** — it's a weak argument on its own; ask *why* the market condition applies to your specific situation.
- **Anchor** on your top few priorities and hold them, while giving ground elsewhere.
- Sequence: start with points you can get quick agreement on to build momentum, save valuation for last (it will have multiple rounds of back-and-forth regardless).
- **Multiple competing term sheets are your single biggest source of leverage** — but never show one VC another's actual term sheet, and never disclose exactly who else you're talking to (it lets competing investors compare notes and collude, intentionally or not, against you).
- Never issue a walk-away threat you're not prepared to actually execute — bluffing, once caught, destroys your position for the rest of that negotiation.

**Fixing a bad deal after the fact.** Terms mostly don't matter until an exit event. A future financing round led by a *new* investor is a natural opportunity to fix earlier bad terms (the new lead usually wants the founders happy and aligned). Even without a new round, a direct, honest conversation with existing investors after some track record has been established often works. As a last resort, acquisition negotiations frequently include a retention/reallocation conversation that can correct old imbalances — but don't play a single-round game against your own investors by teaming with the acquirer against them; it damages trust that outlives the deal.

## 18. Raising Money the Right Way — Practical Do's and Don'ts

- **Don't be a machine.** Fundraising is fundamentally a people decision (the "beer test" / "first-date energy") — the relationship will outlast the average U.S. marriage in duration; let investors get to know you as a person.
- **Don't ask VCs to sign an NDA.** Reputational and time constraints already protect you; an NDA actually prevents a VC from usefully talking to potential co-investors.
- **Don't mass-email/spam VCs.** Personalized, well-researched outreach signals the same diligence you'll apply to running the company.
- **No means no.** Don't keep pushing after a clear pass.
- **Don't ask for a referral after a no** — it puts the VC in an awkward position of implicitly endorsing you while having just declined you, and the referral rarely lands well. (Exception: if you have a real relationship and the "no" is about firm fit, not about you or the company, it's fine to ask.)
- **Don't be a solo founder** if you can avoid it — investors read solo-founder deals as a red flag both for workload sustainability and for the founder's ability to attract believers (exception: repeat/proven entrepreneurs).
- **Don't overemphasize patents** for a software company — for most software startups, patents are at best a defensive asset; execution and team quality matter far more to investors.
- **Don't stay silent about bad behavior** (including harassment) in the ecosystem — call it out and support those affected.

## 19. Financing Stage Differences

**Seed deals** have the lowest legal cost and least contentious negotiation, but the highest risk of setting a bad precedent, since seed-round terms tend to carry forward into every future round. A too-favorable seed valuation, if not later justified by growth, can force an unwanted down round or block a subsequent financing outright if unsophisticated early investors expect every future round to price up. A single committed lead investor (plus some quality angels) beats a scattered **party round** of many small, uncommitted checks — a party round often leaves a company with no one truly invested in helping it survive to the next round.

**Early stage.** Terms set here (especially liquidation preference structure and whether protective provisions vote as one combined class or per-series) tend to lock in for the life of the company. Push hard, at this stage, for combined-class voting across all future preferred series — it avoids compounding veto constituencies later.

**Mid and late stage.** Board composition and voting control become the dominant issues as each new lead investor wants a board seat; mitigation tactics include capping the number/percentage of VC-designated directors, offering observer status to "bumped" directors, or creating a smaller executive committee that can move faster than the full board. Valuation and deal **"structure"** (multiple liquidation preferences, guaranteed minimum multiples, or outright bans on selling below a floor price) become confounding factors — an aggressively high valuation with heavy structure can trap founders into an exit price nobody's happy with. Later-stage term sheets are also more likely to require an unstated investment-committee approval step *after* the term sheet is signed — a real risk that has killed deals after the company already shut down its other options.

## 20. Letters of Intent (LOIs) — The Other Term Sheet

An LOI (also called an indication of interest, MOU, or acquisition term sheet) governs the sale of the company. Unlike a financing (a growing pie, win-win), an LOI negotiates a **fixed pie** — tone and stakes shift accordingly.

**Price is rarely the headline number.** A stated purchase price is typically reduced by: an **escrow/holdback** (commonly **10%–20%** of aggregate deal value, held **12–24 months** to cover breaches of reps and warranties); a **working capital adjustment** (push for a zero target, resisting large-company demands for excess working capital from a startup); an **earn-out** (often a mechanism for the buyer to underpay at close and pay full value only if future milestones are hit); and a **management retention pool** carved out of, or added on top of, the headline price to incentivize key executives to stay.

**Asset deal vs. stock deal.** Sellers generally want stock deals (clean, total transfer, "the company disappears"); buyers often initially propose asset deals (cherry-pick assets, leave liabilities behind) — but stock deals are far more common in practice except in distressed sales, since a lingering shell company with unresolved liabilities is a real burden on the seller. Asset deals in a near-insolvency scenario can also expose officers/directors to fraudulent conveyance liability if creditors aren't properly protected.

**Form of consideration matters enormously.** Cash is unambiguous; private-company stock requires understanding the *acquirer's* own cap table, liquidation preferences, and participation terms before you can value what you're actually receiving — a seemingly attractive "$15M all-stock" offer can be worth far less once you account for $110M of senior liquidation preferences ahead of the common stock you'd be issued.

**Stock option treatment in a merger** has shifted over time: older plans often provided automatic assumption or full acceleration; modern plans typically give the acquirer flexibility (assume, substitute with RSUs/cash plans, or let unvested options simply lapse), which can create real friction among employees with different tenure. Watch for **"revesting"** demands (the acquirer resets the vesting clock even on already-vested options) and be careful to properly value the **barter element** (strike price) of options in the purchase price allocation — sellers often mistakenly let the acquirer capture this value.

**Reps, warranties, and indemnification.** Establish early in the LOI *who* is making representations (the company vs. individual shareholders) and get a rough sketch of indemnification terms before signing — "standard reps and indemnification" language in an LOI is often a deliberate placeholder for a fight to come later. **Fundamental reps** (fraud, capitalization, taxes, and increasingly IP ownership and 409A compliance) typically carve out of the escrow cap; the cap on any carve-out should never exceed total deal value.

**No-shop clauses in an M&A LOI are typically unilateral** (only bind the seller) — push for a defined, reasonable window (45–60 days) and an automatic termination if the buyer walks away from the process.

**Shareholder representative** — the person who manages post-closing escrow disputes and earn-out fights, typically unpaid and often thankless; never assign this role to someone who will keep working for the acquirer post-close (creates an impossible conflict), and always negotiate a dedicated expense fund into the merger agreement for this role. Professional shareholder-rep firms exist specifically to take this burden off an individual.

## 21. Engaging an Investment Banker (M&A Context)

Bankers are broadly discouraged for early stage financings, neutral for later-stage financings, and genuinely valuable in a **sale process** — chiefly because they can canvass 50+ logical acquirers (creating real price discovery and competitive tension), absorb the operational workload of running a process, and serve as a useful "bad guy" buffer so the founder/executive relationship with the acquiring team stays clean through hard negotiations.

**Selection criteria:** referrals from trusted sources; deep, *specific* industry expertise (not generic "tech banker"); fresh personal relationships with actual decision-makers at likely acquirers (not just C-suite name-dropping); a real track record of *closed* deals, not just pitched ones; a firm commitment that the senior banker who pitched you stays personally engaged (watch for bait-and-switch to a junior team); and cultural/working-style fit.

**Engagement letter terms to negotiate:** scope of work (sell-side only, or also fundraising/dual-track — dual-track fundraising fees typically run 5%–10% of funds raised, and the banker must be a licensed broker-dealer/FINRA member to legally raise capital); **success fee** (commonly starts near 10% for sub-$5M deals, declining to 1%–2% for deals nearing $100M, sometimes with a scaled incentive structure for higher exit values — model every plausible outcome against the fee schedule before signing); **retainer** ($5,000–$15,000/month, typically for 6+ months, deductible against the success fee); term/termination/tail provisions (a tail protects the banker's fee if a deal closes with a solicited buyer within 6–12 months of the engagement ending — limit tails to buyers the banker actually engaged); indemnification (carve out gross negligence/recklessness); a key-person clause (protects you if the specific banker you hired leaves the firm); and, for complex cap tables, a fairness opinion (expensive — $1M+ — and mostly relevant for public companies or highly complex/conflicted private cap tables).

## 22. Why Term Sheets Exist — The Underlying Economic Logic

Contracts (and the term sheet that precedes them) exist to address three underlying frictions, and any specific term is best evaluated against which friction it's actually solving:

- **Alignment of incentives / constraining bad behavior.** Absent a contract, self-interest alone can diverge sharply between parties (e.g., a "walking dead" portfolio company where the CEO wants to keep drawing a salary and the VC wants to shut it down and recoup the tax loss). A good term is one that narrows this gap rather than widening it.
- **Transaction costs.** The time and money spent creating and maintaining the relationship — standardized documents and market norms have dramatically lowered these relative to decades past; negotiating detail *up front* (e.g., in the LOI) is nearly always cheaper than resolving ambiguity later during definitive-document drafting.
- **Agency costs and information asymmetry.** No matter how much time a VC and founder spend together, neither will ever know the other's business (or true motivations) as well as their own. Contractual mechanisms — a board seat, an odd-numbered board with an independent member, redemption rights — exist specifically to blunt this asymmetry.
- **Reputation constraints.** In a small, relationship-driven industry, reputation is arguably the single most powerful enforcement mechanism of all — more powerful, in the long run, than any individual clause. How you behave under ambiguity or conflict *becomes* your reputation.

## 23. Legal Fundamentals Every Entrepreneur Should Know

**IP ownership.** Get every founder, employee, contractor, and adviser to sign IP assignment/proprietary-information agreements *before* problems arise — informal conversations ("we came up with this idea over beers") have genuinely led to disputed ownership claims. Code written by a paid contractor is *not* automatically owned by the company unless the agreement explicitly states it is **"work made for hire."**

**Patents.** Utility patents grant a 20-year monopoly; application must generally be filed within 12 months of public disclosure or offer for sale. Provisional applications ($2,000–$10,000) can be converted to full applications ($10,000–$25,000) within a year. Valuable primarily as a competitive/defensive asset in hardware, biotech, and medical devices — much less central for most software businesses, where execution and team quality dominate investor evaluation.

**Trademarks.** A three-step process (clearance search, application, prosecution/publication) protects a distinctive name/symbol/tagline; in the US, ownership is based on first *use*, not first *registration* — a critical distinction from many other countries.

**Corporate structure.** C Corp is required (and expected) for any VC/angel-backed company, since multiple stock classes aren't permitted in an S Corp and most such companies don't distribute profits in their early loss-making years anyway. S Corp or LLC only make sense for a company that will never raise institutional equity.

**409A valuations.** Stock options must be granted at fair-market-value strike prices; using an independent professional valuation firm creates an IRS **"safe harbor"** presumption of correctness. Historically common stock was often valued at ~10% of the last preferred price; post-409A scrutiny has pushed this toward 20%–30% in many early stage companies, raising the effective cost (and reducing net value) of employee options.

**83(b) elections.** Must be filed within **30 days** of a restricted stock grant (no exceptions, no cures if missed) to lock in taxation (and the long-term capital gains clock) at grant-date value rather than vesting-date value — a small, easily-missed administrative step with outsized tax consequences.

**Founders' stock** is legally just common stock issued very early at a very low price (e.g., $0.0001/share) — there's no separate legal category. Founders should subject their own shares to vesting voluntarily (rather than waiting for investors to impose it), which both protects remaining founders if someone departs early and can preempt harsher vesting terms an investor might otherwise demand.

**Consultants vs. employees.** Misclassifying a true employee as a contractor to avoid wage/hour law creates real back-wage liability; to properly structure a contractor relationship, engage them project-by-project, pay by project (not hourly/monthly retainer), avoid directing/supervising/training them directly, let them work for other clients, and avoid open-ended "terminable at will" language.

**Compensating service providers with equity.** Direct stock grants create *immediate* taxable income equal to fair market value in excess of what was paid (mitigated by an 83(b) election on unvested stock); options avoid that immediate tax hit (recipient only pays tax when exercised/sold) at the cost of requiring the holder to pay the exercise price to actually own the shares.
