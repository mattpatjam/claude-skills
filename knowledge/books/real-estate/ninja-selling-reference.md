# Ninja Selling — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, advising on real estate investment/development strategy, or structuring deals.*
*Book: "Ninja Selling: Subtle Skills, Big Results" — Larry Kendall*

---

## 1. The Ninja Philosophy: Attraction, Not Pursuit

The book's foundational distinction is between two eras of selling. "Depression-Era Selling" (Kendall's term for classic high-pressure sales training) runs on manipulation, intimidation, scarcity, and combat: cold-call quotas, "ten nos before a yes," always-be-closing. The internet broke this model in two ways: customers now often know more about a market or product than the salesperson before they ever walk in, and a pushy salesperson's reputation can be destroyed publicly in a way it never could before. Puffery and pitching are now a turn-off, not an edge.

Ninja Selling replaces pursuit with attraction. The central metaphor is the "pursuer-distancer dance" (borrowed from psychology, seen in sales, dating, and family dynamics alike): the harder you chase someone, the faster they distance themselves. You cannot make someone move toward you by pushing; you can only attract them by having something they value. The practical implication is that a salesperson's job is not to convince or close, it's to create enough value that the customer moves toward them voluntarily.

This rests on an abundance mind-set versus a scarcity mind-set. The book quantifies "abundance" using real transaction-rate math: roughly 15% of US households do a real estate transaction in a given year (owner-occupied households specifically), meaning roughly fifteen transactions exist annually for every hundred people you know. Most agents believe the market is scarce because they aren't in flow with the people around them, not because transactions aren't happening. A recurring proof point in the book: a struggling agent's own 200-person database was found (via title search) to have completed 40+ transactions in a year — she just hadn't been in relationship/flow with those people, so they transacted elsewhere.

Four principles structure the entire system and map directly to the book's four parts:
1. **Personal Mastery** — controlling your thoughts and energy (the mind-set layer, must come first because it overrides skills and actions if wrong)
2. **Stop Selling! Start Solving!** — creating value instead of pitching
3. **Ninja Business Strategy** — the daily/weekly system that generates continuous inbound flow
4. **Connect and Communicate** — the science of how customers think and decide, applied through a structured consultation process

The philosophy underneath all four: "Ninjas" (a term coined internally at Kendall's firm, The Group, Inc.) get big results without ego — they are quiet, competent, relationship-first professionals, in deliberate contrast to "samurai" salespeople who rely on flash, charisma, and self-promotion. The system is explicitly designed to work for introverts and non-"power personalities" as well as natural extroverts, because it is built on teachable, repeatable questions and processes rather than charisma.

## 2. Personal Mastery: Energy, Mind-Set, and Programming

Kendall frames humans as broadcasters and receivers of energy ("vibe"), and argues customers and colleagues can feel whether you're operating from a "player" or "victim" mind-set. Victims are avoided; players attract people and opportunity. This isn't purely metaphorical in the book's framing — it ties to a four-quadrant energy model borrowed from sports psychologist Dr. James Loehr:

- **High Positive** — happiness, confidence, inspiration (the "Performance Quadrant" — where top performers operate when it's time to perform)
- **Low Positive** — contentment, relaxation, safety (where you recover between high-positive performance moments; you can't sustain high positive indefinitely)
- **High Negative** — anger, fear
- **Low Negative** — sadness, depression, guilt

Players spend most of their time in the two positive quadrants and treat negative-quadrant excursions as brief episodes because they have "response-ability" (the ability to respond, not just react). Victims get stuck in the negative quadrants and stay there. Strategies to move out of negative quadrants: gratitude, exercise, music, time in nature, learning something new, healthy escape (movies, games), spending time with positive people, and turning to mentors/coaches. Avoid the traps of seeking out other people in negative quadrants, or numbing with substances/spending.

**Rituals vs. routines:** A ritual is a pregame action taken right before a performance moment (a listing appointment, a call, a presentation) to move yourself into the high-positive quadrant — music, visualization, breathing, mental rehearsal of the opening two minutes. A routine is what you do every day regardless of performance moments (sleep, exercise, diet, the structure of your workday). Routines compound; you can predict a salesperson's success by observing their daily routine alone.

**The Ninja Morning Routine** (four steps, roughly 15-30 minutes):
1. Gratitude — give thanks before anything else; the book is explicit that visualization/goal work done *without* first establishing gratitude tends to backfire into a scarcity spiral (you start noticing what you lack instead)
2. Positive reading — even a few pages
3. Affirmations/visualization — focus on a goal as if already achieved
4. Write two personal notes — focused on other people, not yourself

**The Reticular Activating System (RAS):** the book's operating model for why "what you focus on expands." The RAS is the brain's filtering mechanism, tuned to two pathways — want/value/love, or don't-want/fear/threat — and it isn't judgmental about which one you feed it. Fixating on what you don't want (debt, missing a shot, a sand trap) programs the RAS to notice and move toward exactly that. The fix is to consciously redirect focus onto the desired outcome, stated positively. A written, specific "life list" (inspired by adventurer John Goddard, whose 127-item bucket list partly inspired the film *The Bucket List*) is offered as the tool for pointing your RAS at what you actually want in life, not just business.

**Eight-step process for programming/reprogramming the nonconscious mind** (used for goals and affirmations — the nonconscious runs 96-98% of behavior and cannot distinguish a repeated statement from reality, "it can't take a joke"):
1. Make sure it's *your* goal, not someone else's for you
2. State it positively and make it measurable ("I enjoy being free and clear," not "I want to get out of debt")
3. First person ("I," not your name or "you")
4. Present tense (the nonconscious only registers the present moment — "I enjoy earning $250,000" not "I want to earn")
5. See it through your own eyes, not as a third-person observer
6. Make it sensory-specific — see, hear, feel, smell, taste it
7. Repeat daily for 30 days (this is the typical reprogramming window)
8. Use an anchor — a physical object or image that triggers the mental state on sight

Affirmations are called "mental push-ups": write the affirmation 25 times each morning for 30 days (roughly 7 minutes), same logic as physical reps — irregular practice doesn't build strength. The **Net Forward Energy Ratio** (from *Enlightened Leadership* by Oakley and Krug) frames motivation as a ratio of productive "can-do" energy to nonproductive "can't-do"/distracted energy pulling against a goal; the higher the ratio, the more unstoppable the movement toward the goal.

## 3. Creating Value: Stop Selling, Start Solving

The book's reframe of the entire sales function: people don't want to be sold, they want their pain solved or their pleasure increased. Ninjas ask questions to uncover which one is in play, then offer a solution — they don't pitch. Two things people pay for, full stop:
1. Solving a problem (pain relief)
2. Feeling good (pleasure)

**The Law of Value** (from Bob Burg and John David Mann's *The Go-Giver*): your true worth is how much more value you deliver than you're paid. A seller's fee is justified only when the agent demonstrably creates more value than the fee costs — through a higher sale price, faster sale, less stress, or lower risk of the deal falling through. The book is blunt that many agents never grasp this and instead rationalize their fee through tradition or friendship rather than through demonstrated value creation.

**Buyer segmentation by pricing sensitivity** (a research-based 15/5/80 split cited from the book's own experience and industry surveys):
- 15% always buy lowest price, full stop — value-adds don't move them
- 5% always buy highest price (status, or "I get what I pay for" logic)
- 80% buy on **value** — the relationship between quality and price

Strategic implication: don't chase the 15% lowest-price segment. You can't sustain high income-per-hour as a discount provider, it dilutes your positioning with the other 85%, and this segment ("grinders") tends to extract maximum value while draining maximum energy. Instead, differentiate for the 80% value-driven majority. If you're frequently asked to discount, that's a signal your service level hasn't kept pace with the market ("a black-and-white television in a color world") — raise your game rather than your discount.

**The service tiering model** (four layers, used to build a value proposition and to justify fee):
1. **Core/basic services** — what virtually every competitor offers (price opinion, sign, lockbox, MLS listing, brochure, advertising, showings, closing coordination). These are now table stakes, not differentiators.
2. **Differentiated services** — what sets you apart from most competitors (detailed marketing plan, professional photography, enhanced online listings, individual property websites, visual pricing tools).
3. **Custom services** — tailored to the specific property (pre-inspections, staging, professional floor plans, contract-writing packets).
4. **Convenience services** — removing friction/effort from the client's life (handyman/cleaning referrals, home detailing).

A real example given: a self-identified "discount broker" (Estelle) was coached to offer clients a menu — the same reduced-fee/reduced-service program they'd used before, alongside a new enhanced-service/higher-fee program — and simply let the client choose. More than 80% chose the higher tier. This is the general "menu pricing" pattern: present tiers rather than a single fee, and most customers self-select upward.

**Top relevance-driving services** identified by research as most wanted by sellers and buyers (nearly identical lists): access to current active/sold data, a prelisting or buyer packet delivered before commitment, a competent proactive advisor, a smooth transaction with few surprises, and consistent communication. Notably, the book cites a national survey finding only ~20% of agents provide a prelisting packet at all — meaning simply doing this reliably differentiates you from 80% of competitors.

**The Four Pillars of Brand Building** (from market research firm Young & Rubicam, applied to personal/company branding): Recognition (do people know the name at all), Reputation (how do they feel about it), Relevance (do they need it, and are they getting what they actually want from it), Differentiation (how is it different from competitors). Most salespeople and firms only invest in pillars 1-2; Kendall argues pillars 3-4 (relevance and differentiation) are where the real competitive separation happens, because most of the industry treats real estate service as a commodity.

## 4. FORD Questions and Relationship-First Prospecting

**FORD** = Family/Friends, Occupation, Recreation, Dreams — the four core-value question categories used to build rapport and uncover pain/pleasure without ever directly prospecting ("do you know anyone who wants to buy or sell?"). Sample questions per category:
- Family: Where are you from originally? How's the family? What's your son doing?
- Occupation: What do you do? How's business?
- Recreation: What are you doing for fun? How's your golf?
- Dreams: framed indirectly, through future-oriented questions like "what are your plans for the holidays / this summer?"

Mechanically: "your next question is embedded in their last answer" — FORD conversation is meant to flow naturally rather than as a checklist, with each answer supplying the next question. Ninjas aim to keep roughly 75% "time of possession" (a football metaphor for who's talking) with the customer — the more the customer talks, the more connected they feel, and the more the agent learns about personality, decision style, and (critically) pain and pleasure driven by life change.

**The "two-minute warning":** research cited says customers mentally disconnect roughly two minutes after a salesperson starts talking unless that salesperson is unusually compelling. Any answer longer than two minutes should include a check-in ("Are you with me so far?"). This underlies the broader "Socratic method" framing — asking rather than telling, because the answers (and the sale) live inside the customer, not the salesperson's script.

**Listening for change** is the mechanism that turns FORD chitchat into business. Life changes create pain or pleasure that eventually drives a real estate decision:
- Pain signals: job loss, divorce, illness, adult child returning home, elderly parent moving in, longer commute
- Pleasure signals: promotion, inheritance, new baby, new hobby, empty nest, graduation, marriage
- Some changes are both (last child leaving, parent moving in) depending on perspective — the book notes both reactions can appear in the same family simultaneously

FORD questions serve three purposes: building rapport on first contact, prospecting (uncovering pain/pleasure organically rather than "who wants to sell a house"), and negotiating — when a deal stalls on a single sticking point (usually price), pausing and reconnecting to the buyer's or seller's underlying FORD motivation (e.g., "why do you want this house?") can reset perspective and unstick a deal over a comparatively trivial dollar amount.

**Five negotiating points** in any real estate contract (the book stresses most agents fixate only on the first): price, terms, dates (closing/possession), inclusions/exclusions, contingencies.

## 5. The Proactive Trusted Advisor and the Five Rules

Reframing the agent's identity from "salesperson" to **Proactive Trusted Advisor** is a named goal of Principle 2. Breaking down the phrase:
- **Proactive** — the agent calls the client first; if the client ever has to call to ask "what's going on with my deal," that's a failure state
- **Trusted** — client interests come first, even against short-term revenue (the book's central proof case: the firm proactively discontinued its own profitable investor workshops in January 2006, ahead of the Great Recession, and told clients the fundamentals no longer supported buying — long before the crash was obvious. This cost near-term revenue and built long-term trust.)
- **Advisor** — deep market knowledge plus street smarts; a genuine value-add on timing, negotiation, and transaction management, not just an information conduit

**The Five Rules of Ninja Selling** (the operational definition of trusted-advisor behavior):
1. Show up — for work, on time, visibly, reliably
2. Pay attention — be fully present, notice what's unsaid and the body language, "wherever you are, be there"
3. Tell the truth and keep commitments — even when the truth is unwelcome (e.g., telling a seller their price expectation is unrealistic)
4. Create value — actively look for ways to solve problems or generate good feeling, client interest first
5. Don't get attached to the outcome — chasing the commission produces "commission breath" that customers detect and that erodes trust; following rules 1-4 and letting outcomes follow is the intended sequence

**James Kane's four keys to trust** (cited as the research basis for Rule 3 and general trust-building): Character (do you do what you say), Competence (are you actually good at this), Capacity (do you have time/resources to actually help, right now), Consistency (are your behavior and presentation stable over time — inconsistency reads as untrustworthy, and consistency is also the specific driver of referrals, since people only refer someone who will reliably make *them* look good for the recommendation).

## 6. Flow: The Core Business Strategy

**Flow** is defined narrowly and specifically: frequency of interaction with the people who already know you — face-to-face, phone, mail, email, social media, ranked in that descending order of power. The book's central relationship-selling claim: people buy from someone they know, like, trust, AND are in flow with — all four conditions are necessary; missing any one of them, they'll transact with someone else who has all four (typically the person they're most recently in flow with).

**Why past great service doesn't guarantee repeat business** — an NAR-sourced finding the book leans on heavily: ~84% of sellers and ~88% of buyers say they'd definitely or probably use their agent again and refer them, yet only ~25% of sellers and ~12% of buyers actually do use the same agent on their next transaction. The buying/selling decision process takes 9-21 months from first spark to closing, but customers select *which agent* to call in roughly 72 hours, based overwhelmingly on whoever they were most recently in flow with — not on who did the best job years earlier. This reframes flow as the single highest-leverage activity in the business, more important than past performance.

**Auto-flow vs. live flow — the flow hierarchy** (most to least powerful): face-to-face, phone (voice-to-voice), text, mail, email/social media, advertising (least powerful). Two baseline targets:
- **Auto-flow**: minimum 3 scheduled touches/month via mail and email, ideally set on a calendar for the whole year so it runs automatically regardless of busyness or vacation
- **Live flow ("Magic Fifty")**: minimum 50 live interactions per week (face-to-face or phone), where every FORD-based conversation counts — a service club table of ten, weekly seller/buyer update calls, open house visitors, sideline conversations at a kid's game, etc. High-value case study: a "Missouri Mom" agent generating $250K/year in a $137K-average market entirely through her existing social routine (gym friends, coffee shop, church, kids' sports) reframed as disciplined live-flow interviewing.

Flow items must combine **art** (personal notes, tickets, calendars, birthday/anniversary cards — the relationship/heart side) and **science** (market statistics, neighborhood reports, financing info, newsletters — the data/head side); most agents default to whichever type matches their own personality and under-serve the other. All flow must pass one test: does it solve a problem or make the recipient feel good? If not, it's just junk mail, regardless of medium.

**The 8-in-8 system**: a structured eight-week sequence for launching a new relationship, rebranding yourself (new career or new company), reviving a stale database contact, or geographic farming. Weeks 1-6 are value-add mail/notes; week 7 is a phone call; week 8 is a face-to-face meeting (lunch/coffee). After the 8-in-8 burst, the contact rolls into standard 3-per-month auto-flow. Proof case (Kendall's own "Greg" story): an 8-in-8 with one new contact eventually led to helping relocate 120+ employees for that contact's growing company — illustrating that flow investment in one relationship can compound into outsized business.

**Database segmentation** — two independent axes:
- *Relationship tier* (A/B/C/D): A = proactive advocates who refer unprompted (roughly weekly flow, highest investment); B = fans who refer if asked (~monthly flow); C = will transact with you but won't proactively refer, the largest group and most at risk of drifting to another of the ~12 agents the average person knows (quarterly live touch minimum); D = people you want to know but haven't yet connected with (start with 8-in-8, then graduate to C).
- *Urgency*: Hot list (will transact within ~90 days, known and ready) vs. Warm list (will likely transact within a year based on observed life change, even if they don't know it yet) — target roughly 20% of a database on the warm list. A 16-item "move score" checklist of life changes (family size change, kids under 10 or teens, empty nest, over/under-housed relative to means, 7+ years in current home, employer expansion/downsizing, 1+ hour commute, inheritance, marriage/divorce, investment dreams, etc.) is used to systematically build the warm list from an existing database rather than relying on memory.

**Fifteen Post-Closing Contacts** (Wynn Washle's system, cited as best-in-class): a scheduled sequence of touches across the first year after closing (2-day, 2-week, 1-month, 3-month, 6-month/annual follow-ups, both spouses' birthdays and anniversary with a reminder call to the other spouse a few days prior, kids' birthdays, ticket giveaways, a home visit at 3-5 months) — loaded into a calendar so they fire automatically. A companion intake form called "Fun Facts" (names, birthdates, kids, anniversary, favorite restaurant/team) is collected during the transaction to power this system. Only 18% of agents follow up live after closing, and over 50% of past clients in one internal study couldn't correctly recall their agent's name or company just 30 days after closing — this is the gap the system is designed to close.

## 7. The Ninja Nine — Daily and Weekly Production Habits

The book's named list of the specific, low-friction habits observed across top producers — deliberately designed so that none of them can trigger rejection, because all of them create value rather than ask for anything.

**Five daily habits** (roughly 15-30 minutes each morning, before opening email):
1. Gratitude to start the day
2. Show up for work and stay on your own agenda rather than reacting to inbound email first
3. Write two personal notes (three opening phrases that work: "Thank you," "Congratulations," "I was just thinking of you and...")
4. Focus on the hot list daily ("who on this list wants to write a contract this week?")
5. Focus on the warm list daily

**Four weekly habits:**
6. Weekly customer service calls, using a **five-step calling process**: (1) Salutation, (2) Common ground/FORD, (3) Purpose of the call, (4) Common ground/FORD again, (5) Close in 2-3 minutes. Eight categories of calls that always create value: current active customers, recently closed customers, referral sources (say thanks), special-date greetings, 8-in-8 week-7 calls, ticket/giveaway offers, annual real estate reviews, and general thank-you/thinking-of-you calls. The book argues phone beats email for these calls on five grounds: customer preference (78% prefer phone per NAR data), speed (~500% faster than typing), quality (tone carries information text can't — a cited Harris survey found 82% of emails are misread on tone), referral generation (only happens in live conversation, in the "common ground" steps), and simply: it works, based on decades of coaching data.
7. Schedule two live "real estate reviews" per week — an offered annual sit-down (not a disguised listing pitch) to review a client's real estate holdings, values, and options, positioned the same way a client sees their accountant or doctor annually. Tracked conversion: ~43% of face-to-face reviews produce some business (listing, sale, or referral) vs. ~15% for mailed reviews — the single highest-converting activity in the whole system.
8. Fifty live interviews per week (the "Magic Fifty," detailed under Flow above)
9. Update the database weekly: update changed info, add new contacts, delete stale entries (databases decay ~15%/year through moves, deaths, and lost relationships), back it up, and print a current hot/warm list to carry physically.

**The Business Tracker**: a simple bucket/pipeline spreadsheet (warm list → hot list/listings → under contract → closed) used to visualize pipeline health at a glance. The book's warning: agents who focus only on nursing the "under contract" bucket to closing, without refilling warm/hot buckets, get erratic boom-bust income — "they don't have a blowout, they have a slow leak."

**What actually blocks people from doing the Ninja Nine** (named directly, since the activities themselves are trivially easy): fear (usually fear of looking like a pushy salesperson to friends — despite these specific activities being designed to never risk rejection), self-focus rather than customer-focus ("fear is the absence of love" — being in your head about yourself rather than in the game focused on the other person), and wanting instant results rather than trusting the compounding of small daily actions.

## 8. Managing Time: The P/I/E Framework

A time-categorization system (developed by instructor Walt Frey) for deciding where to invest hours, built around one core mechanic: you cannot directly control your most valuable time, you can only control the input that produces it.

- **P time (Productive)** — the three-ingredient definition: you + a customer + a contract or strong potential for one (sitting in a listing appointment, presenting a contract, showing homes to a qualified motivated buyer, negotiating). Prep work for these moments does *not* count as P time if the customer isn't present.
- **I time (Indirectly productive)** — flow time: customer service calls, real estate reviews, the 50 live interviews, personal notes, running the auto-flow system. This is what *generates* P time. The book's key mechanic: since you can't directly increase P time, the lever is always to increase I time.
- **E time (Everything else)** — administrative and non-core work, generally the kind of work that doesn't require a license and can be delegated to an assistant once volume justifies it.

A 30-day personal time log (P/I/E hours per day) is the recommended diagnostic tool to see where time is actually going versus where it's assumed to go — the book notes most agents are startled by how much time disappears into E time. Recommended responses: blend E-time activities into I-time wherever possible (the "Missouri Mom" and "soccer sideline" examples — turning already-scheduled personal time into FORD-based live interviews), schedule the daily/weekly Ninja Nine deliberately, and delegate E time to an assistant as soon as volume supports it. The "moonwalking bear" attention experiment (a selective-attention video demonstration) is used to illustrate that most agents are so focused on the "team in white" (distractions, meetings, email — E time) that they miss the "moonwalking bear" in plain sight (the vital-few P/I activities that actually drive income).

## 9. The Platinum Rule: Personality Types and Decision Strategies

**The Platinum Rule** (an explicit upgrade on the Golden Rule): treat others the way *they* want to be treated, not the way you would want to be treated — adapting to their personality, decision style, learning modality, and communication preference.

**Four personality types** (a DISC-based framework, using Chris Helder's more memorable relabeling — Power, Party, Peace, Perfection — because most salespeople can't recall what "DISC" itself stands for):

- **Power** (~15-20% of population): future-focused, goal/results-driven, fast decisions on minimal data, risk-takers oriented toward winning, outgoing and task-focused, want executive-summary materials and charts, talk and move fast, common in C-suite/entrepreneurial roles.
- **Party** (~25-30%): present-focused, motivated by fun and recognition, impulsive fast deciders prone to buyer's remorse, risk-takers, relationship-oriented, want color/photos/familiar testimonials, common in sales/hospitality.
- **Peace** (~35-40%, the largest group): past/present-focused, motivated by harmony and avoiding conflict/change, very slow decision-makers who need to build trust in you, the process, and themselves before deciding, risk-avoiders, want a clear process and trusted testimonials, common in helping professions (nursing, teaching, therapy).
- **Perfection** (~15-20%): past-focused, motivated by data and precision, slow analytical deciders prone to "paralysis analysis," risk-avoiders, want to verify and recalculate every number, common among accountants/engineers/bankers.

People are generally most comfortable with personalities adjacent to their own on a 2x2 (outgoing/reserved x task/people) grid and find the diagonal opposite hardest to communicate with (Power↔Peace, Party↔Perfection) — that's where deliberately practicing the Platinum Rule matters most.

**Decision strategies** — every customer leans toward a *toward* strategy (risk-taking, moving toward what they want, framed by asking "shall we see if we can buy this?") or a *from* strategy (risk-avoiding, moving away from loss, framed as "shall we make sure we don't lose this?"). Power/Party types skew toward; Peace/Perfection types skew from — the exact same soft-closing question should be phrased differently depending on which strategy the buyer is showing.

**Learning/thinking modalities** — visual (~60% of Americans; want to be shown things, charts, photos, floor plans; speak fast and use "I see what you mean"), auditory (smallest group; comfortable with pure conversation, no visual aid needed, look side to side, "I hear what you mean"), and kinesthetic (need to physically touch/feel/walk a space to understand it, speak more slowly, "this house has a good vibe"). Effective communication uses all three simultaneously — show it, tell it, let them feel it — since everyone uses all three modes even if one dominates.

**Balanced brain decisions**: the book's decision-quality model. The analytical (left) brain sets criteria and boundaries but functions best on only 3-5 variables — beyond 5 it slows, above 9 it enters "paralysis analysis." The emotional (right) brain processes enormous nonconscious data and answers the feel question. A good decision formula: set the 3-5 most important criteria (context), then within those boundaries, if it feels good, do it (content). Buyers who are too impulsive get buyer's remorse; buyers who are too analytical get stuck in nondecision, which becomes a bad decision by default. Overloading customers with data (a stack of MLS sheets, showing more than 5 homes without a running top-3 ranking) is named as the most common way agents unintentionally sabotage their customer's decision quality.

## 10. The Four-Step Ninja Consultation

The book's replacement for the traditional three-step sales presentation (Approach → Present → Close). Ninjas do not present; they consult.

1. **Connection** — decided in the first two minutes; if there's no connection, the customer will distance regardless of what follows. Delivered via the **Three-Step Greeting**: Enroll (a simple low-risk question like "how are you today?" — also a subtle diagnostic: an upbeat answer signals a *toward* decision strategy, a neutral/guarded answer signals *from*), Acknowledge (thank you / welcome / a genuine compliment), Who You Are (state your own name *last*, not first — leading with your name positions you as a typical salesperson). You confirm connection by gently nodding and watching for an involuntary nod back ("the guitars are in tune"). Immediately after connecting, you take control of the *process* (not the decision) by asking permission to proceed: "If it's all right with you, what I'd recommend we do first is...").

2. **Information** — the step most traditional salespeople skip by jumping straight to presenting. Three goals: clarity, confidence, and comfort committing. Questions must follow a specific **sequence** (credited in the book to the same logic found in Thomas Freese's *Secrets of Question-Based Selling*, which Kendall discovered only after realizing Ninja had independently arrived at the same order through trial and error):
   1. Rapport/connection questions (FORD)
   2. Foundational questions — easy questions that access the customer's *prior learning* (things they already know cold), building confidence before anything harder is asked; skipping straight to diagnostic questions here causes confusion and nervousness
   3. Diagnostic questions — aimed at clarity across six areas: who, what, when, where, why, how. (Case example: a buyer touring 40+ homes with no progress turned out to have an unresolved "who" — he wasn't sure his girlfriend was "the one" — no amount of showing more houses would have fixed that.)
   4. Solution/closing questions — once genuinely clear and confident, customers frequently close themselves

3. **Solution** — formulating potential answers to the pain/pleasure uncovered in Information, illustrated by an extended role-play (Kendall coaching his own wife, Pat, through what a Ninja version of a timeshare sales pitch would have looked like) showing how "toward/from," pain/pleasure, and pretend-state questions surface a buyer's own reasoning for a purchase they'd earlier dismissed as "a waste of time."

4. **Proposal** — communicating the solution so the customer actually "gets it." Six communication principles:
   - Maximize communication quality: face-to-face > phone > text/email, because words + tone + body language together carry more signal than words alone (a cited Stanford study found electronic negotiations succeed only ~10% of the time because parties become a "faceless other")
   - Match learning modalities (visual/auditory/kinesthetic, above)
   - Match and mirror voice (pace, volume), body position, and gesture size — a bigger gesture "orbit" than the customer's makes them uncomfortable and erodes trust, regardless of how good the underlying offer is
   - Go from big picture to small picture (general market → price range → neighborhood → specific property/home), never the reverse
   - Maintain a nonanxious presence — achieved by (a) not getting attached to the outcome, and (b) focusing on your process rather than the result (cited via quotes from Olympic skier Mikaela Shiffrin and golfer Rory McIlroy, both describing process-focus over outcome-focus)
   - "It matters who says it" — third-party validation (a friend's social media post, the seller stating their own price after your questions) is more persuasive than the same claim coming directly from you; the entire buyer/seller process is engineered so the *customer* voices the conclusion rather than the agent asserting it

Overall control split: **you control the process, the customer controls the decisions.** Most agents fail because they have no process at all ("winging it"), then try to compensate with a harder close — which reads as taking the decision away from the customer and triggers exactly the distancing the whole system is designed to avoid.

## 11. The Ten-Step Buyer Process

Designed to remove buyers' four core fears before they ever start looking at homes, so that when they find something they like, they have a "green light" rather than "thinking about it." The four fears: losing the home (emotional/impulsive), missing something better (analytical), paying too much, and something being wrong with it (usually resolved via inspection contingency or a pre-inspected listing). The two most common: missing something, and paying too much.

The ten steps: (1) Greeting — three-step greeting at first contact; (2) Meeting — physically move to a fresh space to meet, since a first-meeting space carries residual nervous energy; (3) Buyer Interview — foundational questions first (what they currently own/rent and why), with visible note-taking ("the power of the pad") signaling genuine listening; (4) Buyer Packet — a prepared packet (area maps, relevant websites, mortgage application, closing costs brochure, sample contract, agency disclosure, personal brochure) that itself builds trust through reciprocity and positions the agent as a professional; (5) The Funnel Process — deliberately start with *everything* that could work (all MLS listings, new construction, FSBOs, foreclosures) and eliminate down, rather than the common reverse pattern of buyers self-selecting a small batch off the internet and asking the agent to show just those (a "process of selection," which is slower and leaves buyers perpetually anxious they're missing something); (6) Scale of 1-10 — ask buyers to rate their current urgency/readiness and then "what would need to happen to be a 10," writing down every condition named (this produces a literal checklist of what must occur before the deal can close, useful for both agent and buyer); (7) Dream Home Exercise — each buyer independently lists home features and stars their top three non-negotiables, surfacing and reconciling disagreement between co-buyers; (8) Whats and Whys — for each stated "what" (feature), dig into the underlying "why" (benefit), because buyers ultimately buy their whys, not their whats (e.g., "golf course" often really means "view and open space," which many other property types can also deliver — searching MLS on the *whys* rather than the literal *whats* surfaces better-fit homes); (9) Cash or Loan? — financing discussed deliberately late, once the buyer is in a confident decision-making rhythm, not upfront where it would introduce confusion; includes the "1% = 10%" rule of thumb (each 1-point rise in interest rate cuts buying power by roughly 10% at a fixed payment) and options for price/loan mismatches (give up a "why," bring more resources, or "drive till you qualify"); (10) What Happens Next? — sets expectations before showings begin: confirm nothing's been missed, review current market conditions (including framing devices like the "cream puff" story — everyone wants a great home in great condition at a great price, so those homes sell fast, meaning buyers should act quickly when they find one — and the "85% perfect home" permission-giving story, since no home is ever perfect), preview what the closing signals ("doing the dance") will look like, and revisit the five negotiating points before any actual negotiation begins.

**Showing property** best practices: bring an area map, bring active/sold comps for context, plan the route to pass "big values" (schools, parks, employment centers) that support "small values" (the homes themselves), park across the street (better view than a driveway), enter first for safety and to observe buyers' pupil dilation as an involuntary interest signal, watch body language for "doing the dance" signals (sitting down, walking through twice, mentally placing furniture), and discuss any home again before leaving if there's doubt. To reduce buyer's remorse after a hot, fast "we'll take it": encourage multiple walkthroughs, seeing every room, taking the floor plan, viewing privately (not alongside other buyers at an open house), reviewing photos afterward, and reading the seller disclosure before writing.

**Soft closing questions** — only asked once a buyer is visibly in the "desire" stage (of curiosity → interest → desire → commitment), never earlier. Examples: "Is this one of your top three?" "Would you rate this 85% or better?" "Can you see yourself living here?" "Shall we see if we can buy this?" (toward strategy) vs. "Shall we make sure we don't lose this?" (from strategy). The **scratch-pad solution**: when a buyer loves a home but wants to "think about it," chunk the single big scary decision into the five smaller negotiating decisions (price, terms, dates, inclusions/exclusions, contingencies) on a blank sheet in a pretend-state ("if you were to buy this...") — roughly half the time this converts the buyer on the spot; the other half, it still produces clarity and a same-day or next-day follow-up appointment. The **auction solution** (for buyers leaving town, common in resort markets): after scratch-padding, ask a series of ascending pretend-state price points ("if you could buy it for $X, would you?") to find the buyer's actual comfort ceiling, useful for constructing a below-ask offer that's still likely to open real negotiation.

## 12. The Sixteen-Step Seller Process and Pricing Strategy

The book's framing: "Ninjas don't list houses, we list sellers" — the single biggest lever on a successful sale isn't price or location, it's whether the seller can make good decisions. Four factors used to evaluate a seller before taking the listing: **expectation** (realistic or not, and coachable), **motivation** (do they have a real "gap" and timetable), **cooperation** (are they a team player or adversarial), **communication** (open/honest and responsive, or closed with hidden agendas). If these aren't present, the book explicitly recommends passing on the listing rather than taking on an unwinnable assignment.

Sixteen steps, condensed: (1) **Prelisting interview** — a scripted phone questionnaire (20+ questions) run *before* the in-person meeting, starting with foundational/prior-learning questions (address, why selling, timeline, description of the home) before moving to sensitive ones (asking price, loan balance, what they want in an agent, whether they're interviewing others) — deliberately avoids "selling" over the phone; the agent just gathers and defers ("I'll show you your options when we meet"). Note: being the *first* agent interviewed carries real advantage — ~67% of sellers meet only one agent, and being first sets the comparison bar for anyone else they do see. (2) **Prelisting package** — sent/delivered ahead of the in-person meeting (marketing plan, market info, credentials, required documents); decide one-call (fast, price-clear-from-phone, often Power/Party sellers, likely to be shopping other agents concurrently) vs. two-call (unique/hard-to-price home, or Peace/Perfection sellers who want more time, generally not shopping competitors) process upfront and communicate which one you're using. (3) **Property walkthrough** — build rapport, review/confirm the phone interview details, further qualify the sellers and the house. (4) **What are we selling?** — the wholesale-vs-retail framing (same logic as selling a used car: cosmetic fixes sell faster and for more) used to non-directively ask what condition changes the seller wants to make, without the agent prescribing and thereby owning the outcome. (5) **Other properties to sell?** — a simple, easily-skipped question that regularly surfaces additional listings (case example: a rookie agent's very first listing call surfaced 18 additional properties this way). (6) **The calendar** — work backward from the seller's target move-in date to derive a real listing/contract deadline, using consequence questions ("if it's not sold by X, what will you do?") to build motivation and, if the seller claims no urgency, a pretend-state question ("if you could wave a magic wand and move whenever, when would that be?") to manufacture a working date anyway. (7-9) Review of the prelisting package, answering questions about it, and the qualifying question "do you feel we're qualified to market your home?" — engineered so sellers answer "yes" or "yes, but..." (never flatly no, since a prelisting package alone already differentiates from ~80% of agents who don't provide one); "yes, but" concerns are acknowledged, written down, and *all* surfaced before any are answered (never take the bait on price mid-list), followed by a three-outcome framing ("you hire me / you don't / I tell you honestly if I can't help you") that both reverses need-power dynamics (lower need = more power in the negotiation) and creates a graceful, honest exit if the listing is unwinnable. (10-14) **Pricing** — presented general-to-specific (market → price range → neighborhood → the home), visually (via tools like scattergrams plotting price against square footage, with a trend/"fair market value" line), Socratically (seller participates rather than receiving a seminar), and reduced to just five determinants of value (price, location, size, condition, amenities) rather than an overwhelming stack of raw MLS sheets — engineered so the *seller* ends up stating the price, not the agent. A **walk-away price** should be set in advance: below it, the listing becomes "mission impossible," and the agent should exercise the three-outcome framing from step 9 rather than take an unsellable overpriced listing (which drains marketing dollars, energy, and reputation). (15) **On time and Plan B** — pretend-state, consequence-based questions to pressure-test whether a stated price is actually acceptable ("if we had a contract on the table right now for $X, what would you do?" — used to genuinely call a seller's bluff when a too-high price is being used to sabotage a sale one spouse secretly doesn't want). (16) **The next 72 hours** — pre-framing sellers for the very real possibility of an excellent early offer, so they don't feel shortchanged or assume the agent under-delivered marketing if a strong offer arrives almost immediately (sideline buyers who've seen everything on market jump on anything genuinely new).

**Pricing on the bridge**: buyers and portals search in price bands (e.g., $300-350K); a home priced at $299,900 misses the $300-350K search bucket entirely. Pricing exactly on a natural search "bridge" (e.g., $300,000 flat) captures both adjacent search bands — cited research: homes priced on the bridge get ~28% more showings. Conversely, buyers' agents should search *below* their buyer's stated floor, since studies found 12-22% of inventory sits deliberately just under a bridge.

**Dynamic pricing**: price not just to where the market currently is, but to where it's headed, accounting for seasonal buying patterns in a given market (illustrated via a scattergram/market-timing chart combination that got a stale, previously-overpriced listing re-priced correctly and sold quickly once seasonal demand data was factored in).

## 13. Change Management, Crucial Conversations, and Referral Systems

**Crucial conversations with sellers**: pricing and listing discussions often meet the three-part definition of a "crucial conversation" (from the book *Crucial Conversations*: high stakes, differing opinions, high emotion). Under that pressure, Power/Party personalities tend toward emotional outburst (anger, tears) and Peace/Perfection personalities tend toward silence/shutdown. Two levers to keep the conversation safe: make it *safe* (calm tone, nonanxious presence, explicit framing that agent and seller are on the same team against the market, which is the "bad guy") and make it *clear* (confusion, not disagreement, is usually the real driver of a stuck negotiation). Sellers typically move through five stages of change around their sale — denial, anger, sadness, acceptance, renewal — sometimes in minutes, sometimes over months; the agent's job is to be a "step-down transformer," absorbing and lowering the emotional voltage rather than amplifying it (the "step-up transformer" is the opposite failure mode — someone who magnifies small issues into high drama).

**Your next transaction is embedded in this one** — the book's closing operating principle for referral generation, built on the same 15-20% transaction-rate math introduced in Section 1: any given twenty-neighbor cluster around a new listing will statistically produce three to four transactions in the coming year on its own, and a new listing typically accelerates neighbors' own latent moving intentions ("maybe we should do something too"). The recommended system: within a day of a new sign going up, personally visit roughly twenty neighboring homes face-to-face with a brochure and a scripted, value-framed introduction ("as part of my service to my sellers, I let the neighbors know... we want you to associate a face with the name on the sign") — an embedded suggestion intended to make the agent top-of-mind every time that neighbor later drives past the sign. Paired with this: "bring your A game" even on listings expected to sell fast in a hot market (full staging, professional photography, a proper counter display) — because neighbors and future sellers will walk through that home too, and cutting corners because a fast sale seems assured directly undercuts the next referral this same listing would otherwise generate.

The book's overarching closing frame for the whole system: incremental, compounding daily actions (the Ninja Nine, the flow system, the structured buyer/seller processes) — not big dramatic gestures — are what produce outsized, durable results over time.
