# What Every Real Estate Investor Needs to Know About Cash Flow — Framework Reference

*Pure framework. No company-specific application. Pull from here when building skills, advising on real estate investment/development strategy, or structuring deals.*
*Book: "What Every Real Estate Investor Needs to Know About Cash Flow... And 36 Other Key Financial Measures" — Frank Gallinelli*

---

## 1. Foundational Framework: The Four Ways to Make Money in Real Estate

The book's core premise: an investor doesn't buy a physical property, they buy its anticipated income stream. Every other concept in the book branches off this idea. There are exactly four mechanisms by which an income property generates a return, and any given deal will deliver some blend of the four (never necessarily all in equal measure):

- **Cash Flow** — money in minus money out over a period, regardless of source or category.
  - Formula: Cash In − Cash Out = Cash Flow
  - Worked example: a six-unit building rents two units at $800/mo, two at $900, two at $1,000 (gross $64,800/yr). Mortgage payments total $33,600/yr; taxes, insurance, maintenance, water, and supplies total $22,975/yr. Total outflows $56,575. Cash flow = $64,800 − $56,575 = **$8,225 positive**.
- **Appreciation** — growth in the property's value between purchase and resale.
  - Formula: Future Resale Price − Original Purchase Price = Appreciation
  - Worked example: bought for $1,000,000, sold five years later for $1,450,000 → appreciation of $450,000. Revenue growth (especially net revenue) is what drives appreciation — more income stream to sell commands a higher price.
- **Loan Amortization** — the equity build-up from paying down mortgage principal, effectively funded by the tenants' rent.
  - Formula: Annual Debt Service − Interest Paid = Amortization
  - Worked example: $18,000 in annual mortgage payments, $15,000 of which is interest → $3,000 of amortization (principal paydown) for the year.
- **Tax Shelter** — the property's ability to shield some (or all) of its own income, and sometimes other income, from taxation, principally through the deductibility of mortgage interest and depreciation (a non-cash deduction).
  - Path: Income − Operating Expenses = NOI; then NOI − Mortgage Interest − Depreciation = Taxable Income.
  - Because depreciation is a paper deduction, taxable income is frequently much lower than actual cash flow — sometimes even negative, which can shelter income from other sources too.

The recurring mantra used throughout the book: **decisions to buy, hold, or sell should be driven by the numbers and the income stream, never by emotional attachment to the physical asset.**

## 2. Gathering and Verifying the Data (Due Diligence)

Before any calculation is meaningful, the investor needs accurate raw data — sellers routinely present numbers that need to be tested, not accepted.

**Property-related verification steps:**
- Read the actual leases (not just the seller's summary) — check rent amounts, terms, renewal options.
- Check the property tax bill directly; watch for expiring abatements or a "phased-in" reassessment that will raise future taxes even if current taxes look normal.
- Spot-check utility bills directly with the utility company.
- Ask to see the relevant pages of the seller's tax return (Schedule E for an individual owner, or the entity's own return) — owners rarely overstate income or understate expenses to the IRS, so this tends to be a reliable check.
- Put the seller's representations about leases and rent schedule into the purchase contract itself, with language that the warranty survives closing — this materially changes how carefully sellers describe their own numbers.

**Market-related data:**
- Comparable sales (limited value for income property compared to home sales, but the ratio of price to gross rent across several comps — the gross rent multiplier — can reveal a pattern).
- Lease rates and operating expense benchmarks from commercial brokers and property managers.
- Local capitalization rates from brokers, the Appraisal Institute, or data services (e.g., realtyrates.com).

**The APOD (Annual Property Operating Data) form** is the standard real estate income-and-expense statement, structured top-down: Gross Scheduled Income → less Vacancy & Credit Allowance → Gross Operating Income → less itemized Operating Expenses → Net Operating Income.

**Reconstructing a seller's numbers — the diagnostic method:** compute each expense line as a percentage of GOI, then compare those ratios against several truly comparable properties (same property type/class, same market). The APOD's real value isn't answering questions, it's raising the right ones:
- An expense category present on comparable properties but absent from the subject property (e.g., a common-area electric meter, lawn/snow removal, trash removal, advertising, accounting) signals something is missing or being concealed, not that the owner is more efficient.
- An expense ratio unusually low compared to comps (e.g., repairs at 1.3% vs. a 6–7% norm) is just as suspicious as one unusually high — it can indicate deferred maintenance or a one-time explanation (e.g., a recent remodel) that will not recur.
- Reconstructing Property A in the book's running example moved it from a seller-stated NOI of $13,424 down to a realistic $11,322 once repairs were normalized, electricity corrected, and missing categories (accounting, advertising, lawn/snow, trash) were added in. Total operating expenses ended up at 39.21% of GOI, consistent with the 37–42% range shown by the comparable properties.

**Rule of thumb:** total operating expenses in the high-30s to low-40s percent of GOI is a common range for small residential income property, but there is no universal fixed rule — always benchmark against truly comparable local properties rather than a fixed percentage.

## 3. Income Measures: Gross Scheduled Income, Vacancy & Credit Loss, Gross Operating Income

These three terms form the top of every income analysis, always in the same order.

**Gross Scheduled Income (GSI)**, also called Potential Gross Income — the property's maximum possible annual rent if every unit were rented and every dollar collected.
- Formula: GSI = Total contract rent for occupied space + Total potential (market) rent for vacant space, for the year in question.
- Worked example: a four-unit building with three occupied units generating $20,000 + $20,000 + $22,000, plus a vacant fourth unit with an estimated market rent of $22,000 → GSI = $84,000.
- When rents change mid-year or a unit turns over, GSI must be built up month by month for each unit and then summed (e.g., a unit renting at $1,000/mo for 6 months and $1,200/mo for the other 6 contributes $13,200 for the year, not a flat $12,000 or $14,400).

**Vacancy and Credit Loss** (also "vacancy and credit allowance") — the estimated rental income lost to unoccupied space or unpaid rent.
- Formula: Vacancy & Credit Loss ($) = GSI × estimated vacancy % 
- Rule of thumb: 3–6% is a common range for a stabilized residential property in the absence of hard local data; commercial space often warrants a higher allowance since vacant retail/office space can take much longer to re-lease than an apartment. A newly built project in initial lease-up should assume a materially higher vacancy allowance.
- Counter-intuitive rule of thumb: a property with historically near-zero vacancy has probably been under-priced relative to the market, not run perfectly.

**Gross Operating Income (GOI)**, also called Effective Gross Income (EGI) — the income actually collected.
- Formula: GOI = GSI − Vacancy and Credit Loss
- Worked example: a 12-unit property with mixed rent rolls and two units mid-year turnover works out to GSI of $211,350; applying a 7% vacancy and credit allowance ($14,795) yields GOI of $196,555. The 7% figure was justified, not assumed — the book walked the actual known vacancy months for two specific units and confirmed they totaled just over 5% of GSI, so 7% was a reasonable, slightly conservative allowance.

## 4. Net Operating Income and Expense Ratios

**Net Operating Income (NOI)** is the single most important number in the book — the property's income after vacancy/credit loss and all legitimate operating expenses, before any consideration of financing, income taxes, or capital expenditures.
- Formula (top-down): GSI − Vacancy & Credit Loss = GOI; GOI − Operating Expenses = NOI.
- What counts as an operating expense: insurance, real estate taxes, repairs and maintenance, utilities, supplies, management fees, advertising, trash removal, and similar costs necessary to keep the property producing income.
- What does **not** count: mortgage payments (principal or interest), depreciation, and capital improvements/additions. These are all "below the NOI line."
- Worked example: GSI $100,000, 3% vacancy ($3,000) → GOI $97,000; operating expenses $35,000 → NOI $62,000.
- Rule of thumb (with a caution): some investors use a flat 10% cap rate as a mental gut-check on a seller's asking price (multiply NOI by 10 by simply adding a zero) purely to decide whether a deal merits a closer look — never as a basis for an actual buy/sell decision, since actual local cap rates commonly range from 8–12% and have been observed as low as 5% or as high as 18%.

**Operating Expense Ratio** — the proportion of collected income consumed by a given expense (or by all expenses combined).
- Formula: Operating Expense Ratio = Operating Expense ÷ GOI
- Applies to a single line item or to the total. Always examine individual-line ratios, not just the total — a normal-looking total can mask a red flag hidden inside one specific category (see Section 2).

**Price, Income, and Expenses per Unit** — a simple, dated but still-used shortcut, generally meaningful only for residential properties with fairly uniform units.
- Formulas: Price per unit = Price ÷ number of units; Income per unit = GSI ÷ number of units; Expenses per unit = Operating Expenses ÷ number of units.
- Worked example: a 30-unit building at $1,000/unit/month, $210,000 annual opex, offered at $1.5 million → $50,000 price/unit, $12,000 income/unit, $7,000 expense/unit.

**Price, Income, and Expenses per Square Foot** — the commercial-property analogue, more meaningful than per-unit because square footage has a consistent physical definition (whereas "units" in a shopping center might mix a flower shop with an anchor tenant).
- Formulas: same structure, but divide by Gross Building Area (GBA) or Net Rentable Area, whichever convention the local market uses — be explicit about which one, since they aren't interchangeable.
- Worked example: 8,000 sq ft GBA, $128,000 GSI, $56,000 opex, offered at $720,000 → $90/sq ft price, $16/sq ft income, $7/sq ft expenses.

## 5. The Time Value of Money Toolkit

Every discounting, compounding, and mortgage calculation in the book reduces to four variables: **Present Value (PV)**, **Future Value (FV)**, the **periodic interest/discount rate (i)**, and the **number of periods (n)**. Know any three, and the fourth can always be solved for (by formula, table, or spreadsheet function).

- **Simple Interest** — the rate applies only to the original principal, never to accumulated interest.
  - Interest = Principal × Rate × Time
  - Amount = Principal × [1 + (Rate × Time)]
  - Example: $1,000 at 10% simple interest for 30 months (2.5 years) → Amount = 1,000 × [1 + (0.10 × 2.5)] = **$1,250**.

- **Compound Interest** — the rate applies to principal plus all previously accumulated interest.
  - Amount (FV) = Principal × (1 + periodic rate) ^ number of periods
  - Example: $1,000 at 12% compounded annually for 3 years → 1,000 × 1.12³ = **$1,404.93**.
  - More frequent compounding always yields more total interest for a given nominal rate (the "APY" concept — a nominal 6% compounded monthly yields an effective 6.17%).

- **Rule of 72s** — a fast mental estimate for doubling time.
  - Years to double (approx.) = 72 ÷ annual growth rate
  - Example: property values growing at 8%/year → roughly 9 years to double. (Transposable: 6 years to double implies roughly 12% annual growth.)

- **Present Value of a single Future Cash Flow** — the reverse of compounding.
  - PV = FV ÷ (1 + i)^n
  - Example: $200,000 expected in 5 years, discounted at 11% → PV ≈ **$118,690**.
  - Choosing the discount rate is the hardest and most consequential step: it should equal the return achievable from a comparable-risk alternative investment, since that's the true opportunity cost of tying up the money here instead.

- **The four core annuity/compounding functions** (solvable via Excel's `NPER`, `RATE`, `FV`, `PV`, `PMT`, or via financial tables): given any three of PV, FV, rate, and number of periods, solve for the fourth. Real estate applications include: how many years until a property doubles at an assumed appreciation rate; what appreciation rate is required to double in a target number of years; and what a property was likely worth N years ago given today's value and an assumed historical growth rate.

- **Discounting a lease (or any structured payment stream) to compare proposals** — a practical negotiating application: build each competing rent proposal (including any expense pass-throughs like a tenant's share of property tax increases) as a monthly cash flow stream, discount it at an appropriate annual rate (converted to monthly), and compare the resulting present values directly — this exposes which concessions actually cost the landlord real money and which are effectively free once discounted.

## 6. Valuation Measures: Capitalization Rate, Net Income Multiplier, Gross Rent Multiplier

These three measures estimate a property's value at a single point in time (not over a holding period), and all three are market-driven — they require knowing what similar properties are actually trading for.

**Capitalization Rate (Cap Rate)** — the ratio between NOI and value; the most widely used income-property valuation shorthand.
- Formula: Cap Rate = NOI ÷ Value (transposable to Value = NOI ÷ Cap Rate, or NOI = Value × Cap Rate)
- Worked examples: a $250,000 purchase with $30,000 NOI is a 12% cap rate deal. If the market cap rate for small office buildings is 11% and a subject building has $66,000 NOI, its value estimate is $600,000.
- Higher cap rate → lower value (for a fixed NOI), because the buyer is demanding a higher rate of return for the same dollar of income.
- Rule of thumb: historically cap rates gravitate around 8–12%, with observed extremes as low as 5% and as high as 18% depending on property type, market, and era — always verify current local cap rates rather than assume a number.
- **Buyer vs. seller bias**: buyers tend to prefer capitalizing the trailing 12 months' NOI (lower, more conservative, justifies a lower price); sellers tend to prefer capitalizing the next 12 months' *projected* NOI at a cap rate slightly below market (both moves push the estimated value up). A worked example in the book showed a seller's approach (next year's GOI, last year's expenses, and a cap rate 0.5 points below market) inflating the implied value by roughly $75,000 versus a buyer's more defensible approach using trailing GOI and the true market cap rate.

**Net Income Multiplier (NIM)** — the reciprocal of the cap rate; a quick mental-math valuation shortcut.
- Formula: NIM = 1 ÷ Cap Rate; Value = NIM × NOI
- Example: an 11.5% market cap rate implies an NIM of about 8.7. A property with $88,000 NOI at a 9.09 NIM (11% cap rate) values at roughly $800,000.

**Gross Rent Multiplier (GRM)** — values a property as a multiple of its *gross* rental income, ignoring expenses entirely.
- Formula: GRM = Market Value ÷ GSI (transposable to Value = GRM × GSI)
- Worked example: five comparable apartment sales produced GRMs ranging 6.09–7.42, averaging about 6.86. Applied to a subject property with $150,000 GSI, this suggests a value range of roughly $915,000–$1,110,000, centering near $1,030,000.
- Rule of thumb: a GRM below 4 would be surprising, one above 10 would be alarming — reasonable ranges are highly market- and property-type-dependent.
- GRM's advantage over cruder cash-flow shortcuts is that it partially offsets the risk of a seller temporarily deferring maintenance to prop up a single year's cash flow, since it's driven by gross rent, not net income, benchmarked against truly comparable properties.

## 7. Cash Flow and Taxable Income

From NOI, the analysis splits into two separate branches that only reconnect at the very bottom line (cash flow after taxes).

**Taxable Income branch:**
- Formula: NOI − Mortgage Interest (all loans) − Depreciation (real property + capital additions) − Amortization of Points and Closing Costs + Interest Earned = Taxable Income
- Only the *interest* portion of debt service is deductible, never principal.
- Depreciation: currently 27.5 years for residential property, 39 years for nonresidential, applied only to the building value (never land). The "half-month convention" allows only half a month's depreciation in the month the asset is placed in service and in the month it's disposed of.
- Loan points and acquisition closing costs must be amortized (deducted ratably) over the loan's term — or over a shorter balloon period if the loan is structured with one. Any unamortized balance becomes fully deductible in the year the loan is paid off (sale or refinance).

**Cash Flow branch:**
- Formula: NOI − Debt Service (full P&I) − Capital Additions + Loan Proceeds (new financing, not the original purchase loan) + Interest Earned = Cash Flow Before Taxes (CFBT)
- Then: CFBT − Income Tax Liability = Cash Flow After Taxes (CFAT), where the tax liability = Taxable Income × the investor's marginal tax rate.

**Full worked case study** (a $1,250,000 strip shopping center, three mortgages, 72% of value allocated to the depreciable building, 28% marginal tax bracket): Year 1 NOI of $160,860 flowed to a Taxable Income of $71,037 (after mortgage interest, depreciation, and amortized points across all three loans) and separately to a CFBT of $72,391 (after full debt service on all three loans). Applying the 28% bracket to taxable income produced a $19,890 tax liability, leaving CFAT of $52,501. By year 5, rising NOI pushed taxable income to $92,052 and CFAT to $60,086 — CFBT grew faster than the tax liability, so the after-tax bottom line kept improving year over year.

**Key dynamic — mortgage "seasoning":** early in a loan's life, most of each payment is interest (fully deductible); as the loan seasons, the interest portion shrinks and the principal portion grows. Debt service (and therefore CFBT) can stay flat while taxable income silently rises — because there's less deductible interest to offset NOI. This is not a flaw, it just means the investor is being taxed on the benefit of the tenants paying down the mortgage; it's a reason to project taxable income (not just cash flow) years into the future, and potentially to plan a strategic refinance to extract cash funded by newly-deductible interest.

## 8. Resale, Adjusted Basis, and Gain on Sale

**Rule of thumb underpinning this entire section:** "If it's not worth selling, then it's not worth buying" — always run tomorrow's resale numbers today, even for a property you intend to hold indefinitely.

**Forecasting the resale price:** capitalize the projected NOI in the year of sale at an *assumed future* cap rate — conventionally set a bit higher than today's prevailing market cap rate, as a conservative cushion against the fact that the future rate is unknowable.
- Formula: Estimated Future Value = Projected NOI (sale year) ÷ Assumed Future Cap Rate
- Worked example (from the case study): Year 5 NOI of $174,330 capitalized at an assumed 12% future cap rate → estimated sale price of roughly $1,453,000.

**Sale Proceeds Before Taxes:**
- Formula: Selling Price − Costs of Sale − Mortgage Payoff(s) = Sale Proceeds Before Taxes
- Rule of thumb: total costs of sale (brokerage + legal) commonly estimated around 7% of the selling price, though this is not universal — atypical transactions (environmental issues, etc.) can push it higher.
- Continuing the case study: $1,453,000 sale price − $101,710 costs of sale − $630,184 (1st mortgage payoff) − $61,024 (2nd) − $10,000 (3rd, interest-only, no principal paydown) = **$650,082 before-tax sale proceeds**, against an original $436,400 cash investment.

**Adjusted Basis** — needed to calculate the taxable gain; not the same as original purchase price.
- Formula: Original Purchase Price + Cumulative Capital Improvements + Costs of Sale − Cumulative Depreciation (real estate) − Cumulative Depreciation (capital improvements) = Adjusted Basis
- Case study: $1,250,000 purchase + $101,710 costs of sale − $113,462 cumulative depreciation = $1,238,248 adjusted basis at sale.

**Gain on Sale:**
- Formula: Selling Price − Adjusted Basis = Gain on Sale
- Case study: $1,453,000 − $1,238,248 = $214,752 gain.
- Tax treatment (as of the book's writing, subject to change): the portion of the gain equal to cumulative depreciation taken is "recaptured" and taxed at an ordinary-income-linked rate (capped, historically around 25%); the remainder is taxed at the applicable long-term capital gains rate. Any unamortized loan points can be deducted in full in the year of sale. In the case study, after-tax sale proceeds came to $604,644.
- Rule of thumb: holding a property past the 12-month mark before selling typically qualifies the gain for long-term capital gains treatment — a materially lower rate than short-term/ordinary income, so timing a sale around this threshold can be worth thousands of dollars.

## 9. Return Measures I: Simple, Point-in-Time Metrics

These are the oldest, easiest-to-calculate return measures — useful as quick screens, but each has a structural blind spot (usually: ignoring the time value of money, ignoring the resale, or looking at only a single year).

**Payback Period** ("bingo year") — years required to recover the original cash investment from cash flow.
- Example: $100,000 cash invested, $20,000/year positive cash flow → 5-year payback.
- Blind spot: treats a dollar received in year 5 as equal to a dollar received today; two deals with an identical 5-year payback can have very different real value if the timing of the cash within those years differs.

**Cash-on-Cash Return** (equity dividend rate) — a single year's cash flow expressed as a percentage of the cash invested.
- Formula: Cash-on-Cash Return = CFBT (usually) ÷ Cash Invested
- Example: $10,000 CFBT on a $100,000 cash investment → 10%.
- Popular because it produces an easy, intuitive comparison to alternatives like a CD or T-bill rate, but it only ever reflects one year and ignores everything else (resale, later years, financing structure changes).
- Mathematical breakdown case: when the cash invested is engineered down to $0 (e.g., a fully seller-financed deal), the formula divides by zero — there is no such thing as a "return" on an investment of nothing, because there was no investment (and no capital at risk).

**Return on Equity (ROE)** — two distinct methods, both using Cash Flow After Taxes as the numerator:
- Method 1 (traditional): ROE = CFAT ÷ Initial Cash Investment.
- Method 2 (current equity basis): ROE = CFAT ÷ (Current Resale Value − Current Mortgage Balance), i.e., using the investor's *actual* built-up equity rather than the original cash outlay.
- Insight unique to Method 2: projected forward across several years, ROE can start *declining* even while cash flow, property value, and total equity are all still rising — because equity (fueled by appreciation and accelerating mortgage paydown) is growing faster than cash flow is growing. A falling ROE is a signal to ask whether the capital tied up in that property would perform better refinanced out and redeployed, or through an outright sale and reinvestment in a larger or different property.

## 10. Return Measures II: Time-Value-Adjusted Metrics

These measures fold in the full holding period, the resale, and the timing of every cash flow — a materially more complete way to judge an investment's quality than the point-in-time or single-year measures above.

**Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis** — the present worth of an entire anticipated income stream.
- Method: discount each year's cash flow — including the final year's operating cash flow *combined with* the sale proceeds (the "reversion") — back to present value individually, at a chosen discount rate, then sum all the individual PVs.
- The sum of the discounted cash flows always represents what the investor's *cash outlay* is worth at the chosen rate of return: if the property is bought all-cash, this equals the whole property's value; if leveraged, it represents the value of the investor's equity investment specifically (since financing already reduced both the cash flows and the required cash outlay).
- Choosing the discount rate: it should reflect the return achievable on a similarly risky, comparable alternative investment — this is the opportunity cost being compensated for.
- Both magnitude *and* timing matter: two cash flow streams that sum to the identical total dollar amount can have very different present values purely because of when the money arrives (a stream weighted toward later years discounts to a materially lower PV).

**Net Present Value (NPV)** — the surplus (or shortfall) versus a required rate of return.
- Formula: NPV = PV of all future cash flows (via DCF) − Initial Cash Investment
- NPV > 0 → the investment is expected to outperform the target discount rate; NPV = 0 → it will exactly hit the target; NPV < 0 → it will fall short.
- Worked example: PV of future cash flows $76,474 against a $75,000 cash investment → NPV of $1,474 (a modestly positive result, i.e., an implied return a bit above the 11.5% discount rate used).

**Profitability Index** — closely related to NPV, but expressed as a ratio rather than a dollar difference.
- Formula: Profitability Index = PV of all future cash flows ÷ Initial Cash Investment
- An index of exactly 1.0 means the investment will hit its target rate of return precisely; above 1.0, it will beat the target; below 1.0, it will miss.
- Its distinct value over NPV: because it's a ratio, it is not sensitive to the size of the investment, making it the right tool for comparing two opportunities that require very different amounts of capital. Worked example: a $125,000 investment with a $135,000 PV of cash flows shows the larger NPV ($10,000 vs. $9,000), but a $75,000 investment with an $84,000 PV of cash flows shows the *higher* profitability index (1.12 vs. 1.08) — meaning it returns more per dollar invested, even though its absolute NPV is smaller.

**Internal Rate of Return (IRR)** — probably the most quoted and least understood return measure in real estate; it's the specific discount rate at which the PV of all future cash flows exactly equals the initial cash investment.
- Cannot realistically be solved by hand — it requires an iterative "successive approximations" search, done via spreadsheet function or financial calculator.
- IRR is the single measure most sensitive to *both* the magnitude and the timing of every cash flow (including the resale). Two properties with identical total cash returned over a holding period can have meaningfully different IRRs purely because of how early or late the biggest gains land.
- Powerful and sometimes counterintuitive finding: running IRR for a range of possible holding periods (sell after year 1, year 2, year 3...) can reveal that IRR *peaks* at some specific year and then declines afterward — even while annual cash flow and property value both continue rising every year. This happens because the biggest value-creation moves (e.g., an aggressive lease-up or rehab in the first few years of ownership) get discounted the least, since they occur earliest; later, smaller improvements get diluted by both their smaller size and the extra years of discounting. When IRR shows a clear peak, that year is the mathematically optimal sale point; if IRR is flat across a range of years instead, there's no single optimal exit year and the investor has flexibility on timing.
- Negative cash flow years complicate a standard IRR solve; the book's practical (if imprecise) workaround is to add the total of all projected negative cash flows to the initial investment and zero those years out — effectively assuming the negative amounts are funded on day one rather than as they occur. The more rigorous fix is Modified Internal Rate of Return (MIRR).

## 11. Financing and Lender Risk Measures

These ratios describe how a property looks specifically through a lender's eyes — how much cushion exists between income and obligations, and how much of the purchase price the lender is being asked to risk.

**Debt Coverage Ratio (DCR)** — the margin between NOI and the mortgage payment.
- Formula: DCR = NOI ÷ Annual Debt Service
- A DCR of 1.20 means the property generates 20% more net income than needed to service the debt.
- Rule of thumb: most lenders require a minimum DCR around 1.20 to finance an income property; any loan presentation should show projected DCR over several years, not just year one.

**Break-Even Ratio (BER)**, also called the Default Ratio — estimates how vulnerable the property is to default if rental income declines.
- Formula: BER = (Debt Service + Operating Expenses) ÷ GOI
- Rule of thumb: most lenders look for a BER of 85% or lower; in markets with unusually low occupancy, lenders may require an even tighter BER.

**Loan-to-Value Ratio (LTV)** — the loan amount as a percentage of the lesser of appraised value or actual selling price (never the higher of the two, even if the buyer negotiated what looks like a below-market deal).
- Formula: LTV = Loan Amount ÷ lesser of (Appraised Value, Selling Price)
- Rule of thumb: personal residences can reach LTVs near 80% conventionally (up to ~100% with PMI or government programs like FHA/VA); investment property lenders typically want more borrower equity at risk — 70–80% LTV is common, and it's not unusual to see requirements as low as 70%. The lower the LTV, the more negotiating leverage the borrower generally has on rate and terms.
- Rationale: a bigger equity stake gives the borrower a stronger disincentive to walk away in a downturn, and gives the lender a bigger buffer before a foreclosure would produce a loss.

**Maximum Loan Amount** — ties NOI, the lender's minimum DCR, and the loan's terms (via the annual mortgage constant, see Section 12) together into the largest loan a given property can actually support.
- Formula: Maximum Loan Amount = NOI ÷ Required DCR ÷ Annual Mortgage Constant
- A longer amortization term or a lower interest rate both lower the mortgage constant, which increases the maximum supportable loan; a lender's lower required DCR does the same. Comparing two lenders' offers requires normalizing all three factors, since a "worse" rate or DCR requirement can still produce a bigger loan if the amortization term is long enough.

**Rule of thumb on "no money down" deals:** since return on zero invested cash is mathematically undefined, a heavily leveraged or no-cash deal isn't a shortcut to a great return — it's usually a signal of higher debt service, higher default risk, and (if the seller is providing 100% of the financing) sometimes an inflated purchase price used to compensate the seller for taking on that financing risk.

## 12. Mortgage Mechanics

**Mortgage Constant** — the payment (monthly or annual) required per $1 of loan amount, for a given interest rate and amortization term; a single factor that captures both rate and term.
- Formula: Payment = Loan Amount × Mortgage Constant (for the applicable rate/term)
- Worked example: $275,000 loan, 30-year term, 6.875% → monthly constant of 0.00656929 → monthly payment ≈ $1,806.55.
- Rule of thumb: for a fixed rate, a longer amortization term always produces a lower mortgage constant (lower payment), because principal repayment is stretched further into the future.

**Outstanding Principal Balance / Balloon Payment** — the amount owed at any point mid-loan, useful for refinance/sale planning or for a loan structured with an early "balloon" payoff.
- Formula: Outstanding Balance = Remaining Payment Amount ÷ Mortgage Constant (for the rate and *remaining* term)
- Worked example: $1,000 monthly payments, 336 months (28 years) remaining, 6.25% → monthly constant 0.0063098 → outstanding balance ≈ $158,483.63.
- Practical note: at closing, a per diem interest adjustment is added to the last full-month balance to determine the exact mid-month payoff figure.

**Principal and Interest Split for a Given Payment** — how much of any individual payment goes to interest vs. principal.
- Formula: Interest portion = Outstanding Principal Balance × periodic rate; Principal portion = Payment amount − Interest portion.
- Over the life of a level-payment loan, the interest share steadily shrinks and the principal share steadily grows (a loan late in this process is described as "seasoned") — this is the mechanical driver behind the taxable-income dynamic described in Section 7.

**Points** — a lender fee representing prepaid interest, 1 point = 1% of the loan amount.
- Formula: Dollar Amount of Points = Loan Amount × Number of Points ÷ 100
- Points are amortized (deducted ratably) over the loan's stated term, or over a shorter balloon period if the loan is structured to come due early; any unamortized balance becomes fully deductible when the loan is paid off.
- Rule of thumb: points generally benefit the lender more than the borrower unless the loan stays in place long enough to pass the interest-savings break-even point (often several years, and always longer than the naive rate-difference math suggests once the time value of the up-front payment is accounted for) — keep points as low as possible unless there's high confidence the loan won't be refinanced or the property sold soon.

## 13. Property Tax Mechanics

Property tax is built from four linked components, useful both for budgeting a property's expenses and for sanity-checking (or appealing) an assessment.

- **Appraised Value** — the assessor's periodic estimate of market value (may go stale between revaluation cycles).
- **Assessment Ratio** — the percentage of appraised value actually subject to tax, set by the state or taxing district (can vary by property type — residential vs. commercial vs. farm).
- **Assessed Value** — Appraised Value × Assessment Ratio.
- **Tax Rate ("mill rate")** — the rate applied to assessed value, expressed per $1,000 of assessed value.

Formulas:
- Property Taxes = Assessed Value × Tax Rate
- Appraised Value = Assessed Value ÷ Assessment Ratio (the reverse-engineering formula, useful when only the tax bill is known)

Worked example: assessed value $234,500, mill rate 42.50 → annual property taxes of $9,966.25.

**Practical application:** for income property, back into the implied cap rate the assessor used (Cap Rate = NOI ÷ Appraised Value). If that implied cap rate is clearly out of step with the market's actual prevailing cap rate, that's the basis for an unemotional, numbers-driven assessment appeal — the book cites a real case where this exact method won a material reassessment reduction, because the assessor's own appraisal firm had used an erroneous cap rate.

## 14. Physical Measurement Standards

**Land measurement** — needed to compare price-per-area across parcels and to check zoning constraints tied to lot size.
- Core conversion: 1 acre = 43,560 square feet.
- Other standard conversions: 1 acre ≈ 0.4047 hectares (1 hectare ≈ 2.471 acres); 1 acre ≈ 4,046.86 square meters; 1 meter ≈ 3.28084 feet.
- "Front feet" (linear road frontage) matters for two practical reasons: municipalities often allocate infrastructure costs (e.g., sewer line installation) by frontage rather than lot depth, and retail-suitable land is priced partly on visible road exposure, not just total area.

**Building measurement** — commercial space is rented by the square foot, but "square foot" is not a single standardized concept:
- **Gross Building Area (GBA)** — the building's total floor area, measured from the outside of exterior walls, including elevator shafts, utility rooms, and basement space.
- **Usable Square Footage (USF)** — the actual space within a tenant's demising walls; what the tenant physically occupies.
- **Common Area** — GBA minus USF; the lobbies, corridors, restrooms, utility rooms, and elevator shafts that serve everyone but can't be individually rented.
- **Loss Ratio** — Common Area ÷ GBA; the percentage of the building that is unrentable common space.
- **Rentable Square Footage (RSF)** — USF plus a negotiated allocated share of the common area; this is the figure rent is actually charged against, and its definition is a lease-negotiation point, not a fixed geometric fact.
- Formula set: USF = GBA − Common Area; Loss Ratio = Common Area ÷ GBA; RSF = USF + allocated share of Common Area.
- Rule of thumb: RSF commonly runs 15–20% higher than USF, though this depends entirely on the building's specific proportions and the lease's negotiated common-area allocation.
- Worked example: a 10-level building at 60' × 80' per floor → GBA 48,000 sq ft; common area 8,640 sq ft → USF 39,360 sq ft; Loss Ratio 18%. A tenant occupying 4,000 sq ft USF with a 7%-of-common-area allocation gets roughly 605 additional sq ft, for an RSF of about 4,605 — at $20/RSF, that's $92,100 in annual rent, all driven by how the RSF definition was negotiated, not by the tenant's literal footprint.

## 15. Investor Judgment Calls and Cross-Cutting Rules of Thumb

A set of practical heuristics that recur across nearly every chapter of the book and are worth keeping together as a single checklist:

- **Never accept a single number in isolation.** A cap rate, a GRM, an expense ratio, or a cash-on-cash figure each capture only part of the picture; always cross-check several measures together, and always benchmark against genuinely comparable properties rather than a fixed rule.
- **Cap rates commonly run 8–12%,** with observed extremes from 5% to 18% — always source current local data (brokers, appraisers, or data services) rather than assume a number, since the "right" cap rate is entirely a function of time, place, and property type.
- **Watch for the buyer/seller NOI-year and cap-rate framing bias** — sellers are motivated to capitalize next year's (higher) projected NOI at a cap rate just below market; buyers should default to capitalizing trailing actual NOI at the true market rate, and independently verify any "forward-looking" numbers a seller presents.
- **Vacancy allowance:** 3–6% is typical for stabilized residential; go higher for new lease-up projects or commercial space; treat a historical vacancy rate near zero as a sign of under-market rent, not superior management.
- **Cost of sale:** roughly 7% (combined brokerage + legal) is a common planning estimate, subject to adjustment for atypical deal complexity.
- **The three lender gatekeeper ratios**, all checked before financing is approved: Debt Coverage Ratio ≥ ~1.20, Break-Even Ratio ≤ ~85%, Loan-to-Value ≤ ~70–80% for investment property (as opposed to up to ~100% for an owner-occupied residence with PMI or a government-backed loan).
- **Depreciation and amortized points/closing costs lower taxable income without touching cash flow** — expect taxable income to run below CFBT, especially early in ownership, and expect that gap to narrow over time as the mortgage "seasons" (less deductible interest, more non-deductible principal).
- **Run IRR (and, more broadly, any resale-dependent projection) across a range of possible holding periods**, not just a single assumed exit year — the optimal sale year is a specific, calculable point, not an assumption.
- **"You can't lose money making a profit"** — a positive NPV or a profitability index above 1.0 both simply confirm the deal is expected to outperform the specific discount rate used; always be explicit about what discount rate (i.e., what achievable alternative return) is being used as the yardstick.
- **"If it's not worth selling, then it's not worth buying"** — always model the eventual resale (value, tax, and net proceeds) as part of underwriting a purchase, even for a property intended to be held indefinitely.
- **No-money-down financing produces a mathematically undefined return** (division by zero on invested capital) and typically comes bundled with higher debt service, higher default risk, and/or an inflated purchase price — it is not a shortcut around the fundamentals of capital at risk.
