# Sales & Closing Techniques

*Sources: Sabri Suby, Alex Hormozi, cold outreach sales training reel (FRAME framework)*

---

## Core Philosophy

Sales is not about convincing or pushing. It is about pulling. The prospect should arrive at the decision themselves — your job is to ask the right questions, remove resistance, and guide them to a conclusion they already sense is correct.

The higher the ticket price, the more you pull. The lower the ticket, the more push is required. High-value sales calls are about earning trust, uncovering the real problem, and making inaction feel more dangerous than the investment.

**Warning:** These tactics are powerful and can be misused. Only apply them when you genuinely believe the product or service will transform the prospect's life. Never use them to sell something that doesn't deliver.

---

## FRAME — Call Opening & Discovery Structure

*Source: cold outreach sales training reel.*

A 5-step structure for how to open and run a call, before any of the closing tactics below come into play. Where the rest of this file covers what to do once you're deep in the conversation, FRAME covers how to start it and get to the real problem fast.

- **F — Frame.** Set the expectations for the call up front: what it's for, how long it'll take, what happens at the end. Removes ambiguity that otherwise creates defensiveness.
- **R — Read.** Assume, don't ask. Come in having done your homework on their situation and state it back to them, rather than opening with generic discovery questions. Reading the situation confidently builds authority faster than interrogating for it.
- **A — Admit.** Lay out the problem as you see it, and get them to say it out loud themselves. This is the same mechanism as Hormozi's "admission" below (see Pull, Don't Push and the Power Framework) — once they state the problem in their own words, they own it.
- **M — Match.** Match your solution directly to the problem they just admitted to. Not a generic pitch, the specific fix for the specific thing they said.
- **E — Engage.** Ask if they'd do it, if it adds up for them. A soft, logical close, not a hard ask, just testing whether the match they just heard makes sense to them.

**Where this fits with the rest of this file:** FRAME is the opening shell, Admit maps onto the "admission" concept in Hormozi's framework and Pull Don't Push in Sabri Suby's, and Engage is a lightweight version of the Directional Decision Frame. Use FRAME to structure the call, then drop into the specific tactics below once objections surface.

## The 7 Tactics

### Tactic 1: Kill the Comfort Zone

Most salespeople try to keep prospects comfortable throughout the call. This is the wrong objective.

**The truth:** Transformations don't happen inside comfort zones. Every client who made a life-changing decision did so at a moment of discomfort — when they were pushed to confront the real issue.

- Ask the difficult questions. Go deep. Don't navigate around the painful truth.
- Ask for the sale multiple times. Don't stop at the first "no" or the first hesitation.
- 10 minutes of discomfort on a call can change 10 years of someone's life.
- Prospects will fight to stay in their comfort zone even when what they're fighting for is keeping them stuck.

**Only valid when:** You have done your qualification work and you genuinely, whole-heartedly believe this is the best solution for this person. Then you have no excuse NOT to push.

---

### Tactic 2: Radical Transparency

Never hide the negatives. Bring them up yourself, before the prospect can raise them as objections.

**Why it works:**
- Pre-handles objections: turns live resistance into resolved concerns
- Builds instant trust: "this person isn't commission-hungry, they're being honest"
- Stops comparison shopping: "this is the only person shooting me straight"
- Makes you the trusted advisor, not another salesperson

**How to apply:**
- Identify the 1–3 biggest drawbacks of your offer (long contract, requires client effort, time to results)
- Raise them early — within the first few minutes
- Frame them with reasoning: "This is a 12-month contract. SEO is a long game. If you're not ready to commit 12 months, I'd recommend Google Ads instead — it'll get you results faster."

Example from Sabri: When selling SEO, he would lead with the 12-month contract. Prospects who might have objected later accepted it on the front end because the reasoning was logical and honest. It disarmed the objection and built rapport simultaneously.

**The psychology:** When you're completely upfront about one drawback, the prospect's brain assumes there are no hidden surprises. Their defense system lowers.

---

### Tactic 3: Pull, Don't Push

Pushing creates resistance. Pulling creates desire.

- The harder you push, the less people want it. A voice in the back of their head says: "Why are they working so hard to sell this? It must not be good."
- The higher the price point, the more the pull dynamic applies.
- Ferrari doesn't sell to you — Ferrari qualifies you. You justify yourself to them.

**The pull method:**
1. Ask strategic questions to unearth the root problem
2. Let the prospect arrive at the decision themselves
3. You guide them to the "admission" — the moment they acknowledge their current approach isn't working

**Key questions to open with:**
- "What led you to book this call in the first place?"
- "How long have you been trying to solve this problem?"
- "What solutions have you already tried, and why do you think they didn't work?"

Once you understand all that, you can say: *"Based on everything you've told me, I think you'd be a good fit for what we do."* — not "This is perfect for you" before you even know their situation.

**The admission:** Get them to state out loud that their current situation is not working. Once they say it, they own it. The decision to change becomes their idea, not a sale you pushed on them.

---

### Tactic 4: Cost of Inaction

Prospects fear spending money. But the real danger is NOT taking action.

Reframe the entire conversation: the question is not "can I afford this?" but "what does staying where I am cost me?"

**The framework:**
- Assign a specific dollar cost to inaction over time
- "Every year you stay where you are, this problem costs you $X"
- "What's more expensive — our fee, or $2M in lost revenue over the next 2 years?"
- Time is the enemy: the longer they wait, the bigger the problem grows

**The psychology:** This shifts the risk in the prospect's mind. Your offer stops being the risky option — inaction becomes the risky option.

**How to apply on a call:**
- Once you understand their current situation and target, quantify the gap
- Assign a time dimension: what does staying stuck cost per month/year?
- Then position your offer against that cost, not against the absolute price

---

### Tactic 5: Tonality

People make subconscious judgments about you within 2–5 seconds of hearing your voice. The words you choose matter far less than how you say them.

**The authority tonality:**
- Slower pace
- Flatter delivery — minimal upward inflection
- Measured, calm, deliberate
- Like someone in a position of authority (customs officer, doctor, consultant) — not an enthusiastic salesperson

**What NOT to do:**
- Fast, upbeat, enthusiastic opening → immediately signals "salesperson" → defenses go up
- Upward inflection at the end of statements (sounds uncertain or eager)
- Talking too much too fast

**The frame shift:** You are not there to sell — you are there to assess whether this person is a good fit. That posture changes your entire tonality naturally.

Sabri's example: calling as a cold caller initially got resistance. When he called back as if he was checking on an existing account, the tonality changed and so did the outcome — same words, completely different response.

---

### Tactic 6: Vision Selling

Sell the destination, not the deliverables.

Nobody wants the 12-hour flight. They want the beach. Nobody wants "1 hour coaching + macro plan." They want to drop 3 dress sizes.

**The mistake:** Most salespeople justify the price by walking through every deliverable — modules, PDFs, hours of training. This is actually counterproductive. Deliverables signal work. People don't want work. They want the outcome.

**How to do it:**
- Paint a vivid picture of the desired end state
- Ask: "What would your life look like once you've solved this?"
- Ask: "What would [the outcome] unlock for you that isn't possible right now?"
- Make the outcome feel real — specific, sensory, tangible
- Everything else (deliverables, modules, process) becomes the vehicle. Mention it briefly, don't dwell on it.

**The psychology:** The prospect's brain cannot cleanly distinguish between vividly imagined futures and real ones. When you paint the picture in enough detail, they're already emotionally living in that future state — and the purchase becomes the bridge to get there.

## Alex Hormozi — Logical Closing Framework

*Source: Alex Hormozi — "Selling with Logic to Make Lots of Money"*

---

### Core Philosophy

- **Logical closing beats emotional closing for long-term outcomes.** Emotional closes lead to regret, chargebacks, and buyers who bail when the feeling fades. Logical closes hold — the prospect can still say "yes, this still makes sense" two weeks later when the excitement is gone.
- **The higher the ticket, the more logical the buyer.** Emotional buyers dominate low-ticket. Logical buyers dominate high-ticket. Adjust accordingly.
- **The goal of closing is to help someone decide — not to make them buy.** Train your team to reward getting prospects to a decision (either direction), not just to a "yes." This changes everything: posture, pressure, and trust.
- **Closing starts when you present the offer.** Everything before that is sales. Everything after — objections, hesitation, negotiation — is closing. The "red zone."
- **80% of people need help deciding.** Only 20% are ready to buy regardless. You exist for the 80%. That's the job.
- **Closing is a dance, not a fight.** Seduction, not combat. The moment you argue, you lose. Stay curious — "I wouldn't have thought of it that way, why do you think that?" — and you can hold the frame indefinitely.
- **You can only transfer what you have.** Belief → Trust → Transfer. If you've lost belief in the product (cynicism from learning too much), there's nothing to transfer even if the prospect trusts you. Protect your belief.
- **Commission breath kills deals.** Prospects have thousands of years of evolutionary wiring to detect who's lying or selling. If you have commission breath, they smell it. Reset before every call: human first, sale second.

---

### Pre-Close vs Close: Opticals vs Objections

- **Opticals** = obstacles raised BEFORE you present the offer (pre-suasion phase). Destroy these before you get to the close.
- **Objections** = expressions of disagreement raised AFTER you present the offer. These are what closing handles.

Destroy as many opticals as possible pre-offer. Then when you enter the close, you're only dealing with real objections.

---

### The Power Framework (3 Sources of Blame)

Based on Albert Ellis's rational emotive therapy — the three places people give away their power. These map directly to the objections you'll face:

| Layer | What they're saying | Objection type |
|---|---|---|
| **Circumstances** | "I must get what I want / I can't have what I don't want" | Time, money |
| **Other people** | "Others must approve of me / treat me fairly" | Authority (spouse, boss, partner) |
| **Self** | "I must not make a mistake / I must be certain" | Fear of failure, need to "think about it" |

These are nested like an onion. If someone gives a circumstances objection, there are still 2-3 more layers underneath. Work through all of them. Don't assume one handled = sold.

**The meta-reframe:** Every objection is a powerless statement. Your job is to help the prospect reclaim their power — to make a decision that is their own. When you do that, you've served them regardless of whether they buy.

---

### Objection Handling by Type

#### Time Objection — "I'm too busy / not a good time"

The false belief: life will be less busy in the future.

- "Do you think you'll be busy for the rest of your life?"
- "Have you seen anyone else with a similar schedule make this work?"
- Analogy: "When I'm healed I'll go to the hospital" — wrong order. You go to the hospital to get healed. You join the gym to get fit. You don't wait until you have the result to take the action that produces it.
- "When is the right time? What would have to be different?" — surfaces that there is no right time; the wait is indefinite.

---

#### Money Objection — "I can't afford it"

The false belief: it's a resource problem, not a value problem.

- It is never actually about money. It is always about perceived value. If I handed you the keys to a Ferrari for free, you'd find a way to insure it.
- "You're going to buy the lessons either way — from this program or from years of failed attempts. The question is how you want to spend your time and money."
- Resourceful vs resources: you become resourceful when you value something enough. Tax bill example — people always find the money when they have to.
- "Forces are created by taking a lot of risk with a little money. Forces are maintained by taking a little risk with a lot of money." Help them see the risk asymmetry.
- If value is communicated clearly, resources are never the actual constraint. If they say they can't afford it, you haven't communicated the value.
- Big bold testimonials / before-and-afters. "How many more results would you need to see before you believed this was possible for you?"

---

#### Authority Objection — "I need to talk to my spouse / partner / boss"

The hardest objection. They're saying: "I don't control my life."

3-step process:
1. **Isolate the real objection.** "What part of this do you think they would approve of?" Their answer tells you the actual reason they're hesitating — they'll answer as if speaking for the absent party but it's really their own objection. Now you can address it directly.
2. **Ask why they wouldn't approve.** "Why do you think your partner wouldn't want you to do this?" — surfaces the fear. Address that fear.
3. **Permission vs support reframe.** "Are you looking for permission, or are you looking for support?" They've been seeking permission to stay stuck. Frame it: "Three years from now, you're still in the same place, and all along you've been blaming them. Is that for you?" This makes the pattern visible.

Future-cast the negative: if they keep deferring to this person, where do they end up in 3 years?

---

#### Fear of Mistake / "I Need to Think About It"

What they're really saying: "I want the universe to make the decision for me. I don't trust myself to make a call this big."

**The rocking chair test:** "When you drive home and you're sitting in your chair, what exactly are you going to think about? Walk me through it." They have no answer — because they're not actually going to think about it. Brings the false deferral to light.

**Present the decision criteria out loud:**
- "Do you believe the product would work?" Yes.
- "Do you trust us?" Yes.
- "Do you think it could work for you?" Yes.
- "Do you have access to the money?" Yes.
- "So what would you need to decide right now that you don't already have?"

When you surface that they have all the information needed, the "thinking about it" excuse dissolves.

**New identity close:** "You said you want to be X. Right now you're Y. The only question is whether you want to be X or Y." When they take on a new identity, new priorities follow automatically.

### Past / Present / Future Framework

Use these three levers to reframe the decision:

**Past casting — build the case with their own actions:**
Stack everything they've already done to get to this moment. Bought the ticket, blocked the time, showed up, took notes, had the call. "This isn't a snap decision — you've been building to this for a long time. You've already been deciding with your actions."

"What has the last five years of almost cost you? Five years of almost being in the shape you want. Almost making the money you want. What would you say that cost you?"

**Present — bring the decision here, now:**
"What do you need in order to make a decision right now?" Force the criteria to the surface. Don't let "thinking about it" remain vague and formless.

**Future casting — paint both directions:**
- Positive: "Two years from now, if this works — what does that look like for you?"
- Negative: "Two years from now, if nothing changes — what does that look like?"

Let them feel both futures viscerally. The contrast does the work.

---

### The Directional Decision Frame

One of Hormozi's most-used frameworks. Removes the pressure of making the "perfect" decision.

Replace: "Will this get me exactly what I want?" (impossibly high bar)
With: **"Will this get me closer or further from where I want to go?"**

- Closer = do it.
- Further = don't.
- That's the only decision that needs to be made.

"Each of these is just a brick. You don't need all 10 bricks today. You just need to get one brick closer. If you walk out of here today, do you think you'll be closer to your goal or further? Closer. That's all you need to decide."

Removes decision paralysis. Lowers the bar from "perfect choice" to "directional choice." Works especially well for education and service purchases.

---

### The Mirror Close

When a prospect gives you their reason for not doing it — reflect it back as the reason they should.

- "I don't have time to get in shape." → "That's exactly the reason you need to do this."
- "I don't have money right now." → "That's exactly why this matters."
- "My partner doesn't support it." → "That's part of the reason you need to take this step."

Works because the stated obstacle is usually a symptom of the problem the product solves. Naming it out loud reframes it.

---

### The Brick Principle

People attribute success to the last thing that "got them there." But no single investment delivers all 10 bricks. Each one gets you a step closer.

"I've invested in things in the past that didn't work out completely — but they got me closer. Every step is a brick. This is another brick. And if you're one brick closer, that's worth it."

Use this to handle "it might not work perfectly" or "I've been burned before." Yes — but did it move you closer? That's the standard.

---

### The "No Reason Left" Frame

End of a call: "Both options have no downside risk. Walking out changes nothing. Moving forward has potential upside. If there's no risk either way, which option gives you the upside?"

Best case: life changes. Worst case: you did something, learned, got closer. No case: nothing happened. Which of those three futures do you want?

### Quick Reference: Hormozi Closing Stack

```
1. Set the frame: your job is to help them decide, not make them buy
      ↓
2. Identify which layer of the onion they're on:
   Circumstances (time/money) → Other people (authority) → Self (fear of mistake)
      ↓
3. Match the framework to the objection layer
      ↓
4. Past-cast → Present → Future-cast
      ↓
5. Directional decision frame: closer or further?
      ↓
6. Mirror close if needed: their reason IS the reason
      ↓
7. No reason left: no risk on either side, only one has upside
```

### Cross-Reference with Russell Brunson (Expert Secrets) — CONFIRMED PATTERN

Hormozi's 3 layers (why people don't buy) and Brunson's 3 false belief types are the same framework expressed from two different angles. One is used live on a sales call; the other is used inside a presentation or VSL.

| Hormozi layer | Brunson false belief type | What it sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| **Circumstances** (time/money) | **External** — "outside forces will stop me" | "I can't afford it / I don't have time / the market is against me" |
| **Other people** (authority/approval) | **External** (sub-type) — "someone else controls this" | "I need to talk to my spouse / my partner won't support it" |
| **Self** (fear of mistake) | **Internal** — "I can't do it / I'll fail" | "I'm not sure it's for me / I've tried before and failed" |

**Where Brunson adds a layer Hormozi doesn't cover in the close:**
Brunson has a **Vehicle** false belief — "this method/category doesn't work" — that sits above the other three. Hormozi handles this as a pre-close issue (build belief through proof and testimonials before asking for the sale), not in the closing framework itself. This maps to destroying opticals before the offer.

**The sequencing implication (confirmed by both):**
Brunson's presentation sequence is: Vehicle → Internal → External.
Hormozi's objection onion goes: Circumstances/External → Other People → Self/Internal.
Both say the same thing in different orders: you must build Vehicle/product belief before the close. Then you handle External circumstances. Then Internal self-doubt. They meet at the same place.

**Practical synthesis for OLC and Nirvana:**
- **In presentations and VSLs (Brunson):** Break false beliefs in this order — Vehicle (does this work?) → Internal (can I do this?) → External (will my situation allow it?)
- **In live sales calls (Hormozi):** Handle objections in the order they arise, knowing they layer: Circumstances first, then Authority/Other People, then Self
- **The same stories break the same objections in both contexts.** Build your story inventory once (Brunson's 12-slot grid), then deploy the same stories on calls (Hormozi's framework) and in ads/VSLs (Brunson's presentation)

This is a **Stage 2 cross-confirmed principle** — two independent sources, same underlying structure. High confidence.
