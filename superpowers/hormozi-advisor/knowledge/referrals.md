# Referral Playbook

*Source: Hormozi — $100M Leads, second half of training*

---

## The Big Idea: Leverage

You can only move so fast. The real game isn't doing more yourself, it's getting other people to do it on your behalf.

Leverage = what you get out divided by what you put in.

Four scenarios, each one better than the last:

1. **You are the lead getter.** You do the work, you get the leads, you pay the bills. Solopreneur mode. Respectable. Not scalable.

2. **You get one lead getter.** One day of teaching and that person runs your outreach every day from then on. You did one day's work, they do 365.

3. **You get a new lead getter every day.** Now you're compounding. By day four you have three people doing what you used to do alone.

4. **You get a lead getter who finds lead getters.** You recruit one recruiter. They bring in callers, emailers, a content team, a media buyer. That one hire multiplies permanently.

The four types of lead getters: customers, employees, agencies, affiliates. This section covers customers, which is referrals.

---

## Why Referrals Win

Every other channel is linear. You spend $100 on ads, you get $500 back. You spend $1,000, you get $5,000. Same ratio, just bigger numbers.

Referrals compound. One person tells two, two tell four, four tell eight. If that rate ever outpaces the rate at which you lose customers, the business grows without you doing anything else.

Referred customers also cost less to acquire and tend to spend more and faster. So they're better leads and cheaper to get. That combination is basically the whole game.

---

## The Formula Worth Writing Down

`% of clients who refer monthly - % of clients who churn monthly = monthly compounding growth rate`

Three outcomes:

- **Referrals > churn:** You grow forever without advertising.
- **Referrals = churn:** Ads help you grow but you hold steady on your own.
- **Referrals < churn:** You have to advertise just to break even. This is the hamster wheel. Every new customer you bring in leaves before they tell anyone.

If your product is mediocre and you keep pushing for more leads, you just accelerate the cycle. More people come in, have a bad experience, leave, and say nothing good.

## Why Most Businesses Have No Referrals

Two reasons only:

1. The product isn't as good as they think it is.
2. They never ask.

## How to Fix the Product

Six things you can actually control. The goal with all of them is to move the value customers get as high as possible, because that gap between what they paid and what they got is what determines whether they tell people.

### 1. Sell better customers

Look at your top 20% of customers. Find what they have in common. Filter for those traits on the front end and stop selling to people who don't qualify.

When Hormozi did this at Gym Launch, they found the winners all had: a signed lease, at least one employee, at least 30 members. They told the sales team not to close anyone without those three. Results improved without changing anything about the service itself.

Selling to unqualified people doesn't just hurt your revenue. It makes your product look worse than it is.

### 2. Set better expectations

Stop taking two or three outlier results and building your pitch around them. Use averages. Show what a typical customer gets at month one, month six, month twelve. State it as fact, not a promise.

When you do that, customers come in expecting less than they'll actually get. Goodwill is the gap between what they expected and what they received. Overpromising closes that gap or flips it negative.

Harvard doesn't say "you'll be a millionaire." They publish graduation rates and average starting salaries. The bar is real, and it's still high enough to be compelling.

### 3. Get more people better results

Six steps:

1. Survey customers. Find who got the best results.
2. Interview them. What did they do that others didn't?
3. Find the common actions across the top performers.
4. Force every new customer to take those actions.
5. Measure whether your average results improved.
6. Match your guarantee conditions to those actions.

The last one is the trick. Once you know what actions produce results, require them as a condition of the guarantee. You're telling the customer: "Do these specific things and I'll guarantee the outcome." That sells the program and gets them to do what actually works.

### 4. Deliver faster

Look at what everyone else takes 100 days to do. Figure out how to do it in 30. Then 7. Then 24 hours.

When you can't compress the timeline itself, break the outcome into smaller milestones and communicate each one. A client who hears from you daily about what moved forward feels like they're getting results faster, even if the final date is the same.

Never leave a customer in no man's land. They should always know what happens next. "Book a meeting from a meeting" -- when one interaction ends, the next one is already scheduled.

The first 48 hours after someone signs matter more than most businesses realize. Customers decide how good a business is very early. Make micro-promises and hit every one of them.

### 5. Keep improving

Use support tickets, surveys, and reviews to find the most common complaints. Fix one, test it on a small group of struggling customers (not your best ones), measure the result, then roll it out. Move on to the next complaint. Repeat forever.

### 6. Tell them what to buy next

Customers get a buying itch. If you don't have something for them to buy, they'll satisfy it somewhere else. Always have a clear next offer. Tell them what it is, what they'll need to qualify, and when you'll let them know they're ready.

## The 7 Ways to Ask for Referrals

### 1. One-sided benefit

Pay your customers what it would otherwise cost you to acquire someone through ads. Calculate your total customer acquisition cost and offer that to the person who brings you a new customer. Paying Cindy to send Sarah is better than paying Facebook.

### 2. Two-sided benefit

Split your acquisition cost in half. Half goes to the customer who referred (in cash or credit), half goes to the new person coming in (in credit toward their first purchase). Both win.

Hormozi's service businesses got 25-30% of new customers this way just by making this offer at signup.

### 3. Ask when they buy

The moment someone signs is when enthusiasm is highest. Ask right there. Add a field on the contract or checkout page for names and numbers of people they'd like to bring along.

And ask WHO, not IF. Don't say "do you know anyone?" Say "who in your life would benefit from this?"

Also sell them on doing it with a friend. The research supports it: people who do behavior-change programs with a partner get better results. Frame it as something that helps them, not as a favor to you.

### 4. Use referrals as a negotiation chip

If someone asks for a discount, don't just say no. Say: "I can't lower the price for you without lowering it for everyone. But if you're willing to introduce me to three people, I can make this work at $X."

Start with three. If they balk, come down to two. You've added a variable to the negotiation and you're getting leads instead of giving margin away.

### 5. Referral events

Run a time-limited push (one to four weeks) where customers earn points, credits, or prizes for bringing people in. Make the event about doing the thing together, not about the reward.

Frame it right: "Your results will double if you do this with someone." The referral becomes something that benefits them. These events also deepen community among existing customers, which reduces churn.

### 6. Ongoing language

Don't make referrals a campaign. Make them part of everything. Every piece of content, every post promoting your offer, every onboarding message. Soft-ask constantly: "If you know someone who'd benefit from this, they're welcome to join you."

One operator added this language to all his marketing and saw a 33% lift in total signups with no other changes.

### 7. Bonus unlocks

Create something valuable that customers can't buy directly. A VIP tier, faster response times, an event, a private resource, something. Then make the only way to get it: refer someone AND leave a testimonial.

People feel weird accepting cash for a referral. Give them status and something tangible instead. "You can have this, but only if you bring a friend and leave a review" works differently than "send me someone and I'll pay you."

## The Gift Card Play (combines multiple methods)

Give every new customer a gift card worth up to a third of what they paid. Put an expiration date on it (7-14 days). Tell them to give it to a friend.

What this does:

- The customer becomes the gift-giver instead of a commission salesperson. They look generous.
- The friend feels obligated to use it, not to ignore a direct pitch.
- You get the friend's name legitimately (write it on the card before texting the photo).
- The expiration creates urgency without you having to apply any pressure.

For seasonal pushes, sell these gift cards at 90% off -- a $2,000 gift card for $100 -- with a limit of two per customer. You get paid to acquire new customers.

One rule: match the incentive to what you sell. Free t-shirts for an IT firm attract people who want t-shirts. Free credit toward your service attracts people who want your service.

## The Thought Experiment Worth Running With Your Team

You've lost every customer except one. The gods of advertising ban you from all outreach. The only way you can grow is if that one customer sends you more.

How would you treat them? What would onboarding look like? What results would they need to get? Who would you want that one customer to be?

Whatever you answer, that's how you should be running your business.

---

## Action Items

1. Calculate two numbers: what percentage of your customers refer someone each month, and what percentage leave each month. That gap is your baseline.

2. Work through the six product improvements. Start with "who am I selling to" and "what am I promising" -- those two usually have the most upside for the least effort.

3. Pick one of the seven referral methods and build it into your process this week. The simplest starting point is #3: ask at the moment of purchase, and ask who, not if.
